Chapter Summary

How Nature Works in Harmony (Introduction)

  • All living organisms need food, water, air, and a safe place to live — collectively provided by their surroundings.
  • Every habitat (pond, forest, desert, grassland) contains both living beings and non-living things that form a complex, interconnected world.
  • This chapter explores how living beings are connected, how they obtain food, how waste is recycled, and how natural interactions keep ecosystems stable.
  • Understanding these connections helps us appreciate the importance of protecting habitats and the valuable services ecosystems provide to all life on Earth.

HOW DO WE EXPERIENCE AND INTERPRET OUR SURROUNDINGS?

Habitat — A place where an organism lives is called its habitat. The habitat is a part of the total environment of the region.

  • A habitat must provide to the organisms living therein: food, shelter, and favourable climatic conditions to survive, breed, and flourish.
  • Different habitats have different types of living beings and non-living things. For example, a pond has aquatic animals like fish, while a forest has terrestrial, aerial, and arboreal animals.
  • A pond may have non-living things like water and pebbles, while a forest may have rocks and soil.
  • Organisms living in the same habitat may use available resources in different ways — e.g., a snake comes out at night while a rodent comes out during the day in the same forest.
  • Deforestation and changes in rainfall/temperature shrink natural habitats, forcing animals like elephants to enter human settlements; wildlife corridors are identified to allow safe animal movement between forest areas.

Biotic Component

Biotic component — All living beings such as human beings, animals, plants, and microorganisms are called the biotic component. It is also called the living component.

  • Includes all living organisms within a habitat — humans, animals, plants, and microorganisms.
  • Biotic components interact with each other and with abiotic components for survival.
  • Each organism needs specific biotic conditions (plants and animals) provided by its habitat.

Abiotic Component

Abiotic component — All non-living things such as soil, water bodies, atmosphere, and meteorological factors such as sunlight, temperature, rainfall, wind speed, etc., are called the abiotic component. It is also called the non-living component.

  • Abiotic components include air, water, soil, sunlight, temperature, and rainfall.
  • Each organism needs specific abiotic conditions to survive — e.g., fish obtain oxygen from water.
  • Plants depend on abiotic components like sunlight, carbon dioxide, and water to produce food; soil provides essential nutrients; oxygen is needed for respiration.
  • In turn, biotic components affect abiotic ones — plants release oxygen during photosynthesis, roots hold soil in place and prevent erosion, and plants retain soil moisture and cool the atmosphere.

WHO ALL LIVE TOGETHER IN NATURE?

Population — A group of the same type of organisms living in a habitat is called population.

Community — Different populations which share the same habitat comprise a community. All the biotic components of a habitat (plants, animals, and microorganisms) together form a community.

Pollination — The process by which pollen grains are transferred from the stamen to the pistil through air, water, insects, birds, animals, etc. It is essential for the formation of fruits and seeds.

  • If all organisms in a habitat were the same type, they would compete for the same food, water, and space, leading to scarcity of resources.
  • Different types of organisms live together in a habitat, forming a community that interacts and depends on one another for survival.
  • Flowers depend on the community of insects, birds, bats, etc., of their habitat for pollination and reproduction.
  • The stamen (male part) consists of filament and anther; the anther carries pollen grains producing male gametes. The pistil is the female reproductive part.

DOES EVERY ORGANISM IN A COMMUNITY MATTER?

  • Every organism in a community has direct and indirect effects on other organisms.
  • Example: Pond A has fish (which eat dragonfly larvae), leading to fewer dragonflies but more bees, flies, and butterflies — which results in more flowering plants due to better pollination. Pond B has no fish, so more dragonflies, fewer pollinating insects, and fewer flowers.
  • Fish have an indirect effect on plants in and around ponds — fish reduce dragonfly larvae → fewer dragonflies → more pollinating insects → more flowers.
  • Green arrows represent direct effects; red arrows represent indirect effects in ecological interaction diagrams.
  • This demonstrates that removing even one organism from a community can significantly alter the balance of the ecosystem.

WHAT ARE THE DIFFERENT TYPES OF INTERACTIONS AMONG ORGANISMS AND THEIR SURROUNDING?

Ecosystem — The interactions of biotic components (plants, animals, and microorganisms) and abiotic components (air, water, sunlight, temperature, and soil) in a habitat form an ecosystem.

Human-made ecosystem — An artificially created ecosystem like farmlands, fish ponds, and parks to meet human needs. A farmland is a human-made ecosystem.

  • There are two main ecosystems in nature: Aquatic ecosystems (ponds, rivers, lakes) and Terrestrial ecosystems (forests, farms, large trees like mango, banyan, or pilkhan).
  • Ecosystems can overlap — e.g., a stream (aquatic) flowing through hills, forests, grassland (terrestrial), and farmland (human-made) nearby, all interacting with each other.
  • Living and non-living components in an ecosystem depend on each other.

Among Biotic Components

  • These interactions influence the availability of resources needed for life processes like reproduction, respiration, and nutrition.
  • Example: Frogs and fish both eat insects and small fish — they compete for small insect larvae in water.
  • Example: A water snake eats fish, so fish lay eggs near vegetation to protect them from predators.

Between Biotic and Abiotic Components

  • These interactions are important for survival in any habitat and influence life processes like nutrition, respiration, and reproduction.
  • Example: Earthworms live in moist soil; microbes live in ponds; fish lay eggs in water; plants and animals need oxygen from air for respiration.
  • Plants (biotic) depend on sunlight, CO₂, and water (abiotic) to produce food; soil for nutrients; oxygen for respiration.
  • In return, plants release oxygen during photosynthesis, roots hold soil preventing erosion, and plants retain soil moisture and cool the atmosphere.

Between Two Abiotic Components

  • These mainly influence the physical characteristics of a habitat.
  • Example: Bright sunlight causes high daytime temperature; water evaporates faster due to sunlight; air currents create gentle waves on water surfaces; soil near a pond is moist.

MODES OF NUTRITION

  • In living organisms, food is the basic source of energy.
  • Based on food habits, modes of nutrition are divided into two categories: Autotrophic nutrition and Heterotrophic nutrition.

Autotrophic Nutrition

Autotrophic nutrition — The mode of nutrition in which an organism makes its food itself from simple inorganic substances (auto means self, trophes means nourishment).

Autotrophs — The organisms which make their own food from simple substances present in the environment. They are also called producers.

  • Green plants make their food from carbon dioxide and water in the presence of chlorophyll and sunlight.
  • Autotrophs are the producers of food in an ecosystem.
  • The mode of nutrition in green plants is autotrophic.

Heterotrophic Nutrition

Heterotrophic nutrition — The mode of nutrition in organisms that cannot make their food and depend on others for their food (heteros means other, trophes means nourishment).

Heterotrophs — The organisms which cannot make their food and depend on others for their food. They are also called consumers.

  • All animals and humans are heterotrophs — they eat food prepared by plants or eat flesh of other animals.
  • Depending on the food they eat, heterotrophs are divided into three types: Herbivores, Carnivores, and Omnivores.

Heterotrophic Nutrition in Animals

Herbivores — The animals which eat only grass, plants, or plant products (plant-eaters). Examples: Elephant, Camel, Cow, Deer, Goat, Grasshopper.

Carnivores — The animals which eat meat or flesh of other animals (meat-eaters). Examples: Lion, Tiger, Jackal, Hawk, Frog, Snake, Vulture, Lizard.

Omnivores — The animals which eat both plants and meat (both plant-and-meat-eaters). Examples: Man, Crow, Dog, Ant, Sparrow, Mynah.

  • Food items obtained from plants: Rice, Wheat, Pulses (dals), Fruits, Vegetables.
  • Food items obtained from animals: Milk, Cheese, Curd, Meat, Eggs.
  • Man is an omnivore because humans eat both meat (animals) and plants (vegetables and cereals).

WHO EATS WHOM?

Food chain — The sequence of living organisms in which one organism consumes (or eats) another to transfer the food energy. It is a linear chain of representation of interactions between biotic components based on feeding relationship.

Trophic level — Each organism in a food chain has a specific position, called a trophic level (troph = food).

Food web — Most food chains in an ecosystem are interconnected with each other to form a network, called a food web.

  • A food chain always begins with grass/plant (producers) at the first trophic level.
  • Herbivores (rabbit, deer) are at the second trophic level; small carnivores (frog) at the third; large carnivores (tiger, vulture) at the next level.
  • Example food chain: Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle.
  • If one organism is removed from a food chain, all other organisms get affected — e.g., removing grasshoppers affects frogs, snakes, and eagles.
  • Each organism in a food web may be eaten by two or more types of organisms, making the web more stable than a single chain.

Activity 1: Understanding a Food Chain Represented in a Pyramid

  • A food chain can be represented as a pyramid with the highest number of organisms at the base and lowest at the top.
  • Example: Millet (most numerous, base) → Mouse (middle) → Eagle (fewest, top).
  • The pyramid shape shows that numbers decrease as you move up the food chain.

WHAT HAPPENS TO WASTE IN NATURE?

Saprotrophs — Organisms which derive their food from dead and decaying plants and animals (sapro means rotten, troph means food). Examples: bacteria, fungi (mushroom, yeast).

Decomposers — Organisms which decompose or break down complex substances in dead remains of plants and animals into simple compounds. They help in maintaining the supply of nutrients to growing plants.

Decomposition — The process by which decomposers break down dead plant and animal tissues and convert them into humus, recycling nutrients back into the environment.

Humus — A dark coloured material formed when decomposers feed on dead plant and animal tissues. Humus helps in the release of nutrients from dead and decaying organic matter into the soil.

  • Apart from herbivores, carnivores, and omnivores, saprotrophs form another category of consumers.
  • Microorganisms like bacteria and fungi (mushroom, yeast) are saprotrophs; mushrooms grow on dead organic matter during the rainy season.
  • Tiny insects, ants, beetles, and flies help break down animal droppings (like elephant dung) and recycle nutrients.
  • Decomposers play an important role in recycling nutrients — nature does not waste anything; everything is recycled and reused.

HOW DOES ONE CHANGE LEAD TO ANOTHER?

Ecological damage — Harm caused to the environment, soil, water quality, and overall ecosystem due to disruptions in natural interactions (e.g., overuse of pesticides, decline in frog population).

  • One small change may trigger a domino effect resulting in many other changes.
  • Example: Pollution → plants die in a pond → less oxygen → fish die → more insects (no fish to eat them) → insects spread to farmlands → farmers use pesticides → pesticides flow back to water bodies → cycle repeats.
  • Real-life example: India exported frog legs (Indian bullfrog) in the 1980s → frog population declined → agricultural pests increased (frogs eat insects) → farmers used more synthetic pesticides → harmed environment, soil, and water → Government of India finally banned frog leg export to avoid further ecological damage.
  • When interactions between organisms and environment keep populations and resources stable, the ecosystem stays in balance; a slight disruption can cause imbalance.

HOW DO INTERACTIONS MAINTAIN BALANCE IN ECOSYSTEMS?

  • Organisms in an ecosystem use common resources (food, water, space, sunlight) and must compete for them.
  • Competition prevents one species from increasing too much and causing imbalance — e.g., plants in a forest compete for sunlight and soil nutrients.

Mutualism — A relationship where both organisms benefit from each other. Example: Honey bees collect nectar from flowers and in turn act as pollinators.

Commensalism — A relationship where one organism is benefited while the other stays unaffected. Example: Orchids on tree branches get nutrition while the tree is not affected.

Parasitism — A relationship where one organism benefits from the other while harming it in the process. Example: Ticks feed on a dog's blood, causing skin irritation to the dog.

  • These different types of relationships form part of the complex web of life in an ecosystem.
  • A.J.T. Johnsingh, a famous Indian wildlife biologist, proved that the survival of a predator (tiger, leopard) depended on a healthy prey population (deer, wild boar), helping people understand forest ecosystems through the eyes of animals.
  • Protected areas (national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves, community conserved areas) conserve wildlife and habitats — e.g., Jim Corbett NP, Manas NP, Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve, Chilika Lake, Eaglenest Wildlife Sanctuary, Hemis NP, Keibul Lamjao NP, Pirotan Island Marine NP.

WHAT ARE THE BENEFITS OF AN ECOSYSTEM?

  • Biotic and abiotic components are interdependent for survival and to support various life processes.
  • We get food, timber, fibre, medicines, fresh air, and fertile soil from forest ecosystems; water and food from aquatic ecosystems.
  • Ecosystems provide aesthetic and recreational values; humans are the biggest beneficiaries.
  • Overuse and misuse of natural resources disturbs the natural balance.
  • The Sundarbans (at the junction of Ganga and Brahmaputra, India-Bangladesh) is a threatened ecosystem — largest mangrove forests in the world, home to endangered flora and fauna, acts as a barrier during storms/floods, absorbs CO₂ and releases O₂, declared a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1987.
  • Threats to Sundarbans: cutting mangrove trees for fuelwood/farming, illegal hunting and overuse of forest resources, industrial waste and untreated sewage damaging water and habitat.
  • Other ecosystems across India face similar problems from deforestation, overuse of resources, unsustainable land use, and spread of invasive species.

Human-made Ecosystems

  • These are artificially created ecosystems like farmlands, fish ponds, and parks to meet human needs.
  • If well designed, well managed, and properly cared for, they can reduce pollution, support biodiversity, and provide recreational spaces.

How do Healthy Ecosystems Serve Our Farms?

Green Revolution — The mid-20th century period marked by better farming practices like use of tractors, machines, synthetic fertilizers, and pesticides that helped to increase food production in India.

  • Low crop production between 1950 and 1965 resulted in a food crisis in India, leading to the Green Revolution.
  • Overuse of synthetic chemicals, excessive groundwater extraction, and monoculture (growing one type of crop) have made farming methods unsustainable.
  • Unsustainable farming reduces soil fertility by reducing friendly microorganisms and lowering humus; without the humus-rich top soil layer, soil becomes prone to erosion.
  • A fall in natural predators (like beetles) increases pest population; excessive irrigation and repeated ploughing disturb earthworms and snails, disturbing ecological balance.
  • Increased pesticide use makes pests resistant; monoculture reduces crop diversity and affects pollinators important for food production.
  • Vrikshayurveda, an ancient Indian text, advocates continuous soil nourishment through organic manure like Kunapa Jala (a liquid fertilizer made by fermenting animal and plant waste).
  • Natural pest control by predators (e.g., ladybird beetles eating aphids) is an eco-friendly alternative.
  • Farmers are now moving towards organic and natural farming methods — an initiative towards sustainable farming causing minimal imbalance in natural ecosystems.

Q1. What is a habitat?

A habitat is a place where an organism lives. It is a part of the total environment of the region. A habitat must provide the organisms living there with food, shelter, and favourable climatic conditions to survive, breed and flourish. Examples include a pond, forest, desert or grassland.

Q2. Name two biotic and two abiotic components.

  • Biotic components (living): Plants, animals (e.g., fish, human beings, microorganisms)
  • Abiotic components (non-living): Soil, water, sunlight, air, temperature, rainfall

Q3. Give an example where two organisms in the same habitat use resources in different ways.

In a forest habitat, a snake comes out during the night when it is cool, while a rodent comes out during the day when it is warm. Both live in the same habitat but use the available resources (temperature conditions, time) in different ways. Another example: in a pond, fish obtain food from small animals and plants, while ducks feed on aquatic plants and insects — both share the same habitat but use resources differently.

Q4. What is meant by population?

A population is a group of the same type of organisms living in a habitat. For example, the group of fish of the same kind living together in a pond habitat is the population of that particular fish.

Q5. Define a community.

A community is formed by different populations which share the same habitat. All the biotic components of a habitat — plants, animals and microorganisms — together form a community. They interact and depend on one another for survival. For example, in a pond, fish, turtles, frogs, mosquitoes, ducks, snails, dragonflies, and plants like duckweed, lotus, algae and diatoms together form a community.

Q1. What will happen if a habitat has only one type of living organism?

If a habitat has only one type of living organism, they would all have the same requirements of food, water and space. This will lead to intense competition and possible scarcity of resources. The organism may not survive for long due to lack of food variety, absence of decomposers to recycle nutrients, and lack of pollinators or other interacting species needed for reproduction and ecological balance.

Q2. Give an example of interaction among biotic components in an ecosystem.

Both frogs and fish eat insects and small fish. They may compete for small insect larvae in water. Another example: a water snake eats fish, so fish lays eggs in water near vegetation to protect them from other fish, frogs or water snakes. Pollination is another example — flowers depend on insects, birds and bats for transfer of pollen grains from stamen to pistil, which is essential for the formation of fruits and seeds.

Q3. What is the human-made ecosystem?

A human-made ecosystem is an artificially created ecosystem designed to meet human needs. Examples include farmlands, fish ponds and parks. If they are well designed, well managed and taken care of properly, they can help reduce pollution, support biodiversity and provide recreational spaces.

Q1. What are autotrophs?

Autotrophs are organisms that make their own food from simple inorganic substances present in the environment. The word comes from auto (self) and trophes (nourishment). Green plants make their food from carbon dioxide and water in the presence of chlorophyll and sunlight. Since autotrophs produce food, they are also called producers.

No, heterotrophs cannot make their own food. The word comes from heteros (other) and trophes (nourishment). They depend on other organisms — plants or animals — for their food. All animals and humans are heterotrophs. They eat food prepared by plants or the flesh of other animals.

Q4. What is a food web?

A food web is a network of interconnected food chains in an ecosystem. Most food chains in an ecosystem are interconnected with each other because each organism may be eaten by two or more types of organisms. If one food chain is disturbed, it affects other food chains also. Thus, each organism is dependent on the other in a food web.

LET'S REVISE! (Page 248) — Understanding

Q1. What is the process of decomposition?

Decomposition is the process by which decomposers break down complex substances in dead remains of plants and animals into simple compounds. Decomposers feed on dead plant and animal tissues and convert them into a dark-coloured material called humus. Humus helps in the release of nutrients from dead and decaying organic matter into the soil. This process recycles nutrients back into the environment, ensuring that nature does not waste anything.

Q2. Name a saprotroph.

Microorganisms like bacteria and fungi (such as mushroom and yeast) are saprotrophs. Mushrooms are umbrella-like structures that grow on dead organic matter during the rainy season. They derive their food from dead and decaying plants and animals. (Sapro means rotten and troph means food.)

Q3. How is ecological damage caused?

Ecological damage is caused when human activities disrupt the natural balance of ecosystems. Examples from the chapter:

  • Export of frog legs: During the 1980s, India exported frog legs (especially the Indian bullfrog), causing a decline in frog population. Since frogs eat insects, fewer frogs led to a rise in agricultural pests. Farmers were forced to use more synthetic pesticides, which harmed the environment, soil and water quality, adversely affecting overall environmental and human health.

  • Pollution in water bodies: Pollution causes plants to die in ponds. Fewer plants means less oxygen for fish. Fewer fish means more insects. More insects spread to farmlands, compelling farmers to use pesticides, which again flow back to water bodies and trigger another chain of harmful events.

  • Deforestation, overuse of natural resources, unsustainable land use, and spread of invasive species also damage ecosystems like forests, rivers, wetlands, grasslands and coastal areas.

Q1. Give an example from the ecosystem where both organisms benefit from each other.

This is called mutualism. For example, honey bees collect nectar from flowers. In turn, they act as pollinators to these flowers by transferring pollen from one flower to another. Both the bee (gets nectar/food) and the flower (gets pollinated) benefit from this interaction.

Q2. What are Sundarbans famous for?

The Sundarbans, located at the junction of the Ganga and Brahmaputra rivers between India and Bangladesh, are famous for:

  • Having the largest mangrove forests in the world
  • Hosting many endangered flora and fauna

Q3. Why are producers always at the beginning of a food chain?

Producers (autotrophs like green plants) are always at the beginning of a food chain because they are the only organisms that can make their own food from simple inorganic substances (carbon dioxide and water) using sunlight, chlorophyll and nutrients from soil. If snakes are removed from the food chain (Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle):

  • Frog population will increase rapidly because their predator (snake) is no longer present to control their numbers.
  • With more frogs, the grasshopper population will decrease sharply as more frogs will feed on them.
  • With fewer grasshoppers, the grass may grow unchecked, which might initially seem positive but can lead to imbalance.
  • Eagles that depend on snakes for food will face food scarcity, leading to a decline in their population or forcing them to migrate.
  • The overall balance of the ecosystem will be disturbed, demonstrating how each organism in a food chain is dependent on the others.

- With more insects, nearby farmlands may face pest problems.

Q7. A flower falls dead on the soil, gets mixed with it and disappears in a few days. Can you think of a reason how and why does this happen?

This happens through the process of decomposition. When the flower falls on the soil:

  • Decomposers (such as bacteria, fungi, and tiny insects like ants, beetles and worms) feed on the dead flower.
  • They break down the complex organic substances in the dead flower into simpler compounds.
  • This process converts the dead flower into a dark-coloured material called humus.
  • Humus releases nutrients from the dead and decaying organic matter into the soil.
  • These nutrients are then absorbed by growing plants, completing the nutrient cycle.

This is nature's way of recycling — nothing is wasted. Decomposers play a crucial role in returning nutrients to the soil, ensuring that dead matter does not accumulate and that plants receive the nutrients they need to grow.

Q1. A variety of plants and trees grow in a forest. How does this help the animals in the area?

A variety of plants and trees in a forest helps animals in multiple ways:

  • Food availability: Different plants provide different types of food (leaves, fruits, seeds, nectar) supporting a variety of herbivores, which in turn support carnivores and omnivores through the food web.
  • Shelter and habitat: Trees provide nesting sites for birds, shelter for arboreal animals, and hiding places from predators.
  • Oxygen: Plants release oxygen through photosynthesis, which animals need for respiration.
  • Climate regulation: Plants help cool the atmosphere and maintain favourable temperature and moisture conditions.
  • Soil conservation: Roots hold soil in place and prevent erosion, maintaining the habitat.
  • Pollination and reproduction: Flowering plants depend on insects, birds and bats for pollination, and these animals in turn depend on plants for nectar — a mutualistic relationship.
  • Biodiversity: A variety of plants supports a greater diversity of animals, making the ecosystem more stable and resilient to disturbances.
  • Nutrient cycling: When plants shed leaves or die, decomposers break them down into humus, enriching the soil and supporting the growth of more plants, which in turn supports more animals.

Q2. Why is a food web considered more stable than a food chain?

A food web is considered more stable than a food chain because:

  • In a food web, each organism may be eaten by two or more types of organisms, providing multiple food options. If one food source declines, the organism can rely on alternative food sources.
  • In a food chain, if one organism is removed, the entire chain is disrupted. For example, if grasshoppers are removed from Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle, all other organisms are affected.
  • In a food web, the interconnected nature means that the loss of one species has a lesser impact because other organisms in the web can compensate through alternative feeding relationships.
  • A food web represents the real complexity of an ecosystem, where multiple food chains operate simultaneously, providing redundancy and resilience.
  • The diversity of interactions in a food web ensures that energy flow continues even if one pathway is disturbed, maintaining the overall balance of the ecosystem.

WHAT ARE THE DIFFERENT TYPES OF INTERACTIONS AMONG ORGANISMS AND THEIR SURROUNDING ?

Plants and animals depend on each other for nutrition, respiration and reproduction. Living organism also depend on non-living things for their survival.
Thus, there are the following types of interactions :

Among Biotic Components

These my influence the availability of resources needed for life processes like reproduction, respiration and nutrition.

For example, both frogs and fish eat insects and small fish. They may compete for small insects larvae in water.

Another example is that a water snake eats fish. So, fish lays eggs in water near vegetation to protect them from other fish, frogs or water snakes.

Between Biotic and Abiotic Components

These are important for survival in any habitat. They may influence life processes, like nutrition, respiration and reproduction in biotic components.

For example, earthworms live in moist soil; many microbes live in the pond; fish lays eggs in water; plants and animals need oxygen from air for respiration and water for survival.

Yet another example is that plants (biotic components) depend on :
* sunlight, carbon dioxide and water (abiotic components) to produce food;
* soil (abiotic component) to provide essential nutrients for growth;
* oxygen (abiotic component) for respiration.

In turn, oxygen (abiotic component) is released by plants (biotic components) during photosynthesis, roots hold soil in place and prevent erosion, plants retain soil moisture and help to cool the atmosphere.

MODES OF NUTRITION

In living organisms, food is the basic source of energy. Therefore, it is very important to know how different organisms get their food. Based on the food habits, the modes of nutrition in all living organisms are divided into two categories.

  • Autotrophic nutrition
  • Heterotrophic nutrition

Autotrophic Nutrition

The mode of nutrition in which an organism makes its food itself from simple inorganic substances is called autotrophic nutrition (auto means self and trophes means nourishment).

  • The organisms which make their own food from the simple substances present in the environment are called autotrophs.
  • Autotrophs are the producers of food, so they are also called producers.

Green plants make their food from carbon dioxide and water in the presence of chlorophyll and sunlight. So, the mode of nutrition in green plants is autotrophic.

Heterotrophic Nutrition

The mode of nutrition in organisms that cannot make their food and depend on others for their food is called heterotrophic nutrition (heteros means other and trophes means nourishment). All animals and humans are heterotrophs.

Heterotrophic Nutrition in Animals

  • The organisms which cannot make their food and depend on others for their food are called heterotrophs or consumers. The mode of nutrition in heterotrophs is called heterotrophic.
  • Animals and humans do not make their food. They eat the food prepared by plants or eat the flesh of the smaller animals. Therefore, all animals and humans are heterotrophs.

Depending upon the food they eat, heterotrophs can be divided into the following three types :

  • Herbivores
  • Carnivores

  • Omnivores

  • Herbivores (plant-eaters) : The animals which eat only grass, plants or plant products are called herbivores. Elephant, Camel, Cow, Deer, Goat, Grasshopper etc., are herbivores.

  • Carnivores (meat-eaters) : The animals which eat meat or flesh of the other animals are called carnivores. Lion, Tiger, Jackal, Hawk, Frog, Snake, Vulture, Lizard, etc., are carnivores.
  • Omnivores (both plant-and-meat-eaters) : The animals which eat both plants and meat are called omnivores. Man eats both meat (animal), and plants (vegetables and cereals). Therefore, man is an omnivore. Crow, Dog, Ant, Sparrow and Mynah are some other omnivores.

WHAT HAPPENS TO WASTE IN NATURE ?

Living organisms grow, perform many functions, develop and die. During their life cycle, organisms produce a lot of waste, including food waste and dead matter. Have you ever thought how this waste is managed in nature ?

Apart from herbivores, carnivores and omnivores, there is another category of consumers called saprotrophs. Sapro means rotten and troph means food. They are organisms which derive their food from the dead and decaying plants and animals. Microorganisms like bacteria and fungi (such as mushroom and yeast) are saprotrophs. Mushrooms (a fungus) are umbrella-like structures that grow on dead organic matter during the rainy season.

Then there are organisms called decomposers which decompose or break the complex substances in dead remains of plants and animals to simple compounds. They help in maintaining the supply of nutrients to the growing plants in an ecosystem.

You may find tiny insects, ants, beetles and flies on animal droppings - like elephant dung. They help to break it down and recycle nutrients back into the environment. This process is called decomposition and the organisms carrying out the process are called decomposers.

Decomposers feed themselves on the dead plant and animal tissues and convert them into dark coloured material called humus. Humus helps in the release of nutrients of the dead and decaying organic matter into the soil. Decomposers play an important role in recycling nutrients. Thus, nature does not waste anything. Everything in nature is recycled and reused.

WHAT ARE THE BENEFITS OF AN ECOSYSTEM ?

In an ecosystem, the biotic and abiotic components are interdependent on each other for their survival as well as to support various life processes. We get many useful things like food, timber, fibre, medicines, fresh air and fertile soil from forest ecosystem. We get water and food from aquatic ecosystem. Ecosystems provide aesthetic and recreational values also. The human beings are their biggest beneficiaries. But the overuse and misuse of natural resources disturbs the natural balance.

One of the real-life examples of threatened ecosystem is the Sundarbans located at the junction of Ganga and Brahmaputra river between India and Bangladesh. The Sundarbans not only have the largest mangrove forests in the world, but have many endangered flora and fauna as well. During storms and floods, they act as a barrier by slowing down strong winds and waves. The forest trees absorb carbon dioxide and release oxygen. They were declared a World Heritage site by UNESCO in 1987.

But now, a serious threat is looming large over the Sundarbans due to various human activities.

  • Mangrove trees are being cut either for fuelwood or to use the land for farming.
  • There is illegal hunting and forest resources are being overused - both of which pose a threat to the wildlife here.
  • Industrial waste and untreated sewage flowing in the rivers is damaging the water and habitat.
