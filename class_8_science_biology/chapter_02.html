The Invisible Living World: Beyond Our Naked Eye

Introduction: Lenses and the Microscopic World

Lens — A piece of any transparent material bound by two curved surfaces or by one curved and one plane surface.

Magnifying glass — A converging lens that allows you to see smaller objects more clearly by magnifying them.

  • Our eyes can only see objects of a certain size; extremely tiny objects require a lens to be seen clearly.
  • Lenses are used in cameras, telescopes, microscopes, and spectacles.
  • A round-bottom glass flask filled with water acts as a magnifying glass — letters appear larger when seen through it.
  • The chapter explores the world that cannot be seen with the naked eye and the scientific discoveries that made this possible.

What is a Cell?

Cellular level — The lowest level of organisation which has life; the level at which a cell exists.

Structural and functional unit — The cell is considered the basic structural and functional unit of life.

  • A cell forms the lowest level of organisation that has life.
  • There are many different kinds of cells, each performing a specific function.
  • The cell is the structural and functional unit of life.

Discovery of the Cell

Microscope — A scientific tool that makes extremely small objects visible by magnifying them; Robert Hooke's microscope made things look 200–300 times bigger than what the unaided eye could see.

  • Robert Hooke, an English scientist, discovered the cell in 1665.
  • In his book "Micrographia," Hooke showed detailed drawings of tiny things observed using a microscope.
  • He observed thin slices of cork under a microscope and found box-like compartments forming a honeycomb structure.
  • He called these compartments "cells" — the first time the word "cell" was used in science to describe the basic unit of life.

The Cell

Unicellular — Organisms made up of only one cell (e.g., Amoeba).

Multicellular — Organisms made up of many cells (e.g., humans, animals, plants).

  • All living organisms are made up of very small compartment-like building blocks called cells.
  • A cell is the smallest unit of life, capable of performing all biological activities.
  • Some organisms like Amoeba are unicellular (one cell); most organisms are multicellular (many cells).
  • The cell is a structural and functional unit of life.

Variation in Shape and Structure of Cells

Size of the Cells

  • In multicellular organisms, cells have different shapes and sizes.
  • Cell size may vary from 0.1 micrometre (0.1 × 10⁻⁶ m) to as large as 170 mm × 130 mm.
  • Some blood cells are the smallest in our body (red blood cell ~7 micrometre); nerve cells are the longest (longer than a metre).
  • The egg cell of an ostrich is the largest cell — about 7 cm in diameter.
  • The size of a cell is related to its function, not the body size of the organism.
  • The bacterium Mycoplasma has the smallest cell (~0.1 micrometre); the alga Acetabularia is a single cell 10 cm long.

Shape of the Cells

Elongated — Thread-like shape of certain cells.

Neurons — Another name for nerve cells, which are long, branched, and have thread-like projections.

  • Different cells have different shapes, and the shape is related to the cell's function and location.
  • Amoeba is irregular in shape and keeps changing it; a white blood cell (WBC) can also change its shape.
  • Nerve cells (neurons) are long and branched, helping carry messages quickly to different parts of the body.
  • Muscle cells are thin, flexible, and spindle-shaped, allowing them to contract and relax in a wave-like manner.
  • Plant cells can be rectangular, elongated oval, or tube-like; some form long tubes to carry water throughout the plant.
  • The cell membrane provides shape to cells in both plants and animals.

Cell Structure and Function

  • All cells show some similarities in structure despite differing in shape and size.
  • A typical cell consists of three basic parts: cell membrane (plasma membrane), nucleus, and cytoplasm.

Cell Membrane

Plasma membrane — Another name for the cell membrane; it forms the outer boundary of the cell, is thin and delicate, and allows only certain selected substances to enter or leave the cell.

  • The cell membrane gives shape and size to the cell.
  • It separates cells from one another and from the surrounding medium.
  • It is selectively permeable — allows only certain selected substances to enter or leave the cell.
  • It acts as a gateway of a cell.

Cell Wall

Cell wall — An extra outer wall surrounding the cell membrane, found in plant cells and bacteria, made of a stiff material called cellulose.

Cellulose — The stiff material that makes up the cell wall in plants.

  • The cell wall is present in plant cells and bacteria but absent in animal cells.
  • It is made of cellulose and provides protection against temperature variations, atmospheric moisture, and high wind speed.
  • All plant cells are arranged compactly with each other and look firm in structure due to the cell wall.

Nucleus

Nucleus — A dense, oval-shaped structure bounded by a double-layered membrane that contains genetic materials; it controls all vital functions of the cell and is called the brain of the cell.

Nuclear membrane — A double-layered membrane that surrounds the nucleus and controls the passage of certain materials in and out of the nucleus.

Nucleoplasm — A dense fluid present inside the nucleus.

Nucleolus — A spherical body present inside the nucleus that contains ribonucleic acid (RNA); it makes ribosomes which make protein.

Chromosomes — Thread-like structures composed of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) that contain basic hereditary units called genes.

Genes — Basic hereditary units found on chromosomes, responsible for passing genetic characteristics from parents to offspring.

  • In animal cells, the nucleus is present at the centre of the cell.
  • The nucleus controls all vital functions, regulates all activities within the cell, and regulates growth.
  • The nucleus consists of four parts: nuclear membrane, nucleoplasm, nucleolus, and chromosomes.
  • Chromosomes contain DNA and genes that carry hereditary characteristics from one generation to the next.

Functions of Nucleus

  • Transmission of hereditary characteristics from generation to generation.
  • Controls all life functions taking place inside the cell.

Cytoplasm

Cytoplasm — A thick jelly-like fluid inside the cell membrane, occupying the space between the cell membrane and the nucleus; it contains cell organelles and compounds like carbohydrates, proteins, fats, and mineral salts.

  • All life functions take place in the cytoplasm.
  • It contains many small cytoplasmic bodies called cell organelles.
  • All organelles in the cytoplasm play an active role in the functioning of the cell.

Cell Organelles

Mitochondria — Cell organelles that produce energy from food during respiration; called the "powerhouse of the cell"; present in both plant and animal cells. Energy is produced in the form of ATP.

Endoplasmic reticulum — Cell organelles that help in the transport of substances within the cell and in the synthesis of proteins; when ribosomes are present on them, they are called rough endoplasmic reticulum.

Ribosomes — Cell organelles that help in protein synthesis; found in both plant and animal cells.

Golgi bodies — Cell organelles that synthesize, store, and secrete many substances; synthesized by the endoplasmic reticulum.

Vacuoles — Sac-like structures present in the cytoplasm that store soluble food, wastes, and secretions dissolved in water as cell sap; large in plant cells, small or absent in animal cells.

  • Mitochondria are called the powerhouse of the cell because they produce energy (ATP) from food during respiration.
  • Endoplasmic reticulum helps in transport of substances and protein synthesis within the cell.
  • Ribosomes are responsible for protein synthesis in both plant and animal cells.
  • Golgi bodies synthesize, store, and secrete many substances.
  • Vacuoles are large in plant cells but smaller or absent in animal cells; they store food, wastes, and secretions as cell sap.

Some Other Organelles

Lysosomes — Cell organelles present in animal cells that help in cellular digestion.

Centrosomes — Cell organelles present only in animal cells that play a role in the reproduction of cells.

Plastids — Cell organelles that occur only in plant cells; they are of three types: chloroplasts, leucoplasts, and chromoplasts.

Chloroplasts — Green plastids due to the presence of chlorophyll; they help in the synthesis of food by photosynthesis; in non-green plants, they help in storage of substances.

Leucoplasts — Colourless plastids that help in the storage of food.

Chromoplasts — Coloured plastids (e.g., red, yellow) responsible for the colour of flowers and fruits (e.g., tomatoes, carrots).

  • Lysosomes are present only in animal cells and help in cellular digestion.
  • Centrosomes are present only in animal cells and play a role in cell reproduction.
  • Plastids occur only in plant cells and are of three types: chloroplasts, leucoplasts, and chromoplasts.
  • Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll and carry out photosynthesis.
  • Chromoplasts give colour to flowers and fruits.

Differences Between a Plant Cell and an Animal Cell

  • Plant cells have a rigid, non-living cell wall; animal cells do not have a cell wall.
  • Plant cells usually have one or two large vacuoles; animal cells have vacuoles that are either absent or smaller in size.
  • Plastids are present in plant cells but absent in animal cells.
  • Both plant and animal cells have a cell membrane, nucleus, and mitochondria.

Levels of Organisation in the Body of a Living Organism

Tissue — A group of similar cells that form a type of tissue.

Organ — Different tissues organised together to form an organ.

Organ system — Many organs working together to form an organ system that performs specific functions.

Organism — All organ systems together forming a complete living being like a plant or an animal.

Multicellular — Organisms made up of many cells; the egg divides repeatedly to form a complete living being.

  • The cell is the basic unit of life; a group of similar cells forms a tissue.
  • Different tissues form an organ; many organs form an organ system; all organ systems form an organism.
  • The levels of organisation: Cell → Tissue → Organ → Organ system → Organism.
  • The life of a complex organism begins with a single cell (egg) that divides repeatedly.
  • Human beings, animals, and plants are multicellular organisms.

What are Microorganisms?

Microorganisms / Microbes — Living organisms which cannot be seen by the naked eyes (from mikros meaning small, bios meaning life); some can be seen with a magnifying glass or microscope.

Cyst — A hard outer covering formed by microorganisms to survive extreme conditions of temperature and dryness; when conditions become favourable, they come out, multiply, and go through their life cycles.

Foldscope — A low-cost, foldable paper microscope created by scientists that makes the microscopic world accessible to many people.

  • A microorganism may occur as a single cell or a collection of cells.
  • Microorganisms are very small and cannot be seen with naked eyes — they require a microscope.
  • They are found everywhere — in air, water, soil (desert or marshy land), and inside the bodies of other living organisms.
  • Microorganisms do not infect pickles and murabbas because salt and sugar act as preservatives; high concentrations prevent microbial growth.
  • Microorganisms can survive extreme conditions by forming a hard outer covering called a cyst.
  • Some microorganisms are helpful, while many are harmful as they cause diseases.

Anton Van Leeuwenhoek

  • Anton Van Leeuwenhoek of Holland was the first scientist to observe microscopic plants and other organisms including bacteria.
  • He made better lenses and built more useful microscopes in the 1660s.
  • He is known as the Father of Microbiology.

Types of Microorganisms

Bacteria — A major group of microorganisms; types include bacilli (rod-shaped), spirilla (spiral-shaped), and cocci (spherical, chain-shaped).

Fungi — A major group of microorganisms; examples include bread mould, Penicillium, and Aspergillus.

Algae — A major group of microorganisms; examples include Chlamydomonas, Nostoc, and Oscillatoria.

Protozoa — A major group of microorganisms; examples include Amoeba, Paramecium, and Euglena.

  • The four major groups of microorganisms are: Bacteria, Fungi, Algae, and Protozoa.
  • Bacteria reproduce every 9.5 minutes; E. coli reproduces every 20 minutes.
  • Each group has different shapes and forms, as shown in the chapter diagrams.

Viruses

Viruses — Acellular submicroorganisms that have characteristics of both living and non-living things; they are smaller than the smallest known cell (about 0.015 micron to 0.2 micron).

  • Viruses are acellular and have characteristics of both living and non-living things.
  • They inhabit and reproduce only inside the cells of living organisms (plants, animals, humans).
  • Viruses obtain their food from host cells and can only be seen under an electron microscope.
  • They exhibit a variety of shapes: rod-like, spherical, polygonal, etc.
  • When a virus enters a host cell, it multiplies; the host cell bursts and new virus particles enter other cells.
  • Names of viruses are derived from the host or disease caused (e.g., Tobacco mosaic virus, Bacteriophage, Measle virus, FMDV).

Where do Microorganisms Live?

  • Microorganisms are ubiquitous — they can live in all types of habitats.
  • They are found in air, water (including hot springs, ice-cold water, salty sea water), and soil (including deserts and marshy lands).
  • They live on dead and decaying organic matter and inside the bodies of animals/humans.
  • Some grow on other organisms while others exist freely.
  • Amoeba may live alone, whereas fungi and bacteria may live in colonies.

How are We Connected to Microbes?

Friendly microorganisms — Microorganisms that are useful for us.

Harmful microorganisms — Microorganisms that cause diseases and are harmful to us.

  • Microorganisms play a very important role in our lives and are divided into two classes: friendly and harmful.
  • Friendly microorganisms are useful in preparation of curd, bread, cake; production of alcohol; cleansing the environment; preparation of medicines; and improving soil fertility.

Friendly Microorganisms

  • Useful in preparation of curd, bread, cake, etc.
  • Useful in production of alcohol.
  • Useful in cleansing the environment by breaking down organic wastes.
  • Useful in preparation of certain medicines.
  • Useful in agriculture for improving soil fertility.

Making of Curd and Bread

Lactobacillus — A bacterium that promotes the formation of curd from milk by feeding on lactose (sugar in milk), multiplying, and fermenting the milk to form curd; it produces lactic acid which makes curd sour.

Renin — A bacterium used in cheese production.

Yeast — A beneficial fungus used in preparation of bakery products (bread, cakes) and food items like idli, dosa, dhokla; it causes fermentation of dough and liberates carbon dioxide, making food porous and fluffy.

  • Lactobacillus feeds on lactose in milk, multiplies, and ferments milk to form curd; lactic acid makes curd sour.
  • These bacteria grow well in warm conditions; curd is used in rava idlis and bhaturas for fermentation.
  • Yeast is used in bakery products and foods like idli, dosa, and dhokla.
  • Yeast causes fermentation of dough; carbon dioxide released during respiration makes the dough rise.
  • On baking/heating, dissolved carbon dioxide escapes, making the food item porous and fluffy.
  • Yeast is also used to improve the flavour of certain types of cheese.

Commercial Use of Microorganisms

Fermentation — The enzymatic breakdown of large organic molecules into smaller ones in the absence of air; alcohol is obtained from sugar by fermentation with yeast.

  • Certain microorganisms are used in industry to manufacture alcohol from sugar or starch, or wine from fruit juices.
  • Yeast contains the enzymes zymase and invertase.
  • Sugar → (zymase) → Glucose + Fructose; Glucose → (invertase) → Ethyl alcohol + Carbon dioxide.

Increasing Soil Fertility

Biological nitrogen fixers — Blue-green algae and symbiotic bacteria (like Rhizobium) that fix atmospheric nitrogen as nitrogen compounds in the soil.

Rhizobium — Symbiotic bacteria living in the root nodules of leguminous plants that absorb atmospheric nitrogen and convert it into nitrogenous compounds.

  • Blue-green algae can fix atmospheric nitrogen into nitrogen compounds in the soil.
  • Symbiotic bacteria like Rhizobium live in root nodules of leguminous plants (beans, peas, lentils) and convert atmospheric nitrogen into nitrogenous compounds.
  • Farmers grow legumes in rotation with other crops to naturally increase nitrogen in the soil.
  • Microorganisms enrich the soil with nitrogen and increase its fertility.
  • Microorganisms like fungi and bacteria decompose plant waste into simpler, nutrient-rich natural manure.

Key Players in Cleansing the Environment

  • Many microorganisms decompose dead organic waste of plants and animals into simpler, harmless substances.
  • These simpler substances get into the soil and are used by other plants — microorganisms act as cleansing agents of nature.
  • Anaerobic bacteria (which live without oxygen) degrade animal dung into biogas; the leftover slurry can be used as manure.
  • Methane gas released during decomposition is used as fuel for cooking, heating, generating electricity, and running vehicles.
  • Ancient Indian texts (Vedas like Atharvaveda) refer to the word krimi meaning tiny entities, both visible (drishya) and invisible (adrishya), and mention their beneficial and harmful effects.

Amazing Microalgae: Tiny Helpers in Water

Microalgae — Microscopic plant-like organisms that live in soil, water, air, and on trees; they make their own food using sunlight and release oxygen.

Spirulina — A microalga that is a super food; a good source of vitamin B12; more than 60% of its body weight is protein with little fat and sugar.

  • Microalgae are microscopic plant-like organisms found in soil, water, air, and on trees.
  • They make their own food using sunlight and release oxygen — more than half of Earth's oxygen supply.
  • They are rich in nutrients and act as food for many aquatic animals.
  • Spirulina, Chlorella, and Diatoms are important ingredients of health supplements and medicines.
  • Spirulina farming is becoming a feasible livelihood option; it can be grown at home in a glass tank with pond water.
  • Microalgae also help in cleaning water and are used to make biofuel.
  • Climate change, pollution, and habitat destruction threaten the diversity and abundance of microalgae.

Know a Scientist: Dr. Ananda Mohan Chakrabarty

  • Dr. Ananda Mohan Chakrabarty (1938–2020) was a scientist who studied bacteria.
  • He developed special bacteria that could break down oil spills in 1971.
  • He received a patent for his discovery in 1980, helping clean the environment and solve pollution problems.

Why is Cell Considered to be a Basic Unit of Life?

Unicellular — Organisms consisting of only one single cell (e.g., bacteria, protozoa, Amoeba); all body functions are carried out by a single cell.

Multicellular organisms — Organisms whose bodies consist of several million cells; various functions are divided among different groups of cells.

Nucleoid — A special feature of bacterial cells; instead of a well-defined nucleus and nuclear membrane, bacteria have a nucleoid region.

  • All human beings, plants, and animals consist of several million cells — they are multicellular organisms.
  • In multicellular organisms, various body functions are divided among different groups of cells.
  • Single-celled organisms (unicellular) like Amoeba perform all functions (capturing and digesting food, respiration, excretion, growth, reproduction) using just one cell.
  • Bacteria have a nucleoid instead of a well-defined nucleus and nuclear membrane, distinguishing them from cells of yeast, protozoa, algae, fungi, plants, and animals.
  • Fungi may be unicellular (yeast) or multicellular (mould); they have a cell wall but cannot make their own food because they lack chlorophyll.
  • An electron microscope can magnify a cell about 10,00,000 times, helping us see more structures present in a cell.

Key Terms

Acetabularia — A unicellular organism consisting of a single cell having a length of 10 cm, one of the largest single-celled organisms.

Algae — A group of microorganisms that are plant-like and can make their own food; examples include Chlamydomonas, Nostoc, and Oscillatoria.

Amoeba — A unicellular protozoan with an irregular shape that can change its form and performs all life functions within a single cell.

Bacteria — A major group of microorganisms that have a nucleoid instead of a well-defined nucleus and nuclear membrane; examples include Bacilli, Spirilla, and Cocci.

Bacteriophage — A virus that uses bacteria as its host; also known as a bacteria eater.

Cell — The smallest structural and functional unit of life, capable of performing all biological activities in a living organism.

Cell membrane — Also called plasma membrane; the thin delicate outer boundary of a cell that gives it shape and allows only certain selected substances to enter or leave.

Cell organelles — Small cytoplasmic bodies present in the cytoplasm that play an active role in the functioning of the cell.

Cell wall — An extra outer wall surrounding the cell membrane in plant cells and bacteria, made of stiff material called cellulose, providing protection and rigidity.

Cellular level — The lowest level of organisation in a living organism at which life exists, represented by the cell.

Cellulose — The stiff material that makes up the cell wall in plant cells.

Centrosomes — Cell organelles present only in animal cells that play a role in the reproduction of cells.

Chloroplasts — Green plastids containing chlorophyll that help in the synthesis of food by photosynthesis in plant cells.

Chromoplasts — Coloured plastids (red, yellow, etc.) responsible for the colour of flowers and fruits.

Chromosomes — Thread-like structures composed of DNA inside the nucleus that contain basic hereditary units called genes.

Cyst — A hard outer covering formed by some microorganisms to survive extreme conditions of temperature and dryness.

Cytoplasm — A thick jelly-like fluid inside the cell membrane, occupying the space between the cell membrane and the nucleus, where all life functions take place.

DNA — Deoxyribonucleic acid; the genetic material that makes up chromosomes and contains hereditary information.

Electron microscope — A high-magnification microscope that can magnify a cell about 10,00,000 times, used to see very small structures like viruses.

Endoplasmic reticulum — A cell organelle that helps in the transport of substances within the cell and synthesis of proteins.

III. HIGHER ORDER THINKING SKILLS — Critical Thinking (Page 37)

1. Why is virus not included in the category of microorganisms?

Answer: Viruses are not included in the category of microorganisms because they are acellular submicroorganisms that have the characteristics of both living and non-living things. The key reasons are:

  • Viruses cannot carry out any life activities on their own. They can only inhabit and reproduce inside the cells of living organisms (host cells).
  • Viruses obtain their food from the host cells — they cannot feed independently.
  • Viruses cannot survive or reproduce independently outside a host.
  • They are smaller than the smallest known cell (about 0.015 micron to 0.2 micron) and can only be seen under an electron microscope.

Since viruses do not show all the characteristics of living organisms (such as independent existence, feeding, and reproduction), they are not classified as true microorganisms.

2. Why does curd set faster in summer than in winter?

Answer: Curd sets faster in summer than in winter because the bacterium Lactobacillus, which converts milk into curd, grows well in warm conditions. The chapter explicitly states: "These bacteria grow well in warm conditions."

  • In summer, the higher temperature provides ideal conditions for Lactobacillus to multiply rapidly and ferment the lactose (milk sugar) into lactic acid quickly, causing the milk to turn into curd faster.
  • In winter, the lower temperature slows down the activity and reproduction of Lactobacillus bacteria, so the fermentation process takes longer and curd sets more slowly.

3. A cell requires water, minerals and other nutrients to survive. It is covered with a membrane. How do these things reach inside the cell?

Answer: Water, minerals, and other nutrients reach inside the cell through the cell membrane (plasma membrane). The chapter states that the cell membrane "allows only certain selected substances to enter or leave the cell." This means the cell membrane is selectively permeable. It controls and regulates the passage of materials in and out of the cell, allowing essential substances like water, minerals, and nutrients to enter while preventing harmful substances from entering. Thus, the cell membrane acts as a gateway of the cell, ensuring that the cell receives the necessary materials for its survival.

Q6. Describe the role of microorganisms in increasing soil fertility and in cleansing the environment.

Show Answer
✅ **Answer:** **Increasing soil fertility:** Blue-green algae can fix atmospheric nitrogen as nitrogen compounds in the soil. Symbiotic bacteria like *Rhizobium* live in the root nodules of leguminous plants (beans, peas, lentils) and absorb atmospheric nitrogen, converting it into nitrogenous compounds. This is why farmers grow legumes in rotation with other crops — it naturally increases nitrogen in the soil. Additionally, microorganisms like fungi and bacteria decompose plant and animal waste into simpler, nutrient-rich natural manure.

Cleansing the environment: Many microorganisms decompose dead organic waste of plants and animals into simpler and harmless substances. These simpler substances get into the soil and are used by other plants. Anaerobic bacteria degrade animal dung into biogas (a fuel source), and the leftover slurry can be used as manure. Thus, microorganisms act as the cleansing agents of nature.

Key Players in Cleansing the Environment

Many microorganisms decompose the dead organic waste of plants and animals into simpler and harmless substances.

Such simpler substances get into the soil and used by other plants. Thus, microorganisms act as the cleansing agent of the nature.

Many bacteria and fungi live in an oxygen-free environment. Some of these bacteria can decompose plant and animal waste present in the environment or household waste water. They release a mixture of gases containing carbon dioxide and high proportion of another gas called methane during this process. This gas has been used as a fuel source for cooking, heating, generate electricity and to even run vehicles.

Anaerobic bacteria (which can live without oxygen) degrade the animal dung into biogas and the left-over slurry can be used as a manure.

WHY IS CELL CONSIDERED TO BE A BASIC UNIT OF LIFE ?

The body of all human beings, plants and animals consist of several million cells. Therefore, they are called multicellular organisms. In multicellular organisms, the various functions in the body are divided among different groups of cells.

Certain organisms consist of only one (single) cell. The single-celled organisms are called unicellular. The microorganisms, bacteria and protozoa are unicellular.

In unicellular organisms, all functions of the body are carried out by a single cell. A single-celled organism amoeba performs all the functions (such as capturing and digesting food, respiration, excretion, growing, reproduction) as done by multicellular organisms using specialised group of cells.

Some microorganisms like algae and fungi contain a few one or more cells. The smaller number of cells in smaller organisms does not in any way affect the functioning of such organisms.

Fungi may be made up of one or more cells. Mould is a multicellular fungus while yeast is a unicellular fungus.
