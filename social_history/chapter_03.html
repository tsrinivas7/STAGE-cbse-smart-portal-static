WHO WERE THE MARATHAS?

Yadava dynasty — The ruling dynasty of Maharashtra in the 13th century with Devagiri as its capital.
Khilji Sultanate of Delhi — The power that defeated the Yadavas in the early 14th century.
Deccan Sultanates — Different powers that controlled the region later, under whom Maratha chiefs served.
Bhakti movement — A spiritual movement between the 7th and 17th centuries that inspired people with devotional songs.
Abhangas — Devotional hymns by Tukaram emphasizing faith, equality, and humility.

  • The Marathas were warriors, farmers, and administrators living in the Deccan plateau.
  • They preserved their unique language, customs, and traditions during political changes.
  • Saints like Dnyaneshwar, Namdev, Tukaram, and Ramdas prepared people for unity.
  • By the 17th century, Shivaji Maharaj rose to power combining military skill and popular support.

EXTENDED LEARNING

Maharashtra Desha — An ancient term meaning 'the great nation,' from which the name Marathas is derived.

  • Small Maratha families like the Bhonsles, Ghatges, and Moreys served as local defenders.
  • This local unity laid the groundwork for later political organisation.

SHIVAJI

Dharma — Righteousness; values of bravery, honesty, and justice taught to Shivaji by his mother.
Swarajya — Self-rule; the guiding principle of Shivaji's life and foundation of the empire.
Torna Fort — The first major fort captured by Shivaji in 1645, marking the beginning of the Maratha state.
Wāgh nākh — Tiger claws; sharp metal weapons worn on the hand used by Shivaji.

  • Shivaji was born in 1630 into the respected Bhonsle family in Pune.
  • His mother Jijabai shaped his character with stories from the Ramayana and Mahabharata.
  • He received military training in horse riding, sword fighting, and archery.
  • He developed a unique style of warfare using the rugged terrain of the Sahyadri hills.

Capture of Forts

Raigad — A fort with secret water tanks, crucial for Maratha strategy.
Pratapgad — A fort built to blend into the hills, known for its strategic location.

  • Shivaji captured Torna Fort at sixteen, starting the Maratha state.
  • He expanded control over forts like Purandar, Rajgad, and Kondana (Sinhagad).
  • Forts acted as administrative centres, storehouses, and safe shelters for people.
  • Shivaji used clever negotiations and surprise raids instead of large battles.

Maratha Navy

Konkan coast — The coastal region of Maharashtra often attacked by foreign powers.
Portuguese, British and Dutch — Foreign powers that attacked the Konkan coast.
Siddis of Janjira — Enemies with strong naval bases along the coast.
Proper naval force — A rare naval force built by Shivaji to protect trade routes.
Sindhudurg, Vijaydurg and Suvarnadurg — Strong sea forts constructed by Shivaji.
Kanhoji Angre — A famous Maratha admiral who made the navy powerful.

  • Shivaji realised controlling seas was as important as controlling land.
  • He built sea forts equipped with cannons and secret escape routes.
  • The navy protected fishermen, traders, and villages from pirate raids.
  • It gave Shivaji control over maritime trade along the Arabian Sea.

Guerrilla Warfare

Guerrilla tactics — Fighting methods depending on speed, surprise, and clever use of geography.

  • Soldiers attacked suddenly at night or in rough terrain and disappeared quickly.
  • This method allowed small groups to defeat much stronger armies.
  • It saved time and resources and confused larger enemy armies.
  • Historians credit Shivaji as one of the earliest rulers in Asia to formalise this doctrine.

Major Encounters

Afzal Khan — A powerful general of Bijapur sent to defeat Shivaji.
Shaista Khan — A Mughal nobleman who invaded Shivaji's territories.
Surat — A rich Mughal port city raided by Shivaji for treasure.
London Gazette — An English newspaper that reported on Shivaji's fame.

  • In 1659, Shivaji killed Afzal Khan using wāgh nākh at Pratapgad Fort.
  • In 1663, he carried out a daring night raid on Shaista Khan's camp in Pune.
  • In 1664, he sacked Surat and seized enormous treasure worth nearly one crore rupees.
  • These events spread Shivaji's fame far beyond Maharashtra.

Treaty of Purandar and Agra Episode

Treaty of Purandar — A treaty signed in 1665 where Shivaji handed over 23 forts to the Mughals.
Agra Episode — Shivaji's imprisonment and subsequent escape from Aurangzeb's court in 1666.

  • Shivaji signed the treaty to protect his people from a massive Mughal army.
  • He was insulted at the Mughal court and placed under house arrest.
  • He escaped disguised in baskets filled with sweets and fruits.
  • This episode showed his determination to protect independence at any cost.

Coronation at Raigad (1674)

Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj — The formal royal title given to Shivaji upon coronation.
Shri Raja Shiva Chhatrapati — Shivaji's full formal royal title.
Rāyābhiṣheka shaka — The coronation era started by Shivaji.
Chhatra — Meaning umbrella.
Pati — Meaning King.

  • Shivaji organised a grand coronation ceremony at Raigad in June 1674.
  • He was crowned with full Vedic rites, reviving Hindu kingship based on dharma.
  • New coins were minted and royal seals inscribed in Sanskrit.
  • Nearly 50,000 people attended the celebrations, marking the official birth of the Maratha Empire.

Southern Campaign (Dakshina Digvijaya)

Dakshina Digvijaya — Also known as the Southern Conquest, the last phase of Shivaji's life.

  • Shivaji turned attention to southern regions like Karnataka and Tamil Nadu.
  • These regions were rich in fertile land, resources, and trade routes.
  • He opposed European slave trading practices on the southern coast.
  • This campaign prepared the ground for future Maratha expansion.

Shivaji's Legacy and Death

Alexander the Great — Ancient generals compared to Shivaji by European travellers.
Chhatrasal — A Bundela prince motivated by Shivaji to establish an independent kingdom.
Bhushan — A famous Hindi poet who composed stirring poems in praise of Shivaji.
Swarajya — The dream of an independent Maratha kingdom that encouraged later rulers.

  • Shivaji died in 1680 at Raigad Fort after falling seriously ill.
  • He left behind a powerful state, loyal army, and efficient administration.
  • His legacy lived on in the strength of the empire and inspiration to future generations.
  • He is celebrated as one of the greatest heroes of Indian history.

THE MARATHAS AFTER SHIVAJI

Swarajya — The ideals of freedom, justice, and independence founded by Shivaji.

  • Leaders like Sambhaji, Rajaram, and Tarabai kept the struggle alive against Aurangzeb.
  • The Marathas relied on guerrilla tactics and the rugged geography of the Deccan.
  • They proved that Shivaji's vision was strong enough to outlive him.

Sambhaji (1681-1689)

Sambhaji — The elder son of Shivaji who became the Chhatrapati after his death.

  • He faced Aurangzeb personally coming to the Deccan with a massive army.
  • He fought back fiercely for almost a decade using guerrilla tactics and direct battles.
  • In 1689, he was betrayed, captured, and executed for refusing to surrender.
  • His sacrifice inspired the Marathas to continue their struggle for Swarajya.

Rajaram and Tarabai

Rajaram — Shivaji's younger son who became the new Chhatrapati after Sambhaji.
Gingee — The strong fortress in Tamil Nadu where Rajaram shifted the capital.
Tarabai — The widow of Rajaram who emerged as a remarkable and courageous leader.

  • Rajaram shifted the capital to Gingee to stay away from Aurangzeb's base.
  • He continued the fight using guerrilla warfare until his death in 1700.
  • Tarabai took full control of the kingdom and led armies with great skill.
  • She organised large Maratha armies to invade Mughal territories in the north.

RISE OF THE PESHWAS

Peshwas — Persian term meaning 'prime minister'; originally chief ministers who eventually held more power than the Chhatrapati.
Pune — The capital established by the Peshwas, becoming the centre of Maratha politics.
Bajirao I — A brilliant cavalry commander remembered as the most famous Peshwa (1720–1740).
Nanasahab Peshwa — Bajirao's son who consolidated Maratha power and strengthened administration.
Mahadji Shinde (Scindia) — A powerful Maratha general who regained influence in the north.
Holkers — Powerful Maratha families that expanded influence across northern India.
Gaekwads — Powerful Maratha families that expanded influence across northern India.

  • The centralised state gave way to a decentralised confederacy under the Peshwas.
  • The Peshwas managed civil administration and directed extensive campaigns.
  • Under their leadership, the Marathas became the most decisive political force of 18th-century India.
  • They transformed the Maratha state into a powerful empire stretching from the Deccan to Delhi.

Expansion under the Peshwas

Lightning raids — Rapid cavalry strikes known as Bajirao's rapid cavalry raids.

  • Bajirao I believed the declining Mughal Empire could be replaced by Maratha dominance.
  • His rapid cavalry raids brought large territories under Maratha control.
  • Generals like Scindia, Holkers, and Gaekwads marched as far as Delhi and Lahore.
  • By the mid-18th century, the Marathas were the most powerful force after the Mughals declined.

Battle of Panipat (1761)

Third Battle of Panipat — Fought on 14 January 1761, one of the biggest battles in Indian history.
Ahmad Shah Abdali — The Afghan ruler who invaded India and defeated the Marathas.
Madhavrao I — The able Peshwa who led the Marathas to recover strength after Panipat.

  • The Marathas suffered a terrible defeat with thousands of soldiers killed.
  • This shattered their dream of establishing complete control over northern India.
  • However, they recovered under Madhavrao I and Mahadji Shinde.
  • In 1771, they recaptured Delhi, remaining influential until the British seized it.

Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1818)

First Anglo-Maratha War — Fought 1775–1782, ended with the Treaty of Salbai.
Second Anglo-Maratha War — Fought 1803–1805, where British defeated powerful Maratha chiefs.
Third Anglo-Maratha War — Fought 1817–1818, the final conflict ending Maratha political independence.
Treaty of Salbai — A treaty bringing temporary peace after the First Anglo-Maratha War.

  • The British East India Company saw the Marathas as the biggest obstacle to expansion.
  • Internal disunity weakened the Marathas during the Third War.
  • By 1818, almost all Maratha territories were annexed by the British.
  • The British gained control of India more from the Marathas than from the Mughals.

MARATHA ADMINISTRATION

Civil Administration

Ashta Pradhana Maṇḍala — Council of eight ministers created by Shivaji to govern effectively.
Jagirs — Hereditary rights granting land that often led to corruption, avoided by Shivaji.
Amatya — Finance Minister in the council of eight ministers.
Pradhan — Prime Minister in the council of eight ministers.
Sachiv — Land Revenue Minister in the council of eight ministers.
Nyayādhiśh — Chief Justice in the council of eight ministers.
Panditrāo — Head of Religious Affairs in the council of eight ministers.
Senāpati — Commander-in-chief in the council of eight ministers.
Sumant — Minister for Foreign Affairs in the council of eight ministers.
Mantri — Intelligence Minister in the council of eight ministers.

  • Shivaji reduced the power of old feudal nobles and replaced it with a fair system.
  • Officers were paid fixed salaries from the state treasury to ensure loyalty.
  • Officials were transferred regularly to avoid misuse of power.
  • The welfare of soldiers was prioritized, including pensions for widows.

Military Administration

Infantry — Foot soldiers in the Maratha army.
Cavalry — Horsemen in the Maratha army, the backbone of the forces.
Navy — Naval wing of the Maratha army.
Bārgirs — Soldiers provided with horses, arms, and salaries by the state.
Shiledārs — Soldiers who maintained their own horses and weapons but fought under state command.

  • The army was divided into infantry, cavalry, and navy.
  • Cavalry was divided into Bārgirs and Shiledārs to balance government control.
  • They used traditional weapons as well as firearms, cannons, and rockets.
  • Guerrilla tactics made their military flexible and effective against larger armies.

Revenue Administration

Chauth — A one-fourth (25%) tribute collected from areas not directly ruled by the Marathas.
Sardeshmukhi — An additional 10% tax claimed as the right of the Marathas for being traditional chiefs.

  • Land was measured carefully and revenue assessed based on soil and crops.
  • Farmers felt secure knowing how much tax they had to pay.
  • Detailed record-keeping ensured no farmer was unfairly burdened.
  • These taxes allowed the Marathas to expand influence without directly ruling every region.

Forts

Fortresses — Strong stone structures built on hills or near coasts to protect people and store supplies.
Raigad, Pratapgad, Sinhagadh — Examples of strongholds of Maratha power.

  • Forts were the lifeline of Maratha power and defence.
  • Built on mountains, near rivers, and along the coast for security and trade control.
  • They had strong stone walls, secret passages, and clever designs.
  • Ramchandrapant Amatya said without forts, the kingdom would not survive.

Navy

Kanhoji Angre — Admiral under whose command the Maratha navy grew powerful.
Mocha, Muscat, Malacca — Important ports for overseas trade developed by the Marathas.

  • Shivaji built a strong fleet to guard the Konkan coast and prevent piracy.
  • The navy demanded passes from foreign ships entering their waters.
  • Knowledge of the coastline and swift tactics gave them an advantage over Europeans.
  • They maintained supremacy in the Arabian Sea for many years.

Justice

Panchayats — Councils of respected village elders that settled small disputes.
Kotwals — Police officials in towns who maintained law and order.

  • Shivaji gave great importance to fair justice for ordinary people.
  • Disputes were settled quickly and cheaply by panchayats at the local level.

WHO WERE THE MARATHAS?

The Marathas were a community of warriors, farmers and administrators who lived in the Deccan plateau, mainly in present-day Maharashtra. They are closely associated with the Marathi language, which developed into a strong medium of literature and culture. Over time, the Marathas grew from being local chiefs and cultivators into a powerful political force that shaped the course of Indian history. Their rise was not only due to battles but also because of cultural revival, social unity and political organisation.

During the 13th century, much of Maharashtra was ruled by the Yadava dynasty, with Devagiri (modern Daulatabad) as its capital. However, in the early 14th century, the Yadavas were defeated by the Khilji Sultanate of Delhi. Later, different Deccan Sultanates controlled the region and the Maratha chiefs had to serve under them. Even during this period of political change, the people of Maharashtra preserved their unique language, customs and traditions, which gave them a strong cultural identity.

A major source of Maratha strength came from the Bhakti movement, which spread across India between the 7th and 17th centuries. Saints such as Dnyaneshwar, Namdev, Tukaram and Ramdas inspired people with devotional songs and simple poetry in Marathi, making spiritual teachings accessible to ordinary villagers. Dnyaneshwar translated the Bhagavad–Gītā into Marathi, while Tukaram’s abhangas (devotional hymns) moved people with their emphasis on faith, equality and humility. These saints also criticised social divisions and gave common people confidence in their own worth, preparing them to stand united against oppression. This cultural awakening laid the groundwork for later political unity. By the 17th century, some Maratha chiefs made attempts at independence, but they were not successful. However, the people were ready for a leader who could combine military skill, administrative wisdom and popular support. It was in this environment that Shivaji Maharaj rose to power. Inspired by the cultural traditions of Maharashtra and by the struggles of his time, he built a strong and disciplined state. His dream of Swarāja (self-rule) not only gave the Marathas political strength but also inspired later generations of Indians in their fight for freedom.

Guerrilla Warfare

One of Shivaji's most brilliant innovations in warfare was the use of guerrilla tactics, which depended on speed, surprise and clever use of geography. At a time when most rulers fought long, open-field battles with large armies, Shivaji realised that the Marathas, with their smaller numbers, could not always win in such battles against the huge Mughal and Bijapur forces. Instead, he trained his soldiers to fight smartly.

In this method, soldiers would attack suddenly at night or in rough terrain, loot enemy camps, destroy supplies and then disappear quickly into the hills, forests or narrow passes of the Sahyadris before the enemy could respond. Since the Marathas knew the local terrain very well, they could move swiftly, hide easily and confuse larger armies. This made it almost impossible for the enemy to capture Shivaji or defeat him in battle.

Guerrilla warfare allowed small groups of Maratha soldiers to defeat much stronger armies, saving both time and resources. It gave the Marathas a strong advantage because their enemies could never predict when or where the next attack would happen.

Shivaji's clever use of this strategy earned him the title of a master military strategist.

This method was so great that it became an inspiration for later generations. Even during India's struggle for freedom in the 19th and 20th centuries, freedom fighters used guerrilla-style surprise attacks against colonial powers. Thus, Shivaji's tactics were not only successful in his own time but also became a legacy for future struggles against oppression.

Major Encounters

Shivaji's early victories showed not only his courage and bravery but also his sharp intelligence and strategy. In 1659, Afzal Khan, a very powerful general

of Bijapur, was sent with a large army to defeat Shivaji. The two leaders met at Pratapgad Fort under the pretense of peace talks. Afzal Khan tried to attack Shivaji with hidden weapons, but Shivaji, alert and prepared, defended himself using his wāgh nākh (tiger claws – sharp metal weapons worn on the hand) and killed him. This sudden event threw Afzal Khan's army into confusion and Shivaji's men used this chance to win a decisive Maratha victory, boosting Shivaji's reputation as a fearless leader.

Soon afterwards, the Mughal nobleman Shaista Khan invaded Shivaji's territories with a large army and occupied Pune for nearly three years. Finally, in 1663, with only a small group of soldiers, Shivaji carried out one of his most daring acts—a surprise night raid on Khan's camp. Shivaji and his men quietly entered his residence and launched an attack. Shaista Khan was badly injured, losing some of his fingers and barely managed to escape. Humiliated by this defeat, he left Maharashtra and was later transferred by Aurangzeb. This bold raid is often compared to a modern-day surgical strike because it was sudden, precise and highly effective.

In 1664, Shivaji turned towards Surat, one of the richest Mughal port cities in present-day Gujarat. Surat was a centre of trade and finance, filled with warehouses, merchants and officials loyal to the Mughals. Shivaji's army raided the city and seized enormous treasure worth nearly one crore rupees—a massive amount at that time. The wealth was wisely used to strengthen his forts, expand his army and build his navy. Shivaji was careful not to attack places of worship or harm ordinary people. He even spared the house of Mohandas Parekh, a respected charitable man. A few years later, he sacked Surat once again to secure more resources.

These events became so famous that reports appeared in the London Gazette, an English newspaper of the time. The paper mentioned how Shivaji wrote to European diplomatic agents in Surat, demanding 'immediate presents of money,' and warned that if they refused, he would 'return and ruin that city.'

These three encounters—the killing of Afzal Khan, the daring raid on Shaista Khan and the sacking of Surat—spread Shivaji's fame far beyond Maharashtra. People across India began to see him as a heroic figure who fought for Swarājya (self-rule) against powerful enemies. His actions not only gave confidence to his followers but also inspired respect and fear even among his rivals.

Treaty of Purandar and Agra Episode

The sacking of Surat in 1664 was a huge blow to the Mughal Empire, as Surat was one of its richest port cities and a centre of trade and wealth. Shivaji's daring raid greatly insulted the power and prestige of Emperor Aurangzeb. To defeat the growing Maratha power, Aurangzeb sent Raja Jai Singh of Amber, a distinguished Rajput general, with a massive and well-equipped army.

In 1665, Shivaji's forces, though brave and determined, were not strong enough to face such a large Mughal army in open battle. To protect his people and avoid unnecessary destruction, Shivaji was forced to accept defeat at Purandar Fort near Pune and signed the Treaty of Purandar. According to this treaty, Shivaji had to hand over 23 forts and a large part of his territory to the Mughals. He was, however, allowed to retain control over 12 forts and some adjoining lands. As part of the terms, his son Sambhaji had to enter Mughal service as a mark of loyalty. Although this seemed like a setback, Shivaji used it as a temporary compromise to safeguard the core of his Swarājya (self-rule) and to prepare for future opportunities.

Jai Singh later persuaded Shivaji to visit the Mughal court at Agra in 1666. However, instead of being treated with honour, Shivaji was insulted when Aurangzeb placed him behind lesser nobles and even a Mughal general whom he had defeated earlier. Angered by this humiliation, Shivaji stormed out of the court. Soon after, Aurangzeb placed him under strict house arrest, with guards watching his every move.

Coronation at Raigad (1674)

After years of constant battles, clever strategies and building a strong administration, Shivaji felt it was time to formally declare himself a sovereign and independent ruler. Until then, he was often considered just a local chief or rebel by larger powers like the Mughals and the Deccan Sultanates. In this magnificent ceremony, Shivaji was coronated with full Vedic rites and crowned as Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj, the protector and sovereign of the Marathas. This was very significant because it revived the idea of Hindu kingship based on dharma (righteousness) at a time when most of India was ruled by the Mughals or the Sultanates. The coronation thus gave a strong cultural and religious identity to the Maratha kingdom.

RISE OF THE PESHWAS

After Shivaji's death and the weakening of direct royal authority, the Maratha kingdom gradually changed its structure. The centralised state of Shivaji's time gave way to a more decentralised confederacy, where different chiefs and powerful families controlled separate regions but still remained loyal to the larger Maratha banner. The Chhatrapati continued as the symbolic head of the state, but the real authority slowly shifted to the Peshwas (Persian term meaning 'prime minister'). Originally appointed as chief ministers to assist the king, the Peshwas eventually rose to hold more power than the Chhatrapati himself. Their capital was established at Pune, which became the centre of Maratha politics and administration.

The Peshwas played a crucial role in providing strong organisation, effective leadership and military strategy. From Pune, they not only managed civil administration but also directed extensive campaigns that expanded Maratha power far beyond Maharashtra. They preserved Shivaji's spirit of Swarajya (self-rule) while transforming the Maratha state into a powerful empire. Under their leadership, the Marathas became the most decisive political force of 18th-century India, often influencing the Mughal court in Delhi and shaping the destiny of the subcontinent.

By the latter half of the 18th century, the chief rivals of the British in India were the Marathas. At this time the Mughal Empire had declined and the Marathas were still a powerful force, though divided among several chiefs like the Scindias, Holkars, Bhonsles and Gaekwads. The British East India Company, with its growing strength, modern weapons and superior organisation, saw the Marathas as the biggest obstacle to their expansion in India. A series of three wars, known as the Anglo-Maratha Wars, were fought between 1775 and 1818. These were ultimately ended Maratha political independence.

  • First Anglo-Maratha War (1775–1782): This war began due to the succession disputes within the Maratha leadership. The British tried to take advantage of these internal rivalries. However, the Marathas fought bravely and the war ended with the Treaty of Salbai, which brought temporary peace and left the Marathas still strong.
  • Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803–1805): In this phase, the British defeated some of the most powerful Maratha chiefs, including the Scindias and Bhonsles. The Marathas lost key territories, which greatly reduced their strength and influence in India. The war highlighted the superior military organisation and modern technology of the British compared to the Marathas' divided forces.

The British launched well-planned campaigns with their modern army, completely defeating and crushing Maratha resistance. By 1818, almost all Maratha territories were annexed by the British, marking the end of the British Maratha political power in India. In reality, the British gained control of India more from the Marathas than from the Mughals or any other power, since the Marathas were the last major Indian force resisting them.

Yet, the Maratha legacy did not die. Their bravery, resistance and love for Swarājya (self-rule) inspired later generations of Indians. Many 19th- and 20th-century freedom fighters drew inspiration from the Marathas' long struggle against the British, keeping their spirit alive in India's independence movement.

Revenue Administration

A kingdom cannot run without revenue and Shivaji introduced a well-planned and fair taxation system. Land was measured carefully and revenue was assessed based on the type of soil and crops that could be grown, making the process more accurate and just. Farmers felt secure because they knew how much tax they had to pay and were protected from exploitation by middlemen. Two special taxes were also introduced:

  • Chauth: A one-fourth (25%) tribute collected from areas not directly ruled by the Marathas, in return for military protection.
  • Sardeshmukhi: An additional 10% tax claimed as the right of the Marathas for being the traditional chiefs or overlords of Maharashtra.

These taxes allowed the Marathas to expand their influence without directly ruling every region. Detailed record-keeping was also maintained so that no farmer was unfairly burdened. This revenue system not only gave the state a steady income but also built trust between the people and the Maratha rulers.

MARATHA ADMINISTRATION

Civil Administration

Shivaji set up a strong and centralised civil administration that reduced the excessive power of old feudal nobles and replaced it with a fair system based on merit and responsibility. Instead of granting land as jagirs (hereditary rights that often led to corruption), he ensured his officers were paid fixed salaries from the state treasury. This made them loyal to the government rather than to powerful families. To avoid misuse of power, officials were also transferred regularly. The welfare of soldiers and their families was given importance – for instance, if a soldier died in battle, his widow received a pension and sometimes his son was given a government job, which was a rare practice in those times. To govern effectively, Shivaji created the ashta pradhāna maṇḍala (council of eight ministers). Each minister had a specific duty, such as handling finances, justice, military affairs, foreign relations or religious matters. This division of work made the administration systematic, efficient and fair, ensuring that power was not concentrated in one hand.

Military Administration

The Maratha army became one of the strongest and most feared forces in India because of its discipline, speed and organisation. It was divided into three wings: infantry (foot soldiers), cavalry (horsemen) and navy. The cavalry was the backbone of the army and was divided into two categories:

  • Bārgirs: Soldiers provided with horses, arms and salaries by the state.
  • Shiledārs: Soldiers who maintained their own horses and weapons but fought under state command.

This system balanced government control with private contribution. The Marathas used both traditional weapons like swords, spears and bows as well as firearms and cannons. Remarkably, they even experimented with rockets, showing their innovative spirit. They used guerrilla tactics—striking the enemy suddenly and escaping into hills or forests—which gave them an advantage over much larger Mughal armies. This made their military both flexible and effective.

Forts

Forts were the lifeline of Maratha power and defence. Built on mountains, near rivers and along the coast, they provided security, control over trade routes and protection from enemy invasions. Forts like Raigad, Pratapgad and Sinhagadh became strongholds of Maratha power. These forts had strong stone walls, secret passages and clever designs to make them difficult to capture. They were not just military bases but also administrative centres, food and weapon storehouses and safe shelters for people during attacks. Ramchandrapant Amatya, a senior Maratha minister, famously said that without forts, the kingdom would not survive—showing how central they were to Maratha strategy.

Navy

Shivaji was one of the first Indian rulers to understand the importance of a navy in defending the kingdom. He built a strong fleet to guard the Konkan coast, prevent piracy and control sea trade. Under the command of Kanhoji Angre, the Maratha navy grew powerful enough that even European powers like the Portuguese and the British had to recognise Maratha authority at sea. The Marathas demanded passes from foreign ships entering their waters, showing their dominance. Although their ships were smaller and less advanced than European ones, their knowledge of the coastline and swift tactics gave them an advantage. For many years, they maintained supremacy in the Arabian Sea, protecting trade and strengthening Maratha power.

Justice

Shivaji gave great importance to fair justice for ordinary people. Small disputes were settled by panchayats, which were councils of respected village elders. This made justice quick, cheap and fair at the local level. In towns, kotwals (police officials) maintained law and order, while more serious cases went to higher authorities. Harsh punishments like the death penalty were rare; instead, punishments were moderate and based on fairness. People also had the right to appeal to higher officials if they felt wronged. Shivaji also instructed his officers to protect people from exploitation and not to harm farmers, women or ordinary citizens during wars. This created a sense of trust between the people and the Maratha rulers.

Trade and Economy

The Marathas understood that a strong economy was as important as a strong army. To improve trade, they built and maintained roads, river transport, ferries and bridges, which made the movement of goods and people easier and safer. Well-connected routes linked villages, towns and forts, helping farmers sell their crops and traders carry merchandise without too many delays. This improved communication also supported military needs, as soldiers and supplies could be moved quickly across regions.

The Marathas also engaged in maritime trade by developing coastal ports and building a navy. Important ports such as Mocha, Muscat and Malacca became centres of overseas trade. Through these ports, Indian goods like cotton textiles, spices and food grains were exchanged for horses, weapons and luxury items from other countries. This international trade not only brought wealth and prosperity but also increased the Marathas' political importance.

By encouraging both inland and overseas trade, the Marathas ensured that farmers, artisans and merchants benefited. This wealth supported their administration and army, helping them expand their power. The focus on trade and economy shows that the Marathas were not just warriors but also skilled organisers who cared about the prosperity of their people.

THE ROLE OF MARATHA WOMEN

Women in the Maratha period were not just confined to households; many of them played important roles in politics, administration, culture and even warfare. They became symbols of courage, wisdom and dedication and their contributions kept the Maratha spirit alive during some of the most difficult times in history. Two of the most inspiring women of this era were Tarabai and Ahilyabai Holkar.

Tarabai

[IMAGE_START] A painting of a woman (Tarabai) riding a white horse. She is wearing traditional Indian clothing (yellow/orange). The background shows a landscape with hills. [IMAGE_END]
Tarabai

Tarabai, the daughter-in-law of Shivaji and wife of Rajaram, emerged as a fearless warrior queen after the death of her husband. At a time when the Marathas were under constant attack by Aurangzeb, she personally took charge of the kingdom and led armies with great skill. Tarabai adopted guerrilla tactics, just like Shivaji, to harass and weaken Mughal forces. She not only defended Maratha territories but also realised that with

Aurangzeb busy in the Deccan, north India was left at risk. Taking advantage of this, she organised large Maratha armies and sent them to invade Mughal territories in the north. In this sense, Tarabai was the true architect of the Maratha northward expansion. Her courage, intelligence and determination outsmarted the Mughal Empire and preserved Maratha independence during a critical period. She remains an inspiration for later generations of women leaders in India.

Ahilyabai Holkar

Ahilyabai Holkar, one of the most respected queens in Indian history, belonged to the powerful Holkar dynasty that played a major role in Maratha expansion in north India. After the tragic death of her husband and son, she ruled the Malwa kingdom for thirty years with wisdom, fairness and compassion. Ahilyabai was known for her just governance, where she reduced corruption, cared for the common people and ensured justice for all. She was deeply religious and is remembered for rebuilding many temples destroyed during earlier invasions. Most famously, she rebuilt the Kashi Vishwanath temple in Varanasi and the Somnath temple in Gujarat, restoring their cultural and spiritual importance. She also undertook large public works by constructing wells, ghats, roads and rest houses across India—from Kedarnath in the north to Rameswaram in the south. In addition, she promoted trade and gave special encouragement to the Maheshwar weaving industry in Madhya Pradesh, which continues to produce its famous handloom sarees even today.

Ahilyabai’s rule is often described as a golden period, because she combined simplicity and devotion with efficient governance, cultural revival and economic development. She was not only a queen but also a symbol of motherly care, earning love and respect from her people across India.

MARATHAS IN THANJAVUR

The Marathas not only made their mark in Maharashtra but also established themselves in southern India, creating a unique chapter in Indian history. Their rule in Thanjavur (in present-day Tamil Nadu) brought together northern and southern traditions, giving rise to a rich cultural heritage that continues to influence the region.

Ekoji and the Founding of the Dynasty

Shivaji’s half-brother Ekoji (also called Venkoji) conquered the Thanjavur region in the late 17th century, marking the beginning of Maratha rule in the area. He founded the Thanjavur Maratha dynasty and his descendants ruled for many generations. This brought Maratha culture, administration and traditions to Tamil Nadu, while also respecting and adapting to local Tamil practices. The Thanjavur Marathas were not just rulers but also patrons of literature, music and drama and many rulers themselves were poets and dramatists.

Serfoji II’s Contributions

Among the rulers, Serfoji II (1798–1832) stands out as the most famous and influential ruler. A learned scholar, he was well-versed in Indian as well as European languages. He even wrote a Marathi play called Devendra Kuravanji, which described world geography in detail as it was known at that time. Serfoji II’s court became a vibrant cultural hub where literature in Marathi, Tamil, Telugu and Sanskrit flourished.

He patronised musicians and it was under his guidance that modern Carnatic music took shape, while the dance form Bharatanatyam entered a new stage of revival. Serfoji also encouraged medical knowledge by establishing the Dhanwantari Mahal, a centre that offered free treatment using both Indian and Western systems of medicine. Importantly, he introduced printing technology by setting up one of the earliest printing presses in India run by a native

Syncretic Culture

The Thanjavur Maratha rulers promoted a syncretic culture—a blending of traditions that brought together Tamil, Telugu and Marathi influences. Their courts were centres of art, music and learning, where different cultural streams enriched each other. This created a vibrant, multilingual cultural environment full of innovation. The legacy of the Thanjavur Marathas can still be seen today in the region's music, dance, architecture and literary traditions, making their rule an important link between the north and the south of India.

THE MARATHA LEGACY

The rise of the Marathas marked one of the most important chapters in Indian history, as they built a powerful empire and left behind lessons of unity, courage and administration. Their achievements in politics, military and culture shaped India's destiny and influenced later struggles for freedom. Following are some of the major contributions that form the lasting legacy of the Marathas:

  • Challenge to Mughal Supremacy: The Marathas emerged as one of the strongest Indian powers in the 17th and 18th centuries. They repeatedly challenged Mughal authority at a time when the empire was at its peak. Through their military campaigns and clever use of guerrilla warfare, they broke Mughal dominance in many parts of India. By the mid-18th century, the Marathas had created the largest Indian empire before the British, stretching from the Deccan to Delhi and beyond. This proved that Indian powers could unite and resist mighty empires.
  • Efficient Administration and Military Strength: The Marathas established a well-organised system of governance, inspired by Shivaji's vision. Their administration was based on merit, discipline and careful revenue collection. They maintained a strong military that included infantry, cavalry, forts and even a navy. The system of chauth and sardeshmukhi gave them steady income while expanding their influence. Their ability to run an efficient government and maintain a disciplined army made them a model for later Indian rulers.
  • Encouragement of Cultural Revival: The Marathas not only focused on wars but also promoted culture, literature, religion and regional pride. They rebuilt temples destroyed in earlier invasions, patronised poets and scholars and promoted the use of Marathi and the Modi script in administration. They also encouraged arts, music and dance, blending local traditions with broader Indian culture. This revival gave people a sense of identity and unity.
  • Inspiration for Later Freedom Struggles: Perhaps the greatest legacy of the Marathas was the idea of Swarajya or self-rule. Shivaji's dream of an independent Indian state, free from foreign domination, inspired generations. During the 19th and 20th centuries, leaders of India's freedom movement often remembered Shivaji and the Marathas as symbols of courage and independence. Their resistance to the Mughals and later the British became a source of inspiration for freedom fighters like Bal Gangadhar Tilak, who proudly declared, 'Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it.'

EXTENDED LEARNING

The famous Indian leader Bal Gangadhar Tilak revived the public celebration of Ganesh Chaturthi in the 1890s to promote unity and nationalism. He chose Shivaji as a cultural hero, calling him the 'Father of Indian Nationalism'—linking Maratha Swarajya to modern India's freedom movement.

KEY TERMS

Swarajya
: A Marathi term meaning 'self-rule.' It was Shivaji's dream to establish a kingdom where people could live freely, with justice and dignity, without oppression from foreign rulers.

Guerrilla Warfare
: A style of fighting that uses speed, surprise and knowledge of the land. Shivaji's soldiers attacked suddenly and disappeared into the hills, confusing large Mughal armies.

Fortresses
: Strong stone structures built on hills or near coasts to protect people and store food, weapons and money. Examples include Raigad, Pratapgad and Sindhudurg.

Chauth and Sardeshmukhi
: Special taxes introduced by the Marathas. Chauth was a 25% protection tax collected from regions outside their rule and Sardeshmukhi was an additional 10% claimed as a hereditary right.

Ashta Pradhana Mandal
: A council of eight ministers created by Shivaji to help in administration. Each minister handled specific duties like finance, justice or military affairs.

Peshwa
: The prime minister of the Maratha Empire. Over time, Peshwas became powerful leaders, especially under Bajirao I, who expanded Maratha power across India.

Coronation
: A grand ceremony where a ruler is formally crowned. Shivaji's coronation at Raigad in 1674 declared him the independent king of the Marathas.

Dakshina Digvijaya
: The southern campaign led by Shivaji to expand Maratha power into southern India. It increased the kingdom's wealth and influence.

  • The Marathas, a proud community of warriors, farmers and administrators from the Deccan plateau, rose to power through unity, courage and devotion to the ideal of Swarajya or self-rule, which became the foundation of their empire.
  • The Bhakti movement in Maharashtra, led by saints like Dnyaneshwar, Namdev, Tukaram and Ramdas, united the people through simple Marathi hymns and messages of faith, equality and self-respect, inspiring future political unity.
  • Shivaji Maharaj, born in 1630, was inspired by his mother Jijabai's teachings and trained in warfare and leadership; his vision of self-rule arose from witnessing the oppression of the people under foreign powers.
  • Beginning with the capture of Torna Fort at just sixteen, Shivaji expanded his control through strategy and courage, turning forts into centres of administration, defence and people's safety.
  • Recognising the importance of sea power, Shivaji built a strong navy and sea forts like Sindhudurg and Vijaydurg, protecting the Konkan coast from pirates and foreign invasions, thus pioneering naval defence in India.
  • His brilliant use of guerrilla warfare—swift, surprise attacks using the Sahyadri hills—allowed smaller Maratha forces to defeat much larger Mughal and Bijapur armies, a strategy that became legendary in Indian history.
  • Major encounters like the defeat of Afzal Khan, the daring raid on Shaista Khan's camp and the sacking of Surat showcased Shivaji's courage, intelligence and commitment to justice and self-rule.
  • The Treaty of Purandar and the Agra escape revealed Shivaji's diplomatic skill and determination; even when imprisoned by Aurangzeb, his clever escape restored Maratha pride and strengthened belief in Swarajya.
  • Shivaji's coronation at Raigad in 1674 marked his formal recognition as Chhatrapati, establishing Hindu kingship based on dharma and cultural pride and giving legitimacy to the Maratha state.
  • His southern campaign (Dakshina Digvijaya) extended Maratha influence into Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, making the Marathas a dominant force across peninsular India.
  • After Shivaji's death in 1680, successors like Sambhaji, Rajaram and Tarabai continued the struggle against Aurangzeb, proving that Shivaji's vision of freedom was stronger than any single ruler's life.
  • The Peshwas later expanded Maratha power under the leaders like Bajirao I and Nana Saheb, turning the kingdom into an empire that stretched from the Deccan to Delhi and became India's most powerful native force.
  • Despite their defeat in the Third Battle of Panipat (1761), the Marathas revived under Madhavrao I and generals like Mahadji Shinde, regaining Delhi and maintaining influence until the British conquest after the Anglo-Maratha Wars.
  • The Maratha legacy—strong administration, cultural revival, respect for justice, religious tolerance, women's leadership and the spirit of Swarajya—continues to inspire India's ideals of unity, freedom and self-governance.
