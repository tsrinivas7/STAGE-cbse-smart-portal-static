Chapter Summary

Chapter 1: AI Project Lifecycle

Artificial Intelligence (AI) — A type of technology that gives machines and computers the ability to perform work that normally requires human intelligence.

  • AI systems are designed to mimic human cognitive processes — they can analyse data, recognize patterns, learn from experience, and make predictions or decisions.
  • AI systems learn, adapt, and improve their performance over time by using data and learning from past experiences.
  • In simple words, AI is human-made intelligence that allows machines to behave intelligently.
  • AI can manage huge amounts of data quickly and efficiently, much like humans read and understand information to gain knowledge.
  • The more data AI learns from, the better its performance becomes — similar to how humans improve with practice.

AI at Work: It's All Around Us!

  • In Healthcare: AI helps doctors diagnose diseases faster and more accurately by analysing medical scans and reports.
  • At Home: AI powers smart home devices like speakers that play music on command and thermostats that learn preferred temperatures.
  • Getting Around: AI is the main technology behind self-driving cars and helps navigation apps find the quickest routes.
  • On Your Smartphone: AI is used in voice assistants and photo apps that automatically group pictures of the same person.
  • Personalised Apps: AI suggests new videos, items, and products based on your past activity.

How does AI learn?

Data — The raw information that AI and Machine Learning systems analyse and learn from to recognise patterns and make decisions.

  • The real magic behind AI lies in data — AI and Machine Learning systems learn by analysing huge datasets and recognising patterns.
  • Voice assistants, language translators, and recommendation systems seem intelligent because they have access to massive data and keep training themselves.
  • Every AI-driven program works correctly only when supported by accurate and reliable data.
  • Without good quality data, AI systems cannot function effectively.

What is an AI Project?

AI Project — A step-by-step process of developing a system or model using artificial intelligence technology to solve a real-world problem by learning from past data.

  • Creating an AI application is not magic — it is a defined, structured process.
  • An AI project has a specific goal, such as building a system that recommends a new product to a user or creating a filter that detects spam emails.
  • The developed AI-based system is first trained on previous data, then identifies patterns, and makes predictions or decisions.
  • The entire process of building, testing, and improving the system step by step is called the AI project lifecycle.
  • Similar to science fair projects, AI projects also have clear, specific goals.

AI Project Lifecycle

AI Project Lifecycle — An iterative, six-stage framework for developing, deploying, and maintaining AI tools, starting from defining the problem to continuous monitoring and maintenance.

  • The lifecycle is an iterative process, meaning stages may be revisited and repeated as needed.
  • It consists of six stages: Define the Problem → Data Collection & Preparation → Model Development & Training → Model Evaluation & Refinement → Model Deployment → Monitoring & Maintenance.
  • Each stage builds upon the previous one to create a complete, working AI system.
  • The lifecycle ensures that AI tools are developed systematically, deployed properly, and maintained over time.

Key Phases of AI Project Lifecycle

  • Stage 1 — Define the Problem: Identify goals and determine whether AI is truly needed.
  • Stage 2 — Data Collection & Preparation: Collect and clean data needed for the AI model.
  • Stage 3 — Model Development & Training: Build and train the AI model using the prepared data.
  • Stage 4 — Model Evaluation & Refinement: Test and assess the model's performance, then refine if needed.
  • Stage 5 — Model Deployment: Deploy the trained model in the real world for actual use.
  • Stage 6 — Monitoring & Maintenance: Track the model's performance and keep it updated over time.

1. Define the Problem

  • The first and most crucial step in the AI project lifecycle is to clearly define or identify the problem or goal.
  • At this stage, we must determine how the system should work and whether an AI system is really required for the project.
  • It may be possible to complete the project without AI using simple automation — not every problem needs AI.
  • Identifying the problem or goal that an AI system really needs is an essential task during the development of an AI project.
  • A clear, well-defined problem guides all subsequent stages of the project lifecycle.

Let us take an example of an Automatic Car Wash System

Automation — A system that follows fixed, pre-set rules without learning, adapting, or making decisions based on data.

AI-based system — A system that uses learning, adaptation, and decision-making to replace fixed rules, adjusting its actions based on real-time data.

  • In an automatic (non-AI) car wash, the system follows fixed steps: detect vehicle, spray water for a fixed duration, apply soap for a fixed time, rotate brushes at programmed speed, rinse, dry, and stop.
  • There is no learning, adaptation, or decision-making beyond programmed logic — the machine does the same thing every time regardless of car size or dirt level.
  • This is an example of automation, not AI, because the system has fixed set rules and does not learn from data.
  • In an AI-based car wash, cameras with AI scan the car to detect size, dirt level, and type of dirt, and the system decides water spray time, soap usage, and brush speed based on the dirt instead of fixed timing.
  • The key difference: AI-based systems adapt and make decisions, while automation follows fixed rules without thinking or improving.

Some Other Examples

  • Traffic Signal Control — Time-based (Automation): Traffic lights change every 30 seconds, no matter how many cars are waiting.
  • Traffic Signal Control — AI-based: AI models adjust green light timing based on real-time traffic density.
  • Irrigation System — Time-based (Automation): A timer waters the plants every day at 7 AM, regardless of weather conditions.
  • Irrigation System — AI-based: AI models water plants based on soil moisture and weather conditions using predictive analytics.

2. Data Collection and Preparation

Data Collection — The process of gathering relevant data from various sources based on the problem being solved.

  • After defining the problem, data collection and preparation are the next important phase of the AI project lifecycle.
  • AI systems/models mainly learn from past data, so the quality of that data is a crucial factor affecting their performance.
  • Garbage In, Garbage Out — A well-known saying in AI meaning that if the input data is bad, the AI's output will also be bad.
  • Just like a cake needs the right ingredients and a correct recipe, an AI system needs accurate and reliable data to function well.
  • Example: To predict whether a student will score above 75%, data like attendance percentage, study hours, previous test marks, and class participation are collected from previous students.

Data Collection

Sensor — A device that measures physical changes in the environment and converts them into a measurable electrical signal.

Surveys — Data collected by asking questions and collecting responses from people.

Websites — Data collected from online sources such as articles, social media platforms, and websites.

Historical Records — Past data stored in files, reports, or records.

  • Data can be collected from various sources depending on the problem, including sensors, surveys, websites, and historical records.
  • A temperature sensor measures heat, and a light sensor detects light intensity — both convert physical changes into electrical signals.
  • Surveys gather direct responses from people to collect specific information.
  • Websites and social media platforms provide large amounts of online data.
  • Historical records contain valuable past data stored in files, reports, or other documents.

Data Preparation

Missing Values — Data points that are not recorded, empty, or unavailable in a dataset.

Duplicate Entries — Data points that are repeated more than once in a dataset.

Incorrect Information — Data points that are incorrect and inaccurate.

Irrelevant Data — Data points that are not related or useful to the given problem.

Data Cleaning — The process of removing errors or irrelevant data, filling missing values, and removing duplicate data.

Data Formatting — The process of converting text into numbers (if required) and arranging data neatly in a spreadsheet.

Data Labelling — The process of assigning correct categories to data (e.g., Spam/Not Spam, Pass/Fail).

  • Developing an AI system requires a large amount of data, but collected data is usually raw and unorganised.
  • Raw data may contain missing values, duplicate entries, incorrect information, and irrelevant data — all of which must be addressed.
  • Data cleaning removes errors, fills missing values, and eliminates duplicates.
  • Data formatting converts text to numbers when needed and organises data neatly.
  • Data labelling assigns correct categories to data so the AI model can learn to classify it properly.

3. Model Development and Training

Model Development — Creating an AI model that solves the problem defined in the first stage of the project lifecycle.

AI Model — The brain of the system that learns from data and uses that learning to make predictions.

Model Training — The learning process where the AI model studies data, finds relationships, and identifies patterns to make predictions.

  • Model development means creating an AI model that addresses the problem defined in stage 1.
  • An AI model works like the brain of the system — it learns from data and uses that learning to make predictions.
  • Think of the AI model as a student, the data as a textbook, and the developer as a teacher — the model learns by studying examples from the data.
  • During model training, the model finds relationships in the data (e.g., students with attendance above 75% usually score well, or students who study less than 2 hours rarely cross 75%).
  • The model connects the dots between different data points to learn patterns that help it make accurate predictions.

4. Model Evaluation and Refinement

Testing — Checking the model using a test dataset — a set of data that was not used during training — to understand how well the model performs on new, unseen data.

Accuracy — A measure of how many predictions the model makes correctly, shown as a percentage.

Model Refinement — Improving the model when it does not give satisfactory results, by adding more data, removing incorrect data, changing model settings, or retraining the model.

  • After training, the model is tested using new, unseen data to check how well it works.
  • Accuracy shows how many predictions are correct — for example, if the model correctly predicts 7 out of 10 students, its accuracy is 70%.
  • A test dataset is separate from the training data and helps evaluate the model's real-world performance.
  • If the model's accuracy is low (e.g., only 20%), model refinement is required to improve performance.
  • Refinement can be done by adding more data, removing incorrect data, changing model settings, and retraining the model — this process continues until the model performs well.

5. Model Deployment

Model Deployment — The stage where the trained, tested, and refined AI model is put into real-world use, taking input data, making predictions, and helping users in real situations.

  • After the model has been trained, tested, and refined, it is deployed for real-world use.
  • The deployed model takes input data (e.g., images, signals, or voice), makes predictions, and helps users in real situations.
  • Deployment is like graduation day for the AI — it has learned, passed its tests, and is now ready to work.
  • Example: A model predicting student scores can be deployed in a school software system where teachers enter student information and the system predicts results.
  • Real-life examples of deployment include spam detection in email apps, recommendation systems in shopping apps, voice assistants, and face unlock in smartphones.

6. Monitoring and Maintenance

Monitoring — The continuous checking of the performance of the deployed AI model.

Maintenance — Updating the model to keep it accurate and efficient over time.

  • After deployment, monitoring and maintenance are essential to ensure the model continues to work correctly.
  • Monitoring regularly checks: Is the system working correctly? Is the model giving correct predictions? Has the accuracy remained the same or decreased?
  • Over time, a model's accuracy may reduce because real-world conditions change (e.g., study patterns change, exam patterns change, new types of questions are introduced).
  • If monitoring reveals reduced accuracy or errors, the model needs to be updated or improved.
  • The model can be updated by adding new data, retraining the model, fixing errors, and improving performance.

C. Short Answer Questions

1. How does AI learn?

AI learns from data. Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning systems analyse huge datasets and recognise patterns in the data. They learn from past experiences and examples, much like humans learn from practice. The more data an AI system is exposed to, the better it performs. AI keeps improving over time by learning from new data — it identifies relationships, trends, and patterns, and uses this learning to make predictions or decisions.

2. Why is defining the problem important in an AI project?

Defining the problem is the first and most crucial step in the AI project lifecycle. At this stage, we must:
- Clearly identify the problem or goal.
- Determine how the system should work.
- Decide whether AI is really required for the project.

This is important because some problems can be solved with simple automation (fixed rules) without needing AI. For example, an automatic car wash system that follows fixed steps (spray water for a fixed time, apply soap for a fixed time, etc.) does not need AI — it is just automation. AI is only needed when the system requires learning, adaptation, and decision-making based on data. Identifying the correct problem ensures we do not waste time and resources building an AI system where it is not needed.

3. What happens in the testing stage of an AI project?

After the model is trained, it is tested using a test dataset — a set of data that was not used during training. This helps us understand how well the model performs on new, unseen data. The model's performance is measured by accuracy, which shows how many predictions are correct.

For example, if the model is tested on 10 new students and it correctly predicts the results for 7 of them, the accuracy is 70%. If the accuracy is not satisfactory (e.g., only 20%), then model refinement is required — we improve the model by adding more data, removing incorrect data, changing model settings, and training the model again. This process continues until the model performs well.

4. What is meant by accuracy in AI?

Accuracy is a measure of how many predictions made by an AI model are correct. It is calculated as:

Accuracy = (Number of Correct Predictions / Total Number of Predictions) × 100

For example:
- If a model correctly predicts 8 out of 10 results, the accuracy is 80%.
- If a model correctly predicts 7 out of 10 students' results, the accuracy is 70%.
- If a model correctly predicts only 2 out of 10, the accuracy is 20%, and model refinement is needed.

Accuracy helps us evaluate whether the AI model is performing well enough to be deployed in the real world.

5. Give one real-life example of an AI project.

Spam detection in email apps is a real-life example of an AI project. In this project:
- Problem definition: Build a system that automatically identifies spam emails.
- Data collection: Collect examples of spam and non-spam emails.
- Model training: Train the AI model to recognise patterns that distinguish spam from genuine emails (e.g., suspicious links, certain keywords).
- Testing: Test the model on new, unseen emails to check its accuracy.
- Deployment: Deploy the model into email applications so it automatically filters spam for users.
- Monitoring: Continuously monitor and update the model as new types of spam emails appear.

Other real-life examples include: recommendation systems in shopping apps, voice assistants, and face unlock in smartphones.

Section 4: Descriptive Questions

Q1. Explain the six stages of the AI project lifecycle in order, with a brief description of each stage.

Show Answer
✅ **Answer:** The six stages of the AI project lifecycle are:
1. **Define the Problem:** Identify the goal clearly and determine whether AI is really needed for the project or if simple automation would suffice.
2. **Data Collection and Preparation:** Collect relevant data from various sources (sensors, surveys, websites, historical records) and then clean, format, and label the raw data.
3. **Model Development and Training:** Create an AI model that learns from the prepared data by finding patterns and relationships, similar to a student learning from a textbook.
4. **Model Evaluation and Refinement:** Test the trained model using a test dataset (new, unseen data) to measure accuracy. If results are unsatisfactory, refine the model by adding more data, removing incorrect data, or changing settings.
5. **Model Deployment:** Put the trained and tested model into real-world use where it takes input data, makes predictions, and helps users in real situations.
6. **Monitoring and Maintenance:** Continuously check the deployed model's performance and accuracy, and update it by adding new data, retraining, fixing errors, and improving performance over time.

Chapter 1: AI Project Lifecycle

Imagine a world where machines can hear, see, talk, and even take decisions. This may sound like a scene from a science fiction movie. It is not; it is Artificial Intelligence (AI).

AI is a type of technology that gives machines and computers the ability to perform work that normally requires human intelligence. These machines can perform complex tasks done by humans, like analysing data, finding patterns, predicting trends, solving problems, and making decisions by using data and learning from past experiences.

AI systems are designed to learn, adapt, and improve their performance over time. In simple words, AI is human-made intelligence that allows machines to behave intelligently. How is this possible?

Artificial Intelligence systems have been created to mimic human cognitive processes.

They can:

  • Analyse data: Humans read and understand information to get knowledge. Similarly, AI studies large amounts of data to find meaning and make decisions. It can manage huge data quickly and efficiently.
  • Recognize patterns: AI recognises patterns and relationships in data, such as trends, similarities, or repeated behaviours, by learning from various examples.
  • Learn from experience: AI keeps improving over time by learning from new data. The more it learns, the better its performance becomes, much like humans improving with practice.
  • Make predictions or decisions: Using what it has learned, AI can predict outcomes or make decisions, often based on probabilities and past data.

AI at Work: It's All Around Us!

AI is an important part of our everyday lives.

Here are a few examples:

  • In Healthcare: AI helps doctors diagnose diseases faster and more accurately by looking at medical scans and reports.
  • At Home: AI powers smart home devices like speakers that play your favourite music when you tell them to and thermostats that learn what temperature you like.
  • Getting Around: AI is the main technology behind self-driving cars and helps navigation apps find the quickest routes.
  • On Your Smartphone: AI is used when you use a voice assistant on your phone to set a reminder or when your photo app automatically puts pictures of the same person together.
  • Personalised Apps: It suggests new videos, items, and products you might like based on what you have done in the past.

How does AI learn?

The real magic behind Artificial Intelligence lies in data. Artificial intelligence and Machine Learning systems learn from data. These models learn by analysing huge datasets and recognising patterns. For example, Voice assistants, language translators and recommendation systems are so intelligent that they seem to know everything—that is only because they have access to massive data and keep training themselves. Every AI-driven program works correctly when supported by accurate and reliable data.

What is an AI Project?

Creating an AI application isn't magic, but it is a defined process. An AI project is the journey of developing a smart system to solve a real-world problem by learning from data.

Think of it like a science fair project. You have a clear goal: Will a plant grow better with sunlight? For an AI project, the goal is just as specific, for example, to build a system that can recommend a new product to a user or create a filter that automatically detects spam emails.

An AI project is a step-by-step process of developing a system or model using artificial intelligence technology to solve a real-world problem by learning from past data. Similar to science projects, AI projects also have specific goals, such as recommending songs or detecting spam emails. The developed AI-based system is first trained on previous data, then identifies patterns, and makes predictions or decisions. The entire process of building, testing, and improving the system step by step is called the AI project lifecycle.

AI Project lifecycle:

The AI project lifecycle is an iterative, six-stage framework, for developing, deploying, and maintaining AI tools. It starts with defining the problem, data collection and preparation for modelling, development and training of AI models, model evaluation and its refinement, deployment of the model and continuous monitoring and maintenance.

1. Define the problem:

The first and most crucial step in an AI project lifecycle is to define or identify the problem or goal clearly. At this stage, we must determine how the system should work and whether the AI system is really required for this project. It may be possible to complete the project without AI using simple automation.

Let us take an example of an Automatic car wash system

In this problem, the steps are:

  • The car enters the bay
  • Sensor detects Vehicle Position
  • The car moves forward on the conveyor
  • For a fixed duration, water is sprayed
  • Soap is applied for a fixed time
  • Brushes rotate at programmed speed
  • Rinse cycle run
  • Dryer activates
  • The system stops when the car exits

In this example, there is no learning, adaptation, or decision-making beyond the programmed logic. No matter how big or small the car is, the machine always does the same things in the same way. It doesn't think, this car is really dirty; maybe I should scrub it longer. It simply does what it is told to do. So, you don't need AI to solve this problem. The system only needs rules that could be set up ahead of time and automated. These kinds of systems have fixed set rules and do not learn from data. This is an example of automation, not AI.

To develop an AI-based car wash system, learning, adaptation, and decision-making using AI replace fixed rules, where cameras with AI system scan the car to detect its size, dirt level, and type of dirt, and the system then decides water spray time, soap usage, and brush speed based on the dirt instead of fixed timing.

AI-based car wash uses AI to detect car size and dirt level and adapt washing actions. In contrast, in automation there is no learning, adaptation, or decision-making and the system follows fixed rules without thinking or improving.

* Time-based (automation): Traffic lights change every 30 seconds, no matter how many cars are waiting
* AI-based: Traffic lights can also be controlled using AI. Where AI models are designed to adjust green light timing based on traffic density

☐ Irrigation system:
* Time-based (automation): This irrigation system has a timer that waters the plants every day at 7 AM, no matter what the weather is like
* AI-based: The irrigation system can also be developed using AI. Where AI models are designed to water plants based on soil moisture and weather using predictive analytics.

2. Data Collection and Preparation:

After defining the problem that requires AI, Data collection and preparation are the next important phase of an AI project lifecycle. AI systems/models mainly learn from past data, so the quality of that data is a crucial factor affecting their performance.

Let me give you a fun analogy:
Think about how you would teach a friend to bake a cake. The cake will be a disaster if you do not give the right ingredients or if the recipe is full of mistakes. AI is the same. The AI will be bad if the data is bad. In the world of AI, there is a well-known saying: Garbage In, Garbage Out.

Data collection:

Relevant data can be collected from the various sources based on the problem. Some of the sources of data collection are:

  • Sensors: It is a device that measures physical changes in the environment and convert them into a measurable electrical signal. For example, temperature sensor measures the heat, or light sensor can detect the light intensity and convert it into an electrical signal.
  • Surveys: Data is collected by asking questions and collecting responses from people
  • Websites: Data can be collected from online sources such as articles, social media platforms, and websites.
  • Historical records: These include past data stored in files, reports, or records.

Let us take an example:
To predict whether a student will score more than 75% marks in the exam or not. This is something every student secretly worries about, right?

For this problem, we can collect the following data from the previous students:

  • Attendance percentage shows whether regular attendees usually score better
  • Hours studied per day show if studying more helps improve performance
  • Previous test marks show how they performed in earlier tests
  • Participation in class Yes or No shows whether active students are more likely to succeed
  • Final exams result Above 75% or Below 75% is the result we want to predict
Students Attendance Study Hours Test Marks Participation Final Result
Student 1 90% 3 hours 78 Yes Above 75%
Student 2 60% 1 hours 55 No Below 75%
Student 3 80% 2 hours 70 Yes Above 75%

This step is called data collection.

Data Preparation:

For developing an AI system, we need a large amount of data. However, during the data collection, the data we usually collect is raw or unorganised.

It may contain:
* Missing values are the data points that are not recorded, empty or unavailable in a dataset
* Duplicate entries are the data points that are repeated more than once in a dataset
* Incorrect information are data points that are incorrect and inaccurate
* Irrelevant data are data points that are not related or useful to the given problem

The data we collect is often raw and unorganised. It may contain errors, missing values, or unnecessary information. Therefore, we clean and organise the data before using it to train an AI model.

The following steps are commonly used during the data preparation:

Data Cleaning:
* Remove errors or irrelevant data
* Fill missing values
* Remove duplicate data

Data Formatting:
* Convert text into number (if required)
* Arrange data in a spreadsheet neatly

Data Labelling:
* Assign correct categories (e.g., Spam/Not Spam, Pass/Fail)

3. Model Development and Training:

After the data collection and preparation, the next step is AI model development and its training. Model development means creating an AI model that solves the problem defined in stage 1. An AI model works like the brain of the system. It learns from data and uses that learning to make predictions.

Think of it like this:
You are a teacher, and the AI model is your student. The data is your textbook. You teach the student (the model) by showing it examples from the textbook (the data). Over time, the student learns the patterns and can answer questions on its own.

Let us look at the previous example whether a student will score more than 75% or not. Create an AI model to address this issue by using the following data to learn from past students:
* Study hours
* Attendance
* Test marks
* Participation in class

This learning process of a model is known as model training.

The model will learn from the information of student 1, student 2, and student 3 and find the relationship between these students. It might notice that students with attendance above 75% usually score well. Or that students who study less than 2 hours rarely cross 75%. It's like the model is connecting the dots!

4. Model Evaluation and Refinement

After training, the model is tested using new data to check how well it works. Its performance is usually measured by accuracy, which shows how many predictions are correct.

Testing means checking the model using a test dataset. A test dataset is a set of data that was not used during training. It helps us understand how well the model performs on new, unseen data.

Think of it like this:

You taught your friend how to identify birds using a book with 10 pictures. Now you show them 5 new bird pictures they have never seen before. If they correctly identify 4 out of 5, their accuracy is 80%.

In the same previous example of predicting whether a student will score more than 75% marks in the exam or not, the accuracy of these predictions largely depends on the quality of the data used to train the model.

Let us consider from our training, the model learned that a student would get more than 75% if they have:

  • More than 80% attendance
  • Study more than two hours
  • Get more than 75 on tests
  • Participate in class

If not, the student will likely score below 75%. Now, this trained model will work for two new students, student 4 and student 5. Based on what it learned, the model predicts that student 4 will get more than 75%, but student 5 will get less than 75%.

Students Attendance Study Hours Test Marks Participation Final Result
Student 4 82% 2.5 hours 77 Yes Above 75%
Student 5 70% 1.5 hours 62 No Below 75%

The model correctly predicts for 2 out of 2 students, so the accuracy of the developed model is 100%. Great job, model!

Suppose there are 10 students during the model evaluation, and the developed model correctly predicts for 7 of them. Then the model's accuracy is 70%.

There may be a condition: if our model correctly predicts only 2 out of 10 students, then its accuracy is only 20%. In this case, model refinement is required.

Model Refinement: Model refinement means improving the model when it does not give satisfactory results.

We can improve the model by making changes as given below:

  • Adding more data
  • Removing incorrect data
  • Changing model settings
  • Training the model again
    This process continues until the model performs well.

5. Model Deployment:

After the model has been trained, tested, and refined, the next stage is to deploy it in real-world use. It means the model is now ready for use :
* Take the input data (e.g., images, signals, or voice)
* Make prediction
* Help users in the real situation

It is like graduation day for your AI. It has learned, it has passed its tests, and now it's time for it to get a job!

In the previous example, a model is developed to predict whether a student will score more than 75%. This model can be added or deployed to a school software system after evaluation. The teacher enters the student information (like study hours, attendance, etc.) and then the system predicts the result of which students will score more than 75% and who will not.

Real-life examples of deployment:
* Spam detection in email apps
* Recommendation system in shopping apps
* Voice assistant
* Face unlock in smartphones

6. Monitoring and Maintenance

After the model is deployed and in use in the real world, the next key step is Monitoring and Maintenance. Monitoring is the continuous checking of the performance of the deployed AI model whereas Maintenance is to update the model to keep it accurate and efficient over time.
It regularly checks the following essential tasks:
* Is the system working correctly?
* Is the model giving the correct prediction?
* Has the accuracy remained the same or decreased?

In the previous example of score prediction, after some months or years, the model accuracy may have reduced because:
* Study pattern may change
* The exam pattern may change
* New types of questions may be introduced

If the deployed model is monitored regularly, it can help identify when it is not working properly or when its accuracy reduces. In such cases, the model needs to be updated or improved.
The model can be updated by:
* Adding new data
* Retraining the model
* Fixing the errors
* Improving performance
