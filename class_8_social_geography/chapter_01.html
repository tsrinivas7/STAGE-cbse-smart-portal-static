Natural Resources

  • Natural Resources — Elements given by nature that become useful only when people learn how to use them in a useful way.
  • The use of nature as a resource depends on three important factors:
    • Accessibility — Can we reach it? A natural element becomes a resource only when it is available for people to use (e.g., mining technology for coal).
    • Feasibility — Is it possible to use it? It must be practical and affordable to use (e.g., solar panels for sunlight).
    • Cultural Acceptance — Is it useful to society? People must find it desirable and acceptable (e.g., shift from firewood to clean energy).
  • Human knowledge, skills, and technology turn natural things into resources.
  • Earth is the only known planet that supports life due to its balanced availability of natural resources.

Uses & Importance

  • Resources Essential for Life — Resources so important that without them, no living being can survive (e.g., air, water, food, soil).
  • Resources for Materials — Resources used to build houses, make goods, and carry out daily activities (e.g., wood, stone, minerals, cotton, silk).
  • Resources for Energy — Resources used to produce heat, electricity, and fuel for transport (e.g., coal, petroleum, natural gas, hydropower).
  • Energy resources make life faster, easier, and more efficient for modern society.

Categories of Natural Resources

  • Biotic Resources — Living things that can grow, reproduce, and regenerate naturally (e.g., plants, animals, fish, forests, crops).
  • Abiotic Resources — Non-living things derived from the physical environment (e.g., land, water, minerals, metals, coal, petroleum, air).
  • Most abiotic resources are non-renewable, meaning they cannot be replaced quickly once used up.
  • Human knowledge turns natural things into useful resources, such as turning uranium into nuclear energy.

Classification of Natural Resources

On the Basis of Origin

  • Biotic Resources — Living resources that include all forms of life that support human and ecological balance (e.g., plants, animals, micro-organisms).
  • Abiotic Resources — Non-living resources derived from the physical environment like land, air, and minerals (e.g., coal, petroleum, air).

On the Basis of Development

  • Actual Resources — Resources whose quantity is known and which are being used at present (e.g., coal reserves in Jharkhand, water of the Ganga River).
  • Potential Resources — Resources whose actual quantity is not known and which are not being used at present, but may be used in the future (e.g., uranium in Ladakh, wind energy).

On the Basis of Distribution

  • Ubiquitous Resources — Resources found almost everywhere on Earth (e.g., air, sunlight, water vapour).
  • Localised Resources — Resources found only in specific places and not everywhere on Earth (e.g., iron, copper, gold, coal).

On the Basis of Ownership

  • National Resources — Resources that belong to a country and are under the control of its government (e.g., land, forests, rivers, minerals within 12 nautical miles).
  • International Resources — Resources that lie beyond the control of any one country (e.g., deep-sea minerals beyond 200 km from Exclusive Economic Zone).
  • Individual Resources — Resources owned privately by people (e.g., land, houses, wells, vehicles).
  • Community-Owned Resources — Resources used by all members of a community and belong to no single person (e.g., public parks, grazing lands, lakes).
  • Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) — A sea zone prescribed by the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea over which a state has special rights regarding the exploration and use of marine resources (up to 200 km from coastline).

On the Basis of Renewability

  • Renewable Resources — Resources which can be replaced or restored by natural processes within a human lifetime (e.g., sunlight, wind, water, soil, timber).
  • Non-Renewable Resources — Resources that take millions of years to form and cannot be quickly replaced once used up (e.g., coal, petroleum, natural gas, minerals).
  • Some renewable resources, like groundwater and forests, can become non-renewable if overused.

Distribution of Natural Resources and Its Implications

  • Natural resources are not evenly spread across the Earth; some regions are rich while others have few.
  • Uneven distribution influences how people live, where industries grow, and how trade develops.
  • It directly affects settlement patterns, economic opportunities, and political relations.

Impact on Settlements

  • Areas with abundant natural resources often attract more people and grow into centres of settlement or farming.
  • Fertile soil and water availability support agriculture, while forests provide timber.
  • Regions with very few resources, such as deserts or cold mountains, have sparse populations.

Industrial Growth and Employment

  • Areas rich in resources such as coal, petroleum, or iron ore often develop into major industrial centres.
  • Industrialisation — The process of developing industries in a country or region, which increases production and trade.
  • Industries tend to be set up near resource-rich regions to access raw materials and energy.
  • This growth creates jobs but also brings challenges like pollution and pressure on civic facilities.

Displacement of People

  • Large-scale projects like mining, dam construction, and deforestation can displace people from their homes.
  • Displacement forces families, especially tribal and rural communities, to move and lose their livelihoods.
  • It destroys cultural traditions and social bonds, requiring proper compensation and rehabilitation.

Conflicts Over Resources

  • Uneven distribution often leads to disputes over who should control and use shared resources.
  • Shared resources such as rivers, forests, and minerals frequently become a source of conflict between states or countries.
  • Examples include the Kaveri River dispute in India and tensions over oil fields in West Asia.

The Resource Curse

  • Resource Curse — Also known as the 'Paradox of Plenty,' this is when countries rich in natural resources remain poor and underdeveloped.
  • It happens when wealth earned from resources does not benefit the common people but creates economic, political, and social problems.
  • Some countries with few resources, like Japan, achieve progress by investing in technology and human capital instead.

Causes of the Resource Curse

  • Corruption and Misuse of Wealth — Income from resources is controlled by a few powerful people and wasted or stolen instead of used for development.
  • Inequality in Society — Only certain sections of the population enjoy the benefits, creating a wide gap between rich and poor.
  • Overdependence on Resources — Countries rely only on oil, coal, or minerals and do not invest in other sectors like farming or technology.
  • Continuous Conflicts — Natural resources can become a reason for fights between communities or countries, leading to instability.

7Es EXPLAIN AND ELABORATE - 1

Imagine standing in a world untouched by human hands—where rivers flowed freely, forests stretched endlessly and sunlight, air and soil existed in perfect balance. Everything that supports life on Earth comes from nature, yet these gifts do not automatically become useful to us. Only when humans learned to harness them—with creativity, skill and technology—they transform into what we now call natural resources. From using river water for irrigation to generating electricity through turbines, every step of human progress has been shaped by our ability to understand and use nature's treasures wisely.

But with this power comes great responsibility. As our needs have grown, so has our dependence on these limited resources. How we use them decides not only our present comfort, but also the future health of our planet. In this chapter, we will explore what natural resources are, how they are classified and why accessibility, feasibility and cultural acceptance play such important roles in making them useful. We

will also learn how thoughtful use, conservation and sustainable practices can ensure that these resources continue to support life on Earth for generations to come.

NATURAL RESOURCES

Nature gives us everything we need to live and survive—fresh air to breathe, clean water to drink, fertile soil to grow food, forests that provide wood and oxygen, minerals that support industries and sunlight that gives us energy. But everything in nature is not automatically a 'resource.' Something becomes a resource only when people learn how to use it in a useful way. For example, a flowing river was just water until humans learned to build canals for irrigation, construct dams to store water and install turbines to generate electricity. This shows that human knowledge, skills and technology turn natural things into resources.

The use of nature as a resource depends on three important factors:

  • Accessibility – Can we reach it?
    A natural element becomes a resource only when it is available for people to use. For example, coal hidden deep inside the Earth cannot be useful until humans invent technology for mining. Similarly, oil reserves under the ocean floor can only be used when machines and methods are developed to extract them. Thus, accessibility is the first step in turning nature into a resource.

[IMAGE_START] A photograph showing an oil rig structure in the middle of the ocean. The structure has multiple levels and pipes extending into the water. The sky is overcast. [IMAGE_END]
Oil extraction from ocean floor

  • Feasibility – Is it possible to use it?
    Even if something is available, it can only be called a resource when it is practical and affordable to use. For instance, sunlight is present everywhere, but to make it a resource, people had to invent solar panels and develop ways to store energy. This also required money and technical knowledge. In the same way, wind can be turned into energy, but only where windmills and related infrastructure can be installed.

[IMAGE_START] A photograph showing a field with solar panels in the foreground and several large wind turbines (windmills) in the background against a cloudy sky. [IMAGE_END]
Solar panels and windmills

  • Cultural Acceptance – Is it useful to society?
    For any resource to be widely used, people must find it desirable and acceptable. In earlier times, firewood was the main source of energy because it was easy to collect and use. Today, society prefers clean energy like wind and solar power because people are more aware of environmental problems such as deforestation and pollution. Thus, culture, lifestyle and social needs also decide whether something is accepted as a resource.

[IMAGE_START] An illustration showing a transition from fossil fuel to clean energy. On the left, there are solar panels and windmills. In the middle, there is a factory with smokestacks. On the right, there are houses and trees. [IMAGE_END]
Fossil energy to clean energy

From this, we understand that natural resources are not just 'gifts of nature.' They are shaped by human effort, technology and society's needs. As our knowledge grows, we keep discovering new ways of using nature. For example, uranium was once just another mineral, but with nuclear technology, it became a source of powerful energy. Therefore, it is our responsibility to use resources wisely and sustainably so that they continue to support life on Earth for future generations.

Uses & Importance

Categories of Natural Resources

Natural resources are not all used in the same way. Some are necessary for our survival, while others are used for making things or for producing energy. Broadly, we can group them into three categories:

  • Resources Essential for Life Some natural resources are so important that without them, no living being can survive. Air, water and food are the most basic needs for humans, animals and plants. Air provides oxygen that we breathe in and plants use carbon dioxide from the air to make their own food through photosynthesis. Water satisfies our thirst, helps us stay clean and is needed for growing crops. Soil plays an equally important role because it is where crops and plants grow, which provide us with food. For example, clean drinking water keeps us healthy, fresh air prevents diseases and nutritious food gives us energy to live, study and work. Imagine a world without water—crops would dry, people and animals would suffer and life on Earth would collapse. That is why these resources are called essential resources for life. They form the foundation of survival and must be protected carefully to ensure the well-being of all living beings.

These are called material resources because they provide raw materials for construction, industries and household needs. Minerals like iron and copper are used in making machines, bridges and electronic gadgets. Gold and silver are used to make jewellery and also play an important role in trade and economy.

By processing natural materials, humans create goods that support economic growth and improve living standards. Without material resources, human progress and development would stop.

For example, buses and trains run on fuel, fans and lights run on electricity and industries depend on huge amounts of energy to make goods. Even our digital lives—computers, mobile phones and the internet—depend on electricity. Without energy resources, modern life would come to a standstill and development would not be possible. Therefore, energy resources are among the most valuable assets for any country.

Classification of Natural Resources

On the Basis of Origin

Biotic Resources
Living things are biotic resources. They include plants, animals, fish, forests, agricultural crops and even tiny micro-organisms. Since they have the ability to reproduce and grow naturally, they are considered renewable resources. For example, a tree that is cut down can grow again if another is planted and animals reproduce to maintain their population. Forests provide timber, fruits and medicines, while domestic animals like cows and goats give us milk and meat. These resources are directly linked to human survival and economic activities like farming, fishing and animal husbandry.

Abiotic Resources
Non-living things, such as land, water, minerals, metals, coal, petroleum and air are abiotic resources. These are extremely important for industries, agriculture and human settlements. However, most abiotic resources are non-renewable, meaning that once they are used up, they cannot be replaced quickly. For example, minerals like iron, copper and gold take millions of years to form inside the earth, so their total stock cannot be increased by human effort. Excessive exploitation of such resources can cause shortages in the future. Therefore, careful use and conservation of these resources is very important for sustainable development.

On the Basis of Development

Actual Resources
Actual resources are those whose quantity is known and which are being used at present. For example, the coal reserves in Jharkhand are actual resources because we know how much coal is there and we are already using it to produce energy in factories. Similarly, the water of the Ganga River is an actual resource because it is being used for irrigation, drinking, transportation and hydroelectricity. The dark, fertile soils of the Deccan Plateau in Maharashtra and the petroleum deposits of West Asia are also examples of actual resources. These resources are measurable and play a direct role in our lives and economy.

Potential Resources
Potential resources are those whose actual quantity is not known and which are not being used at present, but may be used in the future. For example, the uranium deposits in Ladakh are potential resources because they are not being fully utilised now but may be used later to generate nuclear energy. Similarly, unexplored oil wells near the Western Coast of India are potential resources waiting to be tapped. Wind energy is another example; earlier, it was not widely used in India, but today, states like Tamil Nadu have developed wind power plants and become leading producers of wind energy. This shows how a potential resource can be turned into an actual resource when technology and demand make it possible.

On the Basis of Distribution

Ubiquitous Resources

Ubiquitous resources are those that are found almost everywhere on Earth. For example, the air we breathe, the sunlight we receive and the water vapour in the atmosphere are all present in most parts of the world.

[IMAGE_START] Image of a sunset or sunrise with clouds, labeled "Sunlight". [IMAGE_END]

These resources are so common that people often take them for granted. However, even though they are found everywhere, their quality may differ. For example, while air is available everywhere, in some areas, it is clean and fresh, whereas in polluted cities, it may be harmful to health.

Localised Resources

Localised resources, on the other hand, are found only in specific places and not everywhere on Earth. Minerals like iron, copper, gold and coal are examples. These are unevenly distributed across the world. For example, while India has rich coal deposits in Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh, other countries may have little or none. This uneven distribution often decides where industries develop, where people settle and sometimes even leads to conflicts or trade between nations.

[IMAGE_START] Image of a large open-pit coal mine with heavy machinery, labeled "Coal mine in Jharkhand". [IMAGE_END]

On the Basis of Ownership

National Resources

National resources are those that belong to a country and are under the control of its government. These include land, forests, rivers, minerals and wildlife found within the political boundaries of that country. It also includes resources in the ocean up to 12 nautical miles (19.2 km) from the coastline. The government can use these resources for the development of the nation, such as building dams, conserving forests or mining minerals. Citizens can use them, but always under rules set by the government.

International Resources

International resources are those that lie beyond the control of any one country. For example, the resources in the open ocean beyond 200 km from a country's Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) belong to all of humanity. No single country can claim them as its own. These resources, like deep-sea minerals, oil or marine life, can only be used with the permission and agreement of international organisations such as the United Nations. This rule ensures fairness so that stronger countries do not exploit all the resources for themselves.

Individual Resources

Individual resources are those owned privately by people. In villages, farmers own land, houses, wells or livestock, while in cities, people own flats, plots, shops or vehicles. These resources are used by individuals for their personal benefit and livelihood. Ownership is usually recognised by legal documents like sale deeds or property papers. For example, a farmer's field or a family's house in the city is an individual resource.

Community-Owned Resources

Community-owned resources are those which are used by all members of a community and belong to no single person. These include public parks, playgrounds, lakes, community halls and grazing lands. For example, villagers may use common grazing fields for their cattle and urban residents may use public parks for recreation. These resources are important because they promote sharing, cooperation and collective responsibility in society.

On the Basis of Renewability

Renewable Resources

Renewable resources are those which can be replaced or restored by natural processes within a human lifetime. Unlike resources that take millions of years

to form, renewable resources are continuously available in nature, but they are not unlimited. Their availability depends on how carefully we use them. For example, sunlight and wind are freely available and never run out, while forests and soil can regenerate if used responsibly. However, if these resources are exploited carelessly—such as cutting down too many trees, polluting rivers or overusing farmland—they may become scarce and difficult to use. Thus, renewable resources are called 'renewable' only when human beings manage them wisely through proper conservation practices.

Examples of Renewable Resources

  • Sunlight: An unlimited and clean source of energy that reaches us daily, useful for solar panels and natural light.
  • Wind: Strong winds can generate electricity through windmills, providing a pollution-free energy source.
  • Water from Rivers: Freshwater is needed for drinking, irrigation and also for generating hydropower.
  • Soil: Fertile soil allows crops to grow, supporting farming and food security.
  • Timber (wood): Forest trees provide wood for houses and furniture, but replanting is necessary to maintain supply.
  • Geothermal Energy: Heat from inside the Earth can be used to produce electricity and warm buildings naturally.
  • Biomass: Organic materials like crop waste, animal dung and plant matter can be converted into biofuel for cooking and electricity generation.

Importance of Renewable Resources

  • Support for Survival: Renewable resources like air, water and soil provide the foundation of life by giving oxygen, drinking water and fertile land for crops. Without them, humans, animals and plants cannot survive.
  • Help in Development: These resources are essential for human progress. For example, water supports agriculture, timber supports construction and soil supports farming communities, ensuring economic growth.
  • Eco-friendly Energy: Resources like sunlight and wind provide clean, renewable energy that does not cause pollution, helping fight climate change and reduce dependence on fossil fuels.

Conservation and Challenges of Renewable Resources

  • Risk of Overuse: Even though renewable, these resources can become scarce if exploited carelessly, like deforestation reducing timber and harming wildlife.
  • Pollution Threat: Pollution of rivers, lakes and air can make renewable resources unsafe for use, harming health and reducing availability.
  • Soil Degradation: Overfarming, heavy use of chemicals and erosion reduce soil fertility, making it difficult to grow crops in the future.
  • Conservation Measures Needed: Practices like afforestation, rainwater harvesting organic farming and promoting solar and wind energy are necessary to protect renewable resources for the future.

Non-Renewable Resources

Non-renewable resources are those that take millions of years to form in nature and cannot be quickly replaced once they are used up. These resources are stored deep inside the Earth and are the result of slow geological processes such as the decay of plants and animals over ages or the compression of minerals under heat and pressure. Since they are formed over such a long period, they are present only in limited amounts. Once consumed, they cannot be replenished in our lifetime. This makes them precious and important for development, but also a cause for concern if used without care. Their overuse not only reduces their supply but also causes serious damage to the environment through pollution, mining and greenhouse gas emissions.

Examples of Non-Renewable Resources

  • Coal: A fossil fuel used in thermal power plants and industries.
  • Petroleum (crude oil): Provides petrol, diesel, kerosene, LPG and plastics used in daily life.
  • Natural Gas: Used for cooking in households and as fuel in industries.
  • Minerals and Metals: Iron, copper, aluminium and gold, which are vital for construction, transport, machines and jewellery.
  • Nuclear Energy: Produced from uranium and thorium, it is a powerful non-renewable source used in nuclear power plants to generate electricity efficiently with very little fuel.

Importance of Non-Renewable Resources

  • Foundation of Industries: Coal, petroleum and natural gas are the backbone of industries, transport and electricity generation, making them central to modern development.
  • Essential Fuels for Daily Life: Petrol, diesel and LPG are used in vehicles, homes and industries, supporting the functioning of cities, towns and rural areas.
  • Building and Technology Needs: Metals like iron and copper are used in construction, machines and electronics, while gold and silver are important for trade ornaments and storing wealth.

Conservation and Challenges of Non-Renewable Resources

  • Limited in supply: These resources take millions of years to form, so once exhausted, they cannot be replaced in our lifetime. Their scarcity can lead to energy crises.

  • Rising Costs and Conflicts: Excessive dependence increases demand, causing prices to rise and even conflicts between nations over oil and mineral-rich regions.

  • Environmental Harm: Burning coal and petroleum releases greenhouse gases that cause global warming, while mining activities damage land and forests.
  • Need for Alternatives and Wise Use: Recycling of metals, energy-efficient practices and shifting to renewable sources like solar, wind and biogas are necessary to reduce dependence on non-renewables.

DISTRIBUTION OF NATURAL RESOURCES AND ITS IMPLICATIONS

Natural resources such as water, soil, forests, minerals and energy sources are not evenly spread across the Earth. Some regions are naturally rich in certain resources, while others have very few. For [1] example, the Middle East has vast oil reserves, while countries like Japan have very limited mineral resources. In India, coal and iron ore are concentrated in states like Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh, while fertile alluvial soil is found in the Indo-Gangetic Plains. This uneven [2] distribution influences how people live, where industries grow, how trade develops and sometimes even leads to disputes between regions or countries. The way resources are distributed directly affects settlement patterns, economic opportunities and political relations. [2]

Impact on Settlements

Areas [1] with abundant natural resources often attract more people and grow into centres of settlement, farming or industry. Fertile soil and water availability support agriculture, while forests provide timber and other products for daily life. For example, river valleys have always been densely populated because of the availability of water and fertile land. On the other hand, regions with very few resources—such as deserts or cold mountains—have sparse populations because they cannot support large-scale farming or industries. Thus, the presence or absence of resources directly influences population growth, settlement patterns and levels of economic development. [7]

Industrial Growth and Employment

Areas that are rich in resources such as coal, petroleum, iron ore or natural gas often develop into major industrial centres. Industries require raw materials and energy, so they tend to be set up near resource-rich regions. For instance, Jharkhand, Odisha and Chhattisgarh in India have large reserves of coal and iron ore. These resources support mining, steel production and heavy industries, which in turn provide thousands of jobs to local people. Workers from other regions also migrate to these areas in search of employment, leading to urban growth.

This process of industrialisation not only increases production and trade, but also changes the lifestyle of people. Cities near mines and industries grow rapidly, with better roads, schools and hospitals being developed. However, such growth also brings challenges like air and water pollution, poor living conditions in crowded cities and pressure on civic facilities. Thus, while resource-rich areas help create economic development and employment, they also require careful planning to ensure balanced growth.

Displacement of People

The use of natural resources often demands large-scale projects like mining, dam construction and deforestation, which can displace people from their homes. When forests are cleared for industries or dams are built for irrigation and hydroelectric power, thousands of families—especially tribal and rural communities—are forced to move. For example, the construction of big dams in India, such as the Sardar Sarovar Dam on the Narmada River, led to the relocation of many villages.

Displacement not only causes the loss of houses but also destroys people's livelihoods. Farmers lose fertile land, tribals lose access to forests and entire communities lose their cultural traditions and social bonds. Often, people face difficulties in adjusting to new places, finding work or maintaining their way of life. This makes displacement a serious issue in resource management and governments need to provide proper compensation, rehabilitation and support to affected communities.

Conflicts Over Resources

Resources are not distributed evenly and this often leads to disputes over who should control and use them. Shared resources such as rivers, forests and minerals frequently become a source of conflict between states or even countries. A well-known example in India is the Kaveri River water dispute between Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, where both states demand a larger share of water for drinking and agriculture.

At the global level, many countries compete for oil, natural gas and freshwater reserves. For instance, countries in West Asia have faced tensions over oil fields, while disputes also occur over river waters that flow across national boundaries, such as the Nile in Africa or the Indus in South Asia. Similarly, the Brahmaputra River, is shared by China, India and Bangladesh, making its waters a source of both cooperation and potential conflict.

THE RESOURCE CURSE

It may seem natural to think that countries rich in natural resources should always be wealthy and developed. However, this is not always true. In fact, some nations with huge reserves of oil, coal or minerals remain poor and underdeveloped. This surprising situation is known as the 'Resource Curse' or the 'Paradox of Plenty.' It happens when the wealth earned from natural resources does not benefit the common people, but instead creates economic, political and social problems.
