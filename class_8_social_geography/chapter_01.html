NATURAL RESOURCES

  • Nature provides fresh air, clean water, fertile soil, forests, minerals, and sunlight essential for survival.
  • Something becomes a resource only when people learn how to use it in a useful way (e.g., building dams on rivers).

Categories of Natural Resources

  • Natural resources are grouped into three categories based on their use: Essential for Life, Materials, and Energy.
  • Some resources are necessary for survival, while others are used for making goods or producing energy.
  • Without material resources, human progress and development would stop.
  • Energy resources are among the most valuable assets for any country as they drive modern life.

Resources Essential for Life

  • Resources Essential for Life — Air, water, and food are basic needs for humans, animals, and plants to survive.
  • Air provides oxygen for breathing and carbon dioxide for photosynthesis; water satisfies thirst and aids hygiene.
  • Soil is where crops grow, providing food; clean drinking water prevents diseases.
  • These resources form the foundation of survival and must be protected carefully.

Resources for Materials

  • Resources for Materials — Natural resources used to build houses, make goods, and carry out daily activities.
  • Wood from forests makes furniture and paper; stones like marble construct buildings.
  • Minerals like iron and copper make machines and gadgets; gold and silver are used for jewellery and trade.
  • Clothes come from cotton, silk, and wool, processed from natural materials to support economic growth.

Resources for Energy

  • Resources for Energy — Energy is the driving force of development for machines, vehicles, and industries.
  • Coal, petroleum, and natural gas produce heat, electricity, and fuel for transport.
  • Hydropower from dams lights homes and powers industries.
  • Modern society increasingly uses renewable energy resources like sunlight, wind, and biogas.

DID YOU KNOW?

  • Only 3% of all water on Earth is freshwater, and less than 1% is available for human use.
  • The rest is locked in glaciers and underground ice caps, making water conservation critical.

Classification of Natural Resources

  • Natural resources are classified based on origin, development, distribution, ownership, and renewability.
  • This classification helps in understanding their availability, usage, and management strategies.
  • Different bases of classification highlight different characteristics like living vs. non-living or known vs. unknown quantities.

On the Basis of Origin

  • Resources are divided into Biotic (living) and Abiotic (non-living) based on their origin.
  • Biotic resources can reproduce and grow naturally, while Abiotic resources generally cannot be replaced quickly.

Biotic Resources

  • Biotic Resources — Living things including plants, animals, fish, forests, crops, and micro-organisms.
  • They have the ability to reproduce and grow naturally, considered renewable resources.
  • Forests provide timber and medicines; domestic animals give milk and meat.
  • Linked to human survival and economic activities like farming, fishing, and animal husbandry.

Abiotic Resources

  • Abiotic Resources — Non-living things such as land, water, minerals, metals, coal, petroleum, and air.
  • Extremely important for industries, agriculture, and human settlements.
  • Most are non-renewable; once used up, they cannot be replaced quickly (e.g., minerals take millions of years to form).
  • Careful use and conservation are vital for sustainable development.

On the Basis of Development

  • Resources are categorized as Actual (known and used) or Potential (unknown quantity or not yet used).
  • Technology and demand determine if a potential resource becomes an actual resource.

Actual Resources

  • Actual Resources — Resources whose quantity is known and which are being used at present.
  • Examples include coal reserves in Jharkhand, water of the Ganga River, and dark fertile soils of the Deccan Plateau.
  • These resources are measurable and play a direct role in lives and economy.

Potential Resources

  • Potential Resources — Resources whose actual quantity is not known and which are not being used at present.
  • May be used in the future when technology and demand make it possible (e.g., uranium deposits in Ladakh).
  • Wind energy was potential but became actual in states like Tamil Nadu with developed wind power plants.

DID YOU KNOW?

  • The deepest mine in the world, Mponeng Gold Mine in South Africa, goes nearly 4 kilometres underground.
  • Temperatures there reach 60°C and special cooling systems are used so miners can work safely.

DID YOU KNOW?

  • Deserts are emerging as new energy hubs; Thar Desert in India and Sahara in Africa host massive solar farms.
  • These farms can generate enough power to light up millions of homes.

On the Basis of Distribution

  • Resources are classified as Ubiquitous (found everywhere) or Localised (found only in specific places).
  • Distribution affects settlement patterns, industrial location, and international relations.

Ubiquitous Resources

  • Ubiquitous Resources — Resources found almost everywhere on Earth.
  • Examples include air, sunlight, and water vapour in the atmosphere.
  • Quality may differ (e.g., clean air vs. polluted city air), but availability is widespread.

Localised Resources

  • Localised Resources — Resources found only in specific places and not everywhere on Earth.
  • Examples include minerals like iron, copper, gold, and coal.
  • Uneven distribution decides where industries develop, where people settle, and sometimes leads to conflicts.

On the Basis of Ownership

  • Resources are classified based on who owns or controls them: National, International, Individual, or Community.
  • Ownership determines rules for usage and management rights.

National Resources

  • National Resources — Resources belonging to a country and under government control within political boundaries.
  • Includes land, forests, rivers, minerals, wildlife, and ocean resources up to 12 nautical miles from coastline.
  • Government uses them for national development (dams, mining); citizens use them under government rules.

International Resources

  • International Resources — Resources lying beyond the control of any one country.
  • Found in open ocean beyond 200 km from a country's Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ).
  • Belong to all humanity; used with permission of international organisations like the United Nations.

Individual Resources

  • Individual Resources — Resources owned privately by people.
  • Includes land, houses, wells, livestock in villages; flats, plots, shops, vehicles in cities.
  • Used by individuals for personal benefit; ownership recognised by legal documents like sale deeds.

Community-Owned Resources

  • Community-Owned Resources — Resources used by all members of a community and belonging to no single person.
  • Include public parks, playgrounds, lakes, community halls, and grazing lands.
  • Promote sharing, cooperation, and collective responsibility in society.

On the Basis of Renewability

  • Resources are divided into Renewable (can be restored) and Non-Renewable (cannot be replaced quickly).
  • Management practices determine if renewable resources remain available.

Renewable Resources

  • Renewable Resources — Resources which can be replaced or restored by natural processes within a human lifetime.
  • Continuously available but not unlimited; availability depends on careful usage (e.g., sunlight, wind, forests).
  • Called 'renewable' only when managed wisely through proper conservation practices.
Examples of Renewable Resources
  • Sunlight: Unlimited and clean energy source useful for solar panels and natural light.
  • Wind: Generates electricity through windmills, providing a pollution-free energy source.
  • Water from Rivers: Freshwater needed for drinking, irrigation, and hydropower generation.
  • Soil: Fertile soil allows crops to grow, supporting farming and food security.
  • Timber (wood): Provides wood for houses/furniture; replanting is necessary to maintain supply.
  • Geothermal Energy: Heat from inside Earth used to produce electricity and warm buildings.
  • Biomass: Organic materials like crop waste converted into biofuel for cooking and electricity.
Importance of Renewable Resources
  • Support for Survival: Provide foundation of life (oxygen, water, fertile land) for humans, animals, and plants.
  • Help in Development: Essential for progress (water for agriculture, timber for construction).
  • Eco-friendly Energy: Clean sources like sunlight and wind fight climate change and reduce fossil fuel dependence.
Conservation and Challenges of Renewable Resources
  • Risk of Overuse: Can become scarce if exploited carelessly (e.g., deforestation reduces timber).
  • Pollution Threat: Pollution makes resources unsafe (e.g., dirty rivers harm health).
  • Soil Degradation: Overfarming and chemicals reduce fertility, making crop growth difficult.
  • Conservation Measures Needed: Afforestation, rainwater harvesting, organic farming, and promoting solar/wind energy are necessary.

Non-Renewable Resources

  • Non-Renewable Resources — Resources that take millions of years to form and cannot be quickly replaced once used up.
  • Stored deep inside Earth; result of slow geological processes (decay of plants/animals, compression of minerals).
  • Present in limited amounts; once consumed, cannot be replenished in our lifetime.
  • Overuse reduces supply and causes environmental damage through pollution and greenhouse gas emissions.
Examples of Non-Renewable Resources
  • Coal: Fossil fuel used in thermal power plants and industries.
  • Petroleum (crude oil): Provides petrol, diesel, kerosene, LPG, and plastics.
  • Natural Gas: Used for cooking in households and fuel in industries.
  • Minerals and Metals: Iron, copper, aluminium, gold vital for construction, transport, machines, and jewellery.
  • Nuclear Energy: Produced from uranium/thorium; powerful source for efficient electricity generation.
Importance of Non-Renewable Resources
  • Foundation of Industries: Backbone of industries, transport, and electricity generation.
  • Essential Fuels for Daily Life: Petrol, diesel, LPG support functioning of cities, towns, and rural areas.
  • Building and Technology Needs: Metals used in construction and electronics; gold/silver for trade and wealth storage.
Conservation and Challenges of Non-Renewable Resources
  • Limited in Supply: Once exhausted, cannot be replaced; scarcity can lead to energy crises.
  • Rising Costs and Conflicts: Dependence increases demand, causing price rises and conflicts between nations.
  • Environmental Harm: Burning releases greenhouse gases (global warming); mining damages land and forests.
  • Need for Alternatives: Recycling metals, energy-efficient practices, and shifting to renewables are necessary.

EXTENDED LEARNING

  • Some renewable resources, like groundwater and forests, can become non-renewable if overused.
  • Groundwater used faster than refilled leads to water shortages and land sinking (land subsidence).
  • Sustainable management is required to prevent renewable resources from becoming depleted.

DISTRIBUTION OF NATURAL RESOURCES AND ITS IMPLICATIONS

  • Uneven distribution affects how people live, where industries grow, and trade development.
  • Directly impacts settlement patterns, economic opportunities, and political relations between regions/countries.
  • Leads to disputes between regions or countries over shared resources like rivers or minerals.

Impact on Settlements Areas

  • Areas with abundant resources attract people and grow into centres of settlement, farming, or industry.
  • Fertile soil and water support agriculture; forests provide timber (e.g., river valleys densely populated).
  • Regions with few resources (deserts, cold mountains) have sparse populations due to lack of farming/industry support.
  • Presence or absence of resources directly influences population growth and economic development levels.

Industrial Growth and Employment

  • Resource-rich areas (coal, petroleum, iron ore) develop into major industrial centres (e.g., Jharkhand, Odisha).
  • Industries require raw materials and energy, setting up near resource-rich regions.
  • Mining and heavy industries provide jobs, leading to urban growth and migration.
  • Growth brings challenges like pollution, poor living conditions, and pressure on civic facilities.

Displacement of People

  • Large-scale projects (mining, dams, deforestation) often displace people from their homes.
  • Tribal and rural communities forced to move; loss of houses, livelihoods, and cultural traditions.
  • Example: Sardar Sarovar Dam relocation caused loss of fertile land and forest access.
  • Governments need compensation, rehabilitation, and support for affected communities.

Conflicts Over Resources

  • Uneven distribution leads to disputes over control and use of shared resources (rivers, forests, minerals).
  • Example: Kaveri River dispute between Karnataka and Tamil Nadu over water share.
  • Global level: Countries compete for oil, gas, and freshwater (e.g., Nile, Indus, Brahmaputra).
  • Conflicts create political tensions, reduce cooperation, and can lead to wars.

THE RESOURCE CURSE

  • Resource Curse — Paradox where nations with huge reserves of oil, coal, or minerals remain poor and underdeveloped.
  • Wealth earned does not benefit common people but creates economic, political, and social problems.
  • Contrasts with resource-poor nations like Japan that achieved progress through technology and human capital.

Causes of the Resource Curse

  • Corruption and Misuse of Wealth: Income controlled by few; wasted or stolen instead of used for education/healthcare.
  • Inequality in Society: Benefits enjoyed by certain sections while others remain poor, widening the rich-poor gap.
  • Overdependence on Resources: Relying only on oil/minerals neglects other sectors; economy suffers if prices fall.
  • Continuous Conflicts: Resources become reasons for fights between communities/groups/countries; instability hinders use.

Examples

  • African countries like Nigeria and Democratic Republic of Congo have abundant oil/diamonds but face poverty.
  • Wealth gets mismanaged, lost in corruption, or fuels conflicts instead of improving lives.
  • Lack of education and poor health facilities persist despite resource wealth.

RESPONSIBLE AND WISE USE OF RESOURCES: STEWARDSHIP

  • Stewardship — Acting as responsible caretakers of nature so resources are available for current and future generations.
  • If resources are wasted or overused, they may get exhausted, causing serious problems for human life.
  • Requires balancing economic growth, social welfare, and environmental care.

Restoration and Regeneration

  • Natural resources (forests, rivers, soils) can renew themselves if humans allow recovery time.
  • Continuous exploitation without recovery weakens and destroys resources eventually.
  • Forests: Replace cut trees via reforestation/afforestation to maintain ecosystem balance.
  • Rivers: Keep clean and pollution-free to ensure capacity to provide fresh water for future generations.
  • Soils: Use natural methods (crop rotation, organic manure) to maintain fertility against chemical fertilisers.
  • Balance in Nature: Restoration maintains biodiversity and strong ecosystems for future generations.

The Case of Cement

  • Cement is essential for construction but is a highly polluting industry (dust, soil/water pollution).
  • Central Pollution Control Board guidelines aim to reduce pollution in cement factories.
  • Factories adopt new technologies to minimise dust and waste.
  • Growing interest in alternative eco-friendly materials (mud, stone, recycled plastic) to reduce pollution.

Judicious Use of Resources (Non renewable)

  • Non-renewable resources (coal, petroleum, natural gas) exist in limited amounts formed millions of years ago.
  • Must be used wisely; wasting them causes future energy and raw material shortages.
  • Transport Choices: Use buses, trains, carpools, or bicycles to reduce fuel consumption and pollution.
  • Energy Efficiency: Use energy-saving machines, recycle raw materials, switch off unused appliances, use LEDs.
  • Renewable Alternatives: Promote solar, wind, biofuel, hydropower, geothermal to reduce dependence on fossil fuels.

Overexploitation of Resources

  • Overexploitation — Using resources faster than nature's ability to replace them, causing scarcity and environmental damage.
  • Groundwater Overuse: Continuous pumping lowers water table, drying wells/tube wells, causing water scarcity.
  • Excessive Extraction: Taking minerals/water/forests beyond nature's capacity makes land infertile and disturbs cycles.
  • Ecosystem Damage: Reduces biodiversity (forest destruction, fish decline, habitat vanishing).
  • Unsustainable Practices: Mining, deforestation, overgrazing leave polluted soil and eroded land for future generations.

Case Study #### Groundwater Crisis in Punjab

  • Punjab ('Granary of India') shifted to high-yield crops requiring more water during Green Revolution.
  • Extensive groundwater pumping through tube wells increased production but depleted water tables.
  • Free electricity encouraged over-pumping; chemical fertilizers polluted underground water.
  • Water table fell to alarming levels (>30m deep); 80% area classified as 'over-exploited'.
  • Long-term consequences: irrigation difficulty, dried wells, unsafe drinking water, soil degradation.

ENVIRONMENTAL IMPACT OF RESOURCE USE

  • Unplanned and careless use reduces resource availability and causes serious harm to the environment.
  • Effects felt in air, water, soil, plants, animals, and human health.

Soil Degradation

  • Farmers use chemical fertilisers/pesticides for quick production, damaging soil health long-term.
  • Kills helpful microorganisms and makes soil less fertile.
  • Reduces long-term agricultural productivity and harms the environment.

Air Pollution

  • Burning coal/petroleum releases harmful gases (CO2, SO2, NOx).
  • Causes air pollution, acid rain, breathing problems, and traps heat leading to global warming.
  • Industrial smokestacks emit thick plumes contributing to haze and health hazards.

Water Pollution

  • Rivers/lakes polluted by toxic chemicals from industries or untreated sewage dumping.
  • Polluted water unsafe for drinking, destroys aquatic life, and spreads diseases among humans.
  • Foamy/discolored water indicates contamination affecting ecosystems.

Deforestation

  • Large-scale cutting destroys habitats, leading to loss of biodiversity.
  • Without trees, soil erodes easily, rainfall patterns change, and flood/drought chances increase.
  • Leaves behind stumps and cleared land, disrupting natural cycles.

Climate and Health

  • Pollution and resource misuse cause diseases (asthma, skin problems, waterborne infections).
  • Climate change causes rising sea levels, melting glaciers, and extreme weather events.
  • Proves urgent need for sustainable practices and reduced wasteful resource use.

  • Successful farming even in desert regions offers a model for water-scarce countries.

SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT

  • Sustainable Development — Using natural resources carefully to meet present needs without destroying/exhausting them for future generations.
  • Like borrowing resources from Earth and returning them so children/grandchildren can enjoy them.
  • Balances economic growth, social welfare, and environmental care.

Principles of Sustainable Development

  • Respect and Care for all Forms of Life — Protect plants, animals, and humans instead of destroying them.
  • Improve the Quality of Human Life — Development means better health, education, and happiness, not just wealth.
  • Conserve the Earth’s Vitality and Diversity — Preserve variety of plants, animals, forests, rivers, and ecosystems connected to survival.

The Three Rs

  • Simple way to practice sustainable development in daily life: Reduce, Reuse, Recycle.
  • Reduce: Use fewer resources to prevent waste (e.g., walk/cycle instead of car, switch off lights). Saves money and protects nature.
  • Reuse: Use items again in different ways (e.g., old clothes into rugs, bottles into containers). Reduces waste and encourages creativity.
  • Recycle: Collect/process old materials to make new things (paper, glass, plastic, iron). Reduces need to cut trees or mine minerals.

EXTENDED LEARNING

  • Recycling aluminium saves up to 95% of energy needed to make new aluminium from ore.
  • Many cities run special aluminium can recycling programs to reduce mining pressure.
  • Conserves useful metal and reduces environmental impact of production.

I. Define the following.

  1. Natural resources: Things found in nature that become useful to humans when they apply knowledge, skills, and technology to use them (e.g., air, water, minerals).
  2. Renewable resources: Resources that can be replaced or restored by natural processes within a human lifetime (e.g., sunlight, wind, forests).
  3. Sustainable development: Using natural resources carefully so that we can meet our present needs without destroying or exhausting them for future generations.

II. Differentiate between the following.

    • Biotic Resources: Living things derived from nature (e.g., plants, animals, fish, forests). They are generally renewable.
    • Abiotic Resources: Non-living things derived from the physical environment (e.g., land, water, minerals, metals). Most are non-renewable.
  2. Renewable resources and Non-renewable resources:
    • Renewable Resources: Can regenerate naturally within a human lifetime if used wisely (e.g., sunlight, water).
    • Non-Renewable Resources: Take millions of years to form; cannot be replaced quickly once used (e.g., coal, petroleum, minerals).

Section 5: Application/Concept Based Questions (Page 28)

I. Answer the following questions in brief.

  1. What are the three main factors that decide whether something can be used as a resource? Explain briefly.
    • Answer: The three factors are:
      1. Accessibility: Can we reach it? (e.g., coal deep inside earth needs mining tech).
      2. Feasibility: Is it possible to use it practically and affordably? (e.g., solar panels needed for sunlight).
      3. Cultural Acceptance: Is it useful and desirable to society? (e.g., shift from firewood to clean energy).
  2. What are resources essential for life? Give examples.
    • Answer: These are basic needs for survival. Examples include Air (oxygen), Water (drinking, hygiene), and Food (from soil/plants).
  3. How do material resources support human development?
    • Answer: They provide raw materials for construction (wood, stone), industries (iron, copper), and daily goods (clothes from cotton/silk). They improve living standards and economic growth.
  4. Why are energy resources considered the driving force of development?
    • Answer: Every machine, vehicle, and industry needs energy to run. Electricity powers homes and industries, and fuel runs transport. Without energy, modern life and development would stop.
  5. What do you understand by accessibility, feasibility and cultural acceptance in resource use?
    • Answer: Accessibility means reaching the resource (e.g., mining tech for deep coal). Feasibility means it is practical and affordable to use (e.g., cost of solar panels). Cultural Acceptance means society finds it desirable (e.g., preferring clean energy over polluting fuels).
  6. Explain with examples the uneven distribution of natural resources on Earth.
    • Answer: Resources are not spread equally. For example, the Middle East has vast oil reserves while Japan has few minerals. In India, coal is concentrated in Jharkhand/Chhattisgarh, while fertile soil is in the Indo-Gangetic Plains. This affects settlement and trade.

II. Answer the following questions in detail.

  1. Describe the classification of natural resources on the basis of origin, development, and ownership with examples.
    • Answer:
      • Origin: Biotic (Living: Plants, Animals) and Abiotic (Non-living: Minerals, Land).
      • Development: Actual (Known quantity, in use: Coal in Jharkhand) and Potential (Not fully explored: Uranium in Ladakh).
      • Ownership: National (Govt control: Rivers, Forests), International (Beyond EEZ: Deep-sea minerals), Individual (Private: Land, House), Community-Owned (Public parks, Grazing lands).
  2. Explain the difference between actual and potential resources with suitable examples from India.
    • Answer: Actual Resources are known and currently used (e.g., Ganga River water, Coal in Jharkhand). Potential Resources exist but are not yet fully utilized due to lack of technology or demand (e.g., Uranium deposits in Ladakh, unexplored oil wells near Western Coast, Wind energy in Rajasthan before development).
  3. Discuss the importance and challenges of renewable and non-renewable resources.
    • Answer:
      • Renewable: Important for survival (air, water) and eco-friendly energy (sun, wind). Challenges: Risk of overuse (deforestation), pollution, soil degradation.
      • Non-Renewable: Foundation of industries and daily life (coal, petrol). Challenges: Limited supply, rising costs, conflicts, environmental harm (pollution, global warming).
  4. What is the impact of overexploitation of natural resources on the environment? Illustrate with the case study of Punjab.
    • Answer: Overexploitation leads to soil degradation, water scarcity, and biodiversity loss. In Punjab, excessive pumping of groundwater for the Green Revolution lowered the water table (up to 30 meters deep), dried up wells, and polluted water with chemicals, posing long-term threats to sustainability despite short-term food security gains.
  5. Describe the principles of sustainable development and explain how the three Rs (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle) help achieve it.
    • Answer: Principles include respecting life, improving quality of life, and conserving Earth's vitality. The 3 Rs help by:
      • Reduce: Using fewer resources (e.g., walking instead of driving).
      • Reuse: Using items again (e.g., old clothes as rags).
      • Recycle: Processing old materials into new ones (e.g., paper recycling), saving natural resources and reducing pollution.
  6. Write a detailed note on responsible and wise use of resources with examples from the Sikkim organic farming initiative and the Chipko Movement.
    • Answer: Responsible use involves stewardship and regeneration. Sikkim adopted organic farming (2016), reducing chemical fertilizers, restoring soil fertility, increasing farmer income, and boosting biodiversity. The Chipko Movement in Uttarakhand showed community action to hug trees to prevent deforestation, protecting forests and livelihoods. Both emphasize balancing development with conservation.

Q1. Describe the classification of natural resources on the basis of origin, development, and ownership with examples.

✅ Answer:
Step 1: On the basis of origin - Biotic Resources are living resources that can grow, reproduce and regenerate naturally and are considered renewable, e.g. plants, animals, fish, forests, agricultural crops, micro-organisms; Abiotic Resources are non-living resources from physical environment like land, water, minerals, metals, coal, petroleum, air, most are non-renewable and take millions of years to form.
Step 2: On the basis of development - Actual Resources are those whose quantity is known, measured and being used at present, e.g. coal reserves in Jharkhand, water of Ganga River for irrigation, drinking, transportation and hydroelectricity, dark fertile soils of Deccan Plateau in Maharashtra, petroleum deposits of West Asia; Potential Resources are those whose actual quantity is not known and not being used at present but may be used in future, e.g. uranium deposits in Ladakh for nuclear energy, unexplored oil wells near Western Coast of India, wind energy earlier not widely used but now developed in Tamil Nadu.
Step 3: On the basis of ownership - National Resources belong to a country under government control within political boundaries including ocean up to 12 nautical miles (19.2 km), e.g. land, forests, rivers, minerals, wildlife; International Resources lie beyond control of any one country beyond 200 km Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ), e.g. deep-sea minerals, oil, marine life, used only with permission of United Nations; Individual Resources owned privately by people with legal documents, e.g. farmers' land, houses, wells, livestock, flats, plots, shops, vehicles; Community-Owned Resources used by all members of a community belonging to no single person, e.g. public parks, playgrounds, lakes, community halls, grazing lands.
Final: Natural resources are classified by origin into biotic-renewable and abiotic mostly non-renewable, by development into actual-in-use and potential-for-future, and by ownership into national, international, individual and community-owned.

- Welfare: if soldier died, widow received pension, sometimes son given government job.
- Justice: small disputes by panchayats of village elders; in towns kotwals maintained law and order; serious cases to higher authorities; moderate punishments; right to appeal; protection to farmers, women, citizens during wars.
2. Revenue System - Careful collection and steady income:
- Based on merit, discipline and careful revenue collection by Amatya and Sachiv.

- Strong disciplined army of infantry and cavalry. Shiledars - soldiers who maintained own horses and weapons but fought under state command. Balanced government control with private contribution.
- Weapons: traditional swords, spears, bows with firearms, cannons; even experimented with rockets. Used guerrilla tactics - sudden attack and escape into hills, forests - advantage over larger Mughal armies.
- Forts - lifeline of power: Raigad, Pratapgad, Sinhagad on mountains, rivers, coasts. Strong stone walls, secret passages, storehouses for food, weapons, money, shelters and administrative centres. Ramchandrapant Amatya said without forts kingdom would not survive.
- Navy: Shivaji one of first to build navy to guard Konkan coast, prevent piracy, control sea trade. Sea forts Sindhudurg, Vijaydurg, Suvarnadurg with cannons; small fast ships; under Kanhoji Angre demanded passes from foreign ships, supremacy in Arabian Sea against Portuguese, British, Siddis of Janjira.

Final: Maratha administration was centralised civil rule by Chhatrapati with ashta pradhan mandala, salaried merit officers and fair justice; revenue from land collection, chauth-sardeshmukhi and trade; and military strength from guerrilla army, hill-sea forts and navy.

Steps:
1. Establishment and Role:
- Created in 1950 as independent constitutional body under Article 324. Independent means not under direct control of government, ensures neutral, unbiased elections.
- Guardian, watchdog and referee of Indian democracy. Conducted first general elections 1951-1952. Ensures free, fair, secure elections to uphold government of the people, by the people and for the people with EVMs and VVPATs.
2. Structure:
- National level: Headed by Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) - senior-most authority. Supported by Two Election Commissioners. Together three-member body takes decisions collectively to avoid bias.
- State/UT level: Chief Electoral Officer (CEO) supervises activities - preparing, updating voter lists, coordinating polling. Returning Officer (RO) conducts elections.
- District level: District Election Officer and Electoral Registration Officer (ERO) manage voter list, set up polling stations, train staff, ensure free voting.
- Large Administrative Network: Thousands of polling officers, staff, security personnel work under Commission in urban and remote villages - over 900 million voters, 12 lakh EVMs, 10 lakh polling stations, even Tashigang at 15,256 feet.
3. Functions:
- Declaring election dates: For Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha, State Assemblies, President and Vice-President, from announcement to results.
- Model Code of Conduct (MCC): Enforces rules to avoid hate speech, bribery, misuse of government resources, to maintain fairness.
- Registering political parties: Recognises parties, decides symbols like hand, plough, lantern, ensures democratic principles.
- Supervising polls: Preparing electoral rolls, arranging ballot papers, EVMs, VVPAT slips, checking voter list, ID proof, indelible ink, secret voting and counting.
- Ensuring fairness: Monitors campaigns, election expenses, addresses complaints, ensures no unfair advantage, builds trust with transparency, accessibility measures - postal ballots, home voting, ramps, wheelchairs, braille-enabled IDs, helpline apps.

Final: ECI is a 1950 Article-324 independent body with CEC plus two Commissioners, CEO-RO and DEO-ERO network, which declares elections, enforces MCC, registers parties, supervises voting with EVM-VVPAT and ensures free-fair-inclusive polls.

Q1. Suppose a village depends entirely on groundwater for farming. Using the example of Punjab, explain what advice you would give to prevent a future water crisis.

✅ Answer:
Claim: The village must use groundwater wisely through stewardship, conservation and sustainable farming, otherwise it will face Punjab-like depletion and pollution crisis.
Evidence: In Punjab, the 'Granary of India,' farmers shifted to high-yielding varieties in the 1960s and 1970s which required far more water and began pumping groundwater extensively through tube wells; free electricity encouraged over-pumping while heavy use of chemical fertilizers and pesticides polluted underground water; water table fell to alarming levels, in some areas more than 30 metres deep, almost 80% of state's area is classified as 'over-exploited,' wells dried up, irrigation became difficult and polluted water created health hazards, soil degradation and unsafe drinking water.
Reasoning: Overexploitation happens when humans use resources faster than nature's ability to replace them; groundwater naturally refills through rainfall and underground recharge only if given time and space to heal; continuous pumping lowers water table and excessive chemicals damage soil and water, so restoration needs rainwater harvesting, crop rotation, organic manure and compost, and avoiding chemical-only farming as shown by Sikkim becoming 100% organic in 2016 with compost, neem and garlic repellents and variety of crops.
Final: Regulate pumping, harvest rainwater, adopt organic methods and crop diversity to prevent scarcity.

Q3. Explain the economic impact of British rule on India, including famines, the drain of wealth, and industrial decline.

Classification of Natural Resources

On the Basis of Origin

Biotic Resources

Living things are biotic resources. They include plants, animals, fish, forests, agricultural crops and even tiny micro-organisms. Since they have the ability to reproduce and grow naturally, they are considered renewable resources. For example, a tree that is cut down can grow again if another is planted and animals reproduce to maintain their population. Forests provide timber, fruits and medicines, while domestic animals like cows and goats give us milk and meat. These resources are directly linked to human survival and economic activities like farming, fishing and animal husbandry.

Abiotic Resources

Non-living things, such as land, water, minerals, metals, coal, petroleum and air are abiotic resources. These are extremely important for industries, agriculture and human settlements. However, most abiotic resources are non-renewable, meaning that once they are used up, they cannot be replaced quickly. For example, minerals like iron, copper and gold take millions of years to form inside the earth, so their total stock cannot be increased by human effort. Excessive exploitation of such resources can cause shortages in the future. Therefore, careful use and conservation of these resources is very important for sustainable development.

On the Basis of Development

Actual Resources

Actual resources are those whose quantity is known and which are being used at present. For example, the coal reserves in Jharkhand are actual resources because we know how much coal is there and we are already using it to produce energy in factories. Similarly, the water of the Ganga River is an actual resource because it is being used for irrigation, drinking, transportation and hydroelectricity. The dark, fertile soils of the Deccan Plateau in Maharashtra and the petroleum deposits of West Asia are also examples of actual resources. These resources are measurable and play a direct role in our lives and economy.

Potential Resources

Potential resources are those whose actual quantity is not known and which are not being used at present, but may be used in the future. For example, the uranium deposits in Ladakh are potential resources because they are not being fully utilised now but may be used later to generate nuclear energy. Similarly, unexplored oil wells near the Western Coast of India are potential resources waiting to be tapped. Wind energy is another example; earlier, it was not widely used in India, but today, states like Tamil Nadu have developed wind power plants and become leading producers of wind energy. This shows how a potential resource can be turned into an actual resource when technology and demand make it possible.

Ubiquitous resources are those that are found almost everywhere on Earth. For example, the air we breathe, the sunlight we receive and the water vapour in the atmosphere are all present in most parts of the world.

Localised resources, on the other hand, are found only in specific places and not everywhere on Earth. Minerals like iron, copper, gold and coal are examples. On the Basis of Ownership

National Resources

National resources are those that belong to a country and are under the control of its government. These include land, forests, rivers, minerals and wildlife found within the political boundaries of that country. It also includes resources in the ocean up to 12 nautical miles (19.2 km) from the coastline. The government can use these resources for the development of the nation, such as building dams, conserving forests or mining minerals. Citizens can use them, but always under rules set by the government.

International Resources

International resources are those that lie beyond the control of any one country. For example, the resources in the open ocean beyond 200 km from a country's Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) belong to all of humanity. No single country can claim them as its own. These resources, like deep-sea minerals, oil or marine life, can only be used with the permission and agreement of international organisations such as the United Nations. This rule ensures fairness so that stronger countries do not exploit all the resources for themselves.

Individual Resources

Individual resources are those owned privately by people. In villages, farmers own land, houses, wells or livestock, while in cities, people own flats, plots, shops or vehicles. These resources are used by individuals for their personal benefit and livelihood. Ownership is usually recognised by legal documents like sale deeds or property papers. For example, a farmer's field or a family's house in the city is an individual resource.

Community-Owned Resources

Community-owned resources are those which are used by all members of a community and belong to no single person. These include public parks, playgrounds, lakes, community halls and grazing lands. For example, villagers may use common grazing fields for their cattle and urban residents may use public parks for recreation. These resources are important because they promote sharing, cooperation and collective responsibility in society.

On the Basis of Renewability

Renewable Resources

Renewable resources are those which can be replaced or restored by natural processes within a human lifetime. Unlike resources that take millions of years

to form, renewable resources are continuously available in nature, but they are not unlimited. Their availability depends on how carefully we use them. For example, sunlight and wind are freely available and never run out, while forests and soil can regenerate if used responsibly. However, if these resources are exploited carelessly—such as cutting down too many trees, polluting rivers or overusing farmland—they may become scarce and difficult to use. Thus, renewable resources are called 'renewable' only when human beings manage them wisely through proper conservation practices.

Examples of Renewable Resources

  • Sunlight: An unlimited and clean source of energy that reaches us daily, useful for solar panels and natural light.
  • Wind: Strong winds can generate electricity through windmills, providing a pollution-free energy source.
  • Water from Rivers: Freshwater is needed for drinking, irrigation and also for generating hydropower.
  • Soil: Fertile soil allows crops to grow, supporting farming and food security.
  • Timber (wood): Forest trees provide wood for houses and furniture, but replanting is necessary to maintain supply.
  • Geothermal Energy: Heat from inside the Earth can be used to produce electricity and warm buildings naturally.
  • Biomass: Organic materials like crop waste, animal dung and plant matter can be converted into biofuel for cooking and electricity generation.

Importance of Renewable Resources

  • Support for Survival: Renewable resources like air, water and soil provide the foundation of life by giving oxygen, drinking water and fertile land for crops. Without them, humans, animals and plants cannot survive.
  • Help in Development: These resources are essential for human progress. For example, water supports agriculture, timber supports construction and soil supports farming communities, ensuring economic growth.
  • Eco-friendly Energy: Resources like sunlight and wind provide clean, renewable energy that does not cause pollution, helping fight climate change and reduce dependence on fossil fuels.

Conservation and Challenges of Renewable Resources

  • Risk of Overuse: Even though renewable, these resources can become scarce if exploited carelessly, like deforestation reducing timber and harming wildlife.
  • Pollution Threat: Pollution of rivers, lakes and air can make renewable resources unsafe for use, harming health and reducing availability.
  • Soil Degradation: Overfarming, heavy use of chemicals and erosion reduce soil fertility, making it difficult to grow crops in the future.
  • Conservation Measures Needed: Practices like afforestation, rainwater harvesting, organic farming and promoting solar and wind energy are necessary to protect renewable resources for the future.

Non-Renewable Resources

Non-renewable resources are those that take millions of years to form in nature and cannot be quickly replaced once they are used up. These resources are stored deep inside the Earth and are the result of slow geological processes such as the decay of plants and animals over ages or the compression of minerals under heat and pressure. Since they are formed over such a long period, they are present only in limited amounts. Once consumed, they cannot be replenished in our lifetime. This makes them precious and important for development, but also a cause for concern if used without care. Their overuse not only reduces their supply but also causes serious damage to the environment through pollution, mining and greenhouse gas emissions.

Examples of Non-Renewable Resources

  • Coal: A fossil fuel used in thermal power plants and industries.
  • Petroleum (crude oil): Provides petrol, diesel, kerosene, LPG and plastics used in daily life.
  • Natural Gas: Used for cooking in households and as fuel in industries.
  • Minerals and Metals: Iron, copper, aluminium and gold, which are vital for construction, transport, machines and jewellery.
  • Nuclear Energy: Produced from uranium and thorium, it is a powerful non-renewable source used in nuclear power plants to generate electricity efficiently with very little fuel.

Importance of Non-Renewable Resources

  • Foundation of Industries: Coal, petroleum and natural gas are the backbone of industries, transport and electricity generation, making them central to modern development.

Causes of the Resource Curse

There are many reasons why resource-rich countries may still struggle with poverty:

  • Corruption and Misuse of Wealth: The income from resources is sometimes controlled by a few powerful people or groups and instead of using it for education, healthcare and infrastructure, it gets wasted or stolen.
  • Inequality in Society: When only certain sections of the population enjoy the benefits of resource wealth, while others remain poor, it creates a wide gap between the rich and the poor.
  • Overdependence on Resources: Many countries rely only on oil, coal or minerals and do not invest in other sectors like farming, industries or technology. If resource prices fall in the global market, their entire economy suffers.
  • Continuous Conflicts: Natural resources can become a reason for fights between communities, groups or even countries. Wars and political instability make it difficult to use resources properly.

Examples

A clear example can be seen in some African countries such as Nigeria and the Democratic Republic of Congo. These countries have abundant reserves of oil, diamonds and minerals. Yet, many people there still face poverty, poor health facilities and lack of education. The wealth from resources often gets mismanaged, lost in corruption or fuels conflicts instead of improving people's lives.

RESPONSIBLE AND WISE USE OF RESOURCES: STEWARDSHIP

The Earth's resources are limited and precious, so they must be used with great care. This careful management of resources is called stewardship. It means acting as responsible caretakers of nature so that resources are available not only for us but also for future generations. If resources are wasted or overused, they may get exhausted and human life will face serious problems. Therefore, wise planning and sustainable practices are necessary to ensure that development continues without harming the environment.

Restoration and Regeneration

Natural resources such as forests, rivers and soils have the natural ability to renew and restore themselves, but this process works only if humans allow nature enough time and space to heal. If resources are continuously exploited without giving them a chance to recover, they become weak and eventually get destroyed.

  • Forests: When trees are cut down for timber, fuel or farming, it is important to replace them with new trees through reforestation and afforestation. Healthy forests provide oxygen, absorb carbon dioxide and are home to countless animals and plants. Without regeneration, the entire forest ecosystem can collapse.

The Case of Cement

Cement has become an essential part of modern life. Our houses, schools, hospitals, bridges, roads and airports are all built using cement. Without it, construction and development would be very difficult. However, cement production is also considered one of the most polluting industries. Case Study

Groundwater Crisis in Punjab

Punjab, known as the 'Granary of India,' played a key role in the Green Revolution by producing large amounts of wheat and rice to ensure food security for the country. However, this success came at a cost. Farmers shifted to high-yielding varieties of crops in the 1960s and 1970s, which required far more water than traditional varieties. To meet this demand, they began pumping groundwater extensively through tube wells.

In the short term, this practice increased crop production, but in the long term, it caused severe depletion of groundwater. Free electricity for agriculture encouraged over-pumping, while heavy use of chemical fertilizers and pesticides polluted underground water. As a result, the water table in Punjab has fallen to alarming levels, in some areas more than 30 metres deep. Today, almost 80% of the state's area is classified as 'over-exploited,' meaning water is being withdrawn much faster than it can be naturally replenished.

The combined effect is that irrigation has become difficult, wells have dried up and polluted water has created health hazards. While Punjab ensured food security for India in the short run, the long-term consequences—falling water levels, soil degradation and unsafe drinking water—pose a serious threat to sustainability. This shows how overexploitation of a single resource can damage the environment and affect millions of lives.

SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT

Sustainable development means using natural resources carefully so that we can meet our present needs without destroying or exhausting them for future generations. It is like borrowing resources from the Earth and returning them in such a way that our children and grandchildren can also enjoy them. To achieve this, people must balance economic growth, social welfare and environmental care.

Some important principles of sustainable development are:
* Respect and Care for all Forms of Life – Every plant, animal and human being has a role to play in nature. We must protect them instead of destroying them.
* Improve the Quality of Human Life – Development should not just mean more wealth but also better health, education and happiness for people. (handwritten note: value)
* Conserve the Earth’s Vitality and Diversity – The variety of plants, animals, forests, rivers and ecosystems must be preserved, as they are all connected to human survival.

The Three Rs

One simple way to practice sustainable development in daily life is to follow the Three Rs: Reduce, Reuse and Recycle.

  • Reduce: We should try to use fewer resources wherever possible to prevent waste. For example, if your school is nearby, walking or cycling instead of taking a car saves fuel and reduces pollution. Switching off lights, fans and TVs when not in use saves electricity. Closing taps tightly helps save water. By reducing wastage, we also save money and protect nature.
  • Reuse: Instead of throwing things away, we can use them again in different ways. Old clothes can be stitched into rugs, mops or doormats. Used bottles can be refilled or turned into containers. Old newspapers and jute sacks can be made into shopping bags. Reusing not only reduces waste but also helps us to be creative and resourceful.
  • Recycle: Recycling means collecting and processing old materials to make new things. Items like paper, glass, tin, plastic and iron can be recycled many times. For example, old newspapers sold to scrap dealers are sent to recycling factories where they are turned into new paper products. Recycling reduces the need to cut more trees or mine new minerals, thus saving natural resources and reducing pollution.
