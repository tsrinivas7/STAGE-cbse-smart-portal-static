Using Earth's Resources Wisely

Everything around us becomes a resource only when humans apply knowledge and technology. Resources vary by use, origin, availability, ownership, and renewability—ranging from biotic forests to abiotic minerals, renewable sunlight to non-renewable coal. True progress lies in stewardship—restoring nature through reforestation, organic farming, and cleaner industries. Sustainable development through the 3Rs—Reduce, Reuse, and Recycle—ensures balanced growth, protecting both people and the planet.

NATURAL RESOURCES

Nature gives us everything we need to live and survive—fresh air, clean water, fertile soil, forests, minerals, and sunlight. But everything in nature is not automatically a 'resource.' Something becomes a resource only when people learn how to use it in a useful way. Human knowledge, skills, and technology turn natural things into resources. The use of nature as a resource depends on three important factors:

Accessibility

Accessibility — Can we reach it? A natural element becomes a resource only when it is available for people to use. For example, coal hidden deep inside the Earth cannot be useful until humans invent technology for mining. Similarly, oil reserves under the ocean floor can only be used when machines and methods are developed to extract them.

Feasibility

Feasibility — Is it possible to use it? Even if something is available, it can only be called a resource when it is practical and affordable to use. For instance, sunlight is present everywhere, but to make it a resource, people had to invent solar panels and develop ways to store energy. This also required money and technical knowledge.

Cultural Acceptance

Cultural Acceptance — Is it useful to society? For any resource to be widely used, people must find it desirable and acceptable. In earlier times, firewood was the main source of energy because it was easy to collect and use. Today, society prefers clean energy like wind and solar power because people are more aware of environmental problems such as deforestation and pollution. Thus, culture, lifestyle, and social needs also decide whether something is accepted as a resource.

EXTENDED LEARNING

Earth is the only known planet that supports life because of its balanced availability of natural resources. Earth's stable climate, oxygen-rich atmosphere, and water cycle make life possible.

DID YOU KNOW?

When early humans learned to control fire nearly 1.5 million years ago, it became one of the first examples of a natural element turning into a resource. This discovery changed diet, safety, and even social life forever.

Categories of Natural Resources

Natural resources are grouped into three categories based on their use:

Resources Essential for Life

Some natural resources are so important that without them, no living being can survive. Air, water, and food are the most basic needs for humans, animals, and plants. Air provides oxygen that we breathe in and plants use carbon dioxide from the air to make their own food through photosynthesis. Water satisfies our thirst, helps us stay clean, and is needed for growing crops. Soil plays an equally important role because it is where crops and plants grow, which provide us with food. Clean drinking water keeps us healthy, fresh air prevents diseases, and nutritious food gives us energy to live, study, and work.

Resources for Materials

Beyond basic survival, we need other natural resources to build houses, make goods, and carry out daily activities. These are called material resources because they provide raw materials for construction, industries, and household needs. Wood from forests is used to make furniture, paper, and pencils. Stones like marble and granite are used to construct buildings and monuments. Minerals like iron and copper are used in making machines, bridges, and electronic gadgets. Gold and silver are used to make jewellery and play an important role in trade and economy. Clothes come from cotton plants, silk from silkworms, and wool from sheep.

Resources for Energy

Energy is the driving force of development. Every machine, vehicle, and industry needs energy to run. Natural resources like coal, petroleum, and natural gas are used to produce heat, electricity, and fuel for transport. Electricity generated from water in dams (hydropower) lights up our homes and powers industries. Modern society also makes increasing use of renewable energy resources like sunlight, wind, and biogas, which are clean and never run out.

Classification of Natural Resources

On the Basis of Origin

Biotic Resources

Biotic Resources — Living things are biotic resources. They include plants, animals, fish, forests, agricultural crops, and even tiny micro-organisms. Since they have the ability to reproduce and grow naturally, they are considered renewable resources. Forests provide timber, fruits, and medicines, while domestic animals like cows and goats give us milk and meat.

Abiotic Resources

Abiotic Resources — Non-living things, such as land, water, minerals, metals, coal, petroleum, and air are abiotic resources. These are extremely important for industries, agriculture, and human settlements. However, most abiotic resources are non-renewable, meaning that once they are used up, they cannot be replaced quickly. Excessive exploitation of such resources can cause shortages in the future.

On the Basis of Development

Actual Resources

Actual Resources — Actual resources are those whose quantity is known and which are being used at present. For example, the coal reserves in Jharkhand are actual resources because we know how much coal is there and we are already using it to produce energy in factories. Similarly, the water of the Ganga River is an actual resource because it is being used for irrigation, drinking, transportation, and hydroelectricity.

Potential Resources

Potential Resources — Potential resources are those whose actual quantity is not known and which are not being used at present, but may be used in the future. For example, the uranium deposits in Ladakh are potential resources because they are not being fully utilised now but may be used later to generate nuclear energy. Wind energy is another example; earlier, it was not widely used in India, but today, states like Tamil Nadu have developed wind power plants.

On the Basis of Distribution

Ubiquitous Resources

Ubiquitous Resources — Ubiquitous resources are those that are found almost everywhere on Earth. For example, the air we breathe, the sunlight we receive, and the water vapour in the atmosphere are all present in most parts of the world. These resources are so common that people often take them for granted.

Localised Resources

Localised Resources — Localised resources, on the other hand, are found only in specific places and not everywhere on Earth. Minerals like iron, copper, gold, and coal are examples. These are unevenly distributed across the world. This uneven distribution often decides where industries develop, where people settle, and sometimes even leads to conflicts or trade between nations.

On the Basis of Ownership

National Resources

National Resources — National resources are those that belong to a country and are under the control of its government. These include land, forests, rivers, minerals, and wildlife found within the political boundaries of that country. It also includes resources in the ocean up to 12 nautical miles (19.2 km) from the coastline.

International Resources

International Resources — International resources are those that lie beyond the control of any one country. For example, the resources in the open ocean beyond 200 km from a country's Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) belong to all of humanity. No single country can claim them as its own. These resources can only be used with the permission and agreement of international organisations such as the United Nations.

Individual Resources

Individual Resources — Individual resources are those owned privately by people. In villages, farmers own land, houses, wells, or livestock, while in cities, people own flats, plots, shops, or vehicles. These resources are used by individuals for their personal benefit and livelihood. Ownership is usually recognised by legal documents like sale deeds or property papers.

Community-Owned Resources

Community-Owned Resources — Community-owned resources are those which are used by all members of a community and belong to no single person. These include public parks, playgrounds, lakes, community halls, and grazing lands. These resources are important because they promote sharing, cooperation, and collective responsibility in society.

On the Basis of Renewability

Renewable Resources

Renewable Resources — Renewable resources are those which can be replaced or restored by natural processes within a human lifetime. Unlike resources that take millions of years to form, renewable resources are continuously available in nature, but they are not unlimited. Their availability depends on how carefully we use them. For example, sunlight and wind are freely available and never run out, while forests and soil can regenerate if used responsibly.

Examples of Renewable Resources
  • Sunlight: An unlimited and clean source of energy that reaches us daily, useful for solar panels and natural light.
  • Wind: Strong winds can generate electricity through windmills, providing a pollution-free energy source.
  • Water from Rivers: Freshwater is needed for drinking, irrigation, and also for generating hydropower.
  • Soil: Fertile soil allows crops to grow, supporting farming and food security.
  • Timber (wood): Forest trees provide wood for houses and furniture, but replanting is necessary to maintain supply.
  • Geothermal Energy: Heat from inside the Earth can be used to produce electricity and warm buildings naturally.
  • Biomass: Organic materials like crop waste, animal dung, and plant matter can be converted into biofuel for cooking and electricity generation.
Importance of Renewable Resources
  • Support for Survival: Renewable resources like air, water, and soil provide the foundation of life by giving oxygen, drinking water, and fertile land for crops.
  • Help in Development: These resources are essential for human progress. For example, water supports agriculture, timber supports construction, and soil supports farming communities.
  • Eco-friendly Energy: Resources like sunlight and wind provide clean, renewable energy that does not cause pollution, helping fight climate change.
Conservation and Challenges of Renewable Resources
  • Risk of Overuse: Even though renewable, these resources can become scarce if exploited carelessly, like deforestation reducing timber and harming wildlife.
  • Pollution Threat: Pollution of rivers, lakes, and air can make renewable resources unsafe for use, harming health and reducing availability.
  • Soil Degradation: Overfarming, heavy use of chemicals, and erosion reduce soil fertility, making it difficult to grow crops in the future.
  • Conservation Measures Needed: Practices like afforestation, rainwater harvesting, organic farming, and promoting solar and wind energy are necessary to protect renewable resources for the future.

Non-Renewable Resources

Non-Renewable Resources — Non-renewable resources are those that take millions of years to form in nature and cannot be quickly replaced once they are used up. These resources are stored deep inside the Earth and are the result of slow geological processes. Since they are formed over such a long period, they are present only in limited amounts. Once consumed, they cannot be replenished in our lifetime.

Examples of Non-Renewable Resources
  • Coal: A fossil fuel used in thermal power plants and industries.
  • Petroleum (crude oil): Provides petrol, diesel, kerosene, LPG, and plastics used in daily life.
  • Natural Gas: Used for cooking in households and as fuel in industries.
  • Minerals and Metals: Iron, copper, aluminium, and gold, which are vital for construction, transport, machines, and jewellery.
  • Nuclear Energy: Produced from uranium and thorium, it is a powerful non-renewable source used in nuclear power plants.
Importance of Non-Renewable Resources
  • Foundation of Industries: Coal, petroleum, and natural gas are the backbone of industries, transport, and electricity generation.
  • Essential Fuels for Daily Life: Petrol, diesel, and LPG are used in vehicles, homes, and industries, supporting the functioning of cities, towns, and rural areas.
  • Building and Technology Needs: Metals like iron and copper are used in construction, machines, and electronics, while gold and silver are important for trade ornaments and storing wealth.
Conservation and Challenges of Non-Renewable Resources
  • Limited in Supply: These resources take millions of years to form, so once exhausted, they cannot be replaced in our lifetime. Their scarcity can lead to energy crises.
  • Rising Costs and Conflicts: Excessive dependence increases demand, causing prices to rise and even conflicts between nations over oil and mineral-rich regions.
  • Environmental Harm: Burning coal and petroleum releases greenhouse gases that cause global warming, while mining activities damage land and forests.
  • Need for Alternatives and Wise Use: Recycling of metals, energy-efficient practices, and shifting to renewable sources like solar, wind, and biogas are necessary to reduce dependence on non-renewables.

DISTRIBUTION OF NATURAL RESOURCES AND ITS IMPLICATIONS

Natural resources such as water, soil, forests, minerals, and energy sources are not evenly spread across the Earth. Some regions are naturally rich in certain resources, while others have very few. For example, the Middle East has vast oil reserves, while countries like Japan have very limited mineral resources. In India, coal and iron ore are concentrated in states like Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh, while fertile alluvial soil is found in the Indo-Gangetic Plains. This uneven distribution influences how people live, where industries grow, how trade develops, and sometimes even leads to disputes between regions or countries.

Impact on Settlements

Areas with abundant natural resources often attract more people and grow into centres of settlement, farming, or industry. Fertile soil and water availability support agriculture, while forests provide timber and other products for daily life. For example, river valleys have always been densely populated because of the availability of water and fertile land. On the other hand, regions with very few resources—such as deserts or cold mountains—have sparse populations because they cannot support large-scale farming or industries.

Industrial Growth and Employment

Areas that are rich in resources such as coal, petroleum, iron ore, or natural gas often develop into major industrial centres. Industries require raw materials and energy, so they tend to be set up near resource-rich regions. Workers from other regions also migrate to these areas in search of employment, leading to urban growth. This process of industrialisation not only increases production and trade, but also changes the lifestyle of people. However, such growth also brings challenges like air and water pollution, poor living conditions in crowded cities, and pressure on civic facilities.

Displacement of People

The use of natural resources often demands large-scale projects like mining, dam construction, and deforestation, which can displace people from their homes. When forests are cleared for industries or dams are built for irrigation and hydroelectric power, thousands of families—especially tribal and rural communities—are forced to move. Displacement not only causes the loss of houses but also destroys people's livelihoods. Farmers lose fertile land, tribals lose access to forests, and entire communities lose their cultural traditions and social bonds.

Conflicts Over Resources

Resources are not distributed evenly and this often leads to disputes over who should control and use them. Shared resources such as rivers, forests, and minerals frequently become a source of conflict between states or even countries. A well-known example in India is the Kaveri River water dispute between Karnataka and Tamil Nadu. At the global level, many countries compete for oil, natural gas, and freshwater reserves. These conflicts can create political tensions, reduce cooperation, and in extreme cases, even lead to wars.

THE RESOURCE CURSE

It may seem natural to think that countries rich in natural resources should always be wealthy and developed. However, this is not always true. In fact, some nations with huge reserves of oil, coal, or minerals remain poor and underdeveloped. This surprising situation is known as the 'Resource Curse' or the 'Paradox of Plenty.' It happens when the wealth earned from natural resources does not benefit the common people, but instead creates economic, political, and social problems.

Causes of the Resource Curse

  • Corruption and Misuse of Wealth: The income from resources is sometimes controlled by a few powerful people or groups and instead of using it for education, healthcare, and infrastructure, it gets wasted or stolen.
  • Inequality in Society: When only certain sections of the population enjoy the benefits of resource wealth, while others remain poor, it creates a wide gap between the rich and the poor.
  • Overdependence on Resources: Many countries rely only on oil, coal, or minerals and do not invest in other sectors like farming, industries, or technology. If resource prices fall in the global market, their entire economy suffers.
  • Continuous Conflicts: Natural resources can become a reason for fights between communities, groups, or even countries. Wars and political instability make it difficult to use resources properly.

RESPONSIBLE AND WISE USE OF RESOURCES: STEWARDSHIP

The Earth's resources are limited and precious, so they must be used with great care. This careful management of resources is called stewardship. It means acting as responsible caretakers of nature so that resources are available not only for us but also for future generations. If resources are wasted or overused, they may get exhausted and human life will face serious problems. Therefore, wise planning and sustainable practices are necessary to ensure that development continues without harming the environment.

Restoration and Regeneration

Natural resources such as forests, rivers, and soils have the natural ability to renew and restore themselves, but this process works only if humans allow nature enough time and space to heal.
* Forests: When trees are cut down for timber, fuel, or farming, it is important to replace them with new trees through reforestation and afforestation. Healthy forests provide oxygen, absorb carbon dioxide, and are home to countless animals and plants.
* Rivers: Rivers naturally refill through rainfall and underground recharge. Keeping rivers clean and pollution-free ensures that future generations can also depend on them.
* Soils: Fertile soil forms very slowly over thousands of years. To keep it healthy, farmers must use natural methods such as crop rotation, organic manure, and compost. Relying only on chemical fertilisers reduces soil fertility in the long run and harms the environment.
* Balance in Nature: Restoration and regeneration are essential to maintain the balance of nature. When humans support natural processes, resources remain available, biodiversity flourishes, and ecosystems stay strong for future generations.

The Case of Cement

Cement has become an essential part of modern life. Our houses, schools, hospitals, bridges, roads, and airports are all built using cement. However, cement production is also considered one of the most polluting industries. During its manufacture, fine dust is released into the air, which can enter human and animal lungs, causing breathing problems. To address these problems, the Central Pollution Control Board has made strict guidelines for cement factories to ensure that pollution is reduced or eliminated. Many factories are now adopting new technologies to minimise dust and waste. There is also growing interest in using alternative materials that are more eco-friendly, such as mud, stone, plant-based products, and recycled materials from waste plastic.

Judicious Use of Resources

Non-renewable resources like coal, petroleum, and natural gas exist in limited amounts. These resources were formed millions of years ago from the remains of plants and animals buried under the Earth. Since they take such a long time to form, once they are used up, they cannot be replaced easily.
* Use with Care: Because of their limited nature, these resources must be used wisely. If we waste them, future generations will have to face shortages of energy and raw materials.
* Transport Choices: Using private vehicles all the time consumes large amounts of petrol and diesel. Instead, if people use buses, trains, carpools, or bicycles, fuel consumption is reduced, traffic pollution decreases, and energy is saved.
* Energy Efficiency: Industries are among the biggest users of fossil fuels. By using modern, energy-saving machines and recycling raw materials, they can reduce energy waste. In homes, switching off lights, fans, and electronic gadgets when not needed or using LED bulbs and solar water heaters, can also save electricity.
* Renewable Alternatives: To reduce overdependence on coal and petroleum, we should promote renewable sources like solar energy, wind energy, biofuel, hydropower, and geothermal. These sources are clean, unlimited, and do not harm the environment.

Overexploitation of Resources

Overexploitation happens when humans use resources faster than nature's ability to replace them. This creates a situation where resources become scarce and the environment gets damaged.
* Groundwater Overuse: Continuous pumping of underground water for farming and drinking lowers the water table, causing wells and tube wells to dry up. This leads to water scarcity and makes irrigation difficult in many regions.
* Excessive Extraction: Taking resources like minerals, water, and forests beyond nature's capacity makes land infertile, turns fields barren, and disturbs natural cycles such as soil renewal and rainfall.
* Ecosystem Damage: Overexploitation reduces biodiversity as forests are destroyed, fish populations decline, and wildlife habitats vanish, upsetting the balance of nature.
* Unsustainable Practices: Mining, deforestation, and overgrazing leave behind polluted soil, eroded land, and long-term environmental problems, making resources scarce for future generations.

ENVIRONMENTAL IMPACT OF RESOURCE USE

The unplanned and careless use of natural resources not only reduces their availability, but also causes serious harm to the environment. The effects of human activities are felt in air, water, soil, plants, animals, and even human health.
* Soil Degradation: Farmers often use chemical fertilisers and pesticides to increase crop production quickly. However, long-term use of these chemicals damages soil health, kills helpful microorganisms, and makes soil less fertile.
* Air Pollution: Burning coal in power plants and petroleum in vehicles releases harmful gases such as carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, and nitrogen oxides. These gases cause air pollution, acid rain, breathing problems, and also trap heat in the atmosphere, leading to global warming.
* Water Pollution: Rivers, lakes, and ponds often get polluted when industries release toxic chemicals or when untreated sewage is dumped into them. Polluted water is unsafe for drinking, destroys aquatic life, and spreads diseases among humans.
* Deforestation: Large-scale cutting of forests destroys habitats of animals and birds, leading to loss of biodiversity. Without trees, the soil is easily eroded, rainfall patterns change, and the chances of floods and droughts increase.
* Climate and Health: Pollution and resource misuse affect human health by causing diseases like asthma, skin problems, and waterborne infections. Climate change caused by global warming leads to rising sea levels, melting glaciers, and extreme weather events.

SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT

Sustainable Development — Sustainable development means using natural resources carefully so that we can meet our present needs without destroying or exhausting them for future generations. It is like borrowing resources from the Earth and returning them in such a way that our children and grandchildren can also enjoy them. To achieve this, people must balance economic growth, social welfare, and environmental care.
* Respect and Care for all Forms of Life: Every plant, animal, and human being has a role to play in nature. We must protect them instead of destroying them.
* Improve the Quality of Human Life: Development should not just mean more wealth but also better health, education, and happiness for people.
* Conserve the Earth’s Vitality and Diversity: The variety of plants, animals, forests, rivers, and ecosystems must be preserved, as they are all connected to human survival.

The Three Rs

One simple way to practice sustainable development in daily life is to follow the Three Rs:
* Reduce: We should try to use fewer resources wherever possible to prevent waste. For example, if your school is nearby, walking or cycling instead of taking a car saves fuel and reduces pollution. Switching off lights, fans, and TVs when not in use saves electricity. Closing taps tightly helps save water. By reducing wastage, we also save money and protect nature.
* Reuse: Instead of throwing things away, we can use them again in different ways. Old clothes can be stitched into rugs, mops, or doormats. Used bottles can be refilled or turned into containers. Old newspapers and jute sacks can be made into shopping bags. Reusing not only reduces waste but also helps us to be creative and resourceful.
* Recycle: Recycling means collecting and processing old materials to make new things. Items like paper, glass, tin, plastic, and iron can be recycled many times. For example, old newspapers sold to scrap dealers are sent to recycling factories where they are turned into new paper products. Recycling reduces the need to cut more trees or mine new minerals, thus saving natural resources and reducing pollution.

Community-Owned Resources — Resources used by all members of a community and belonging to no single person, such as public parks.
Conservation — The careful management and protection of natural resources to ensure their availability for future generations.
Cultural Acceptance — The factor determining if a resource is useful to society based on culture, lifestyle and social needs.

Fossil Fuels — Non-renewable energy sources like coal, petroleum and natural gas formed from ancient organic matter over millions of years.
Global Warming — The increase in Earth's average temperature caused by greenhouse gases trapping heat in the atmosphere.
Individual Resources — Privately owned resources such as land, houses and vehicles used by individuals for personal benefit.
International Resources — Resources lying beyond the control of any one country that belong to all of humanity.
Judicious Use — Using limited resources wisely and carefully to prevent waste and ensure availability for future generations.
Localised Resources — Resources found only in specific places and unevenly distributed across the world, such as minerals.

Non-Renewable Resources — Resources that take millions of years to form and cannot be quickly replaced once they are used up.
Overexploitation — Using resources faster than nature's ability to replace them, leading to scarcity and environmental damage.
Potential Resources — Resources whose actual quantity is not known and which are not being used at present but may be used in the future.
Reforestation — The restoration of forest cover by replanting trees after they have been cut down for timber or farming.
Renewable Resources — Resources that can be replaced or restored by natural processes within a human lifetime if used wisely.
Resource Curse — A situation where nations with huge reserves of natural resources remain poor due to mismanagement and conflict.
Sustainable Development — Using natural resources carefully to meet present needs without destroying or exhausting them for future generations.
Stewardship — Acting as responsible caretakers of nature so that resources are available not only for us but also for future generations.
Ubiquitous Resources — Resources that are found almost everywhere on Earth, such as air, sunlight and water vapour.

C. Application/Concept Based Questions

I. Answer the following questions in brief.

  1. Question: What are the three main factors that decide whether something can be used as a resource? Explain briefly.
    Answer: The three factors are:
    • Accessibility: Can we reach it? (e.g., coal deep inside earth needs mining tech).
    • Feasibility: Is it possible to use it practically and affordably? (e.g., solar panels needed for sunlight).
    • Cultural Acceptance: Is it useful to society? (e.g., society prefers clean energy over firewood now).
  2. Question: What are resources essential for life? Give examples.
    Answer: These are resources necessary for survival. Examples include Air (oxygen), Water (drinking/crops), Food (nutrients), and Soil (growing crops).
  3. Question: How do material resources support human development?
    Answer: Material resources provide raw materials for construction, industries, and household needs. Examples: Wood for furniture, stones for buildings, minerals for machines, gold/silver for trade. They improve living standards and economic growth.
  4. Question: Why are energy resources considered the driving force of development?
    Answer: Every machine, vehicle, and industry needs energy to run. Energy makes life faster and more efficient (e.g., electricity for homes/fans, fuel for transport). Without energy, modern life and development would stop.
  5. Question: What do you understand by accessibility, feasibility and cultural acceptance in resource use?
    Answer:
    • Accessibility: Availability for use (e.g., ocean oil needs extraction tech).
    • Feasibility: Practicality and affordability (e.g., windmills needed for wind energy).
    • Cultural Acceptance: Societal desire for the resource (e.g., preference for clean energy over fossil fuels).
  6. Question: Explain with examples the uneven distribution of natural resources on Earth.
    Answer: Resources are not spread evenly. For example, the Middle East has vast oil reserves, while Japan has limited minerals. In India, coal is in Jharkhand/Chhattagargh, while fertile soil is in Indo-Gangetic Plains. This affects settlement and industry location.

II. Answer the following questions in detail.

  1. Question: Describe the classification of natural resources on the basis of origin, development, and ownership with examples.
    Answer:
    • On Basis of Origin: Biotic (Living: Plants, Animals) and Abiotic (Non-living: Minerals, Water).
    • On Basis of Development: Actual (Known and used: Coal in Jharkhand) and Potential (Not yet used: Uranium in Ladakh).
    • On Basis of Ownership: National (Govt controlled: Rivers, Forests), International (Beyond EEZ: Deep-sea minerals), Individual (Privately owned: Farm land), Community-Owned (Public parks, lakes).

    Answer:
    • Actual Resources: Quantity is known and currently used. Example: Coal reserves in Jharkhand, Water of Ganga River.
    • Potential Resources: Quantity unknown or not used yet, but may be used later. Example: Uranium deposits in Ladakh, Unexplored oil wells near Western Coast, Wind energy in Rajasthan (earlier potential, now actual).
  3. Question: Discuss the importance and challenges of renewable and non-renewable resources.
    Answer:
    • Renewable: Importance: Support survival (air, water), help development (timber, soil), eco-friendly energy. Challenges: Risk of overuse (deforestation), pollution threat, soil degradation.
    • Non-Renewable: Importance: Foundation of industries, essential fuels, building materials. Challenges: Limited supply, rising costs/conflicts, environmental harm (pollution, global warming).
  4. Question: What is the impact of overexploitation of natural resources on the environment? Illustrate with the case study of Punjab.
    Answer: Overexploitation leads to soil degradation, air/water pollution, deforestation, and climate change. Case Study of Punjab: During the Green Revolution, farmers pumped groundwater extensively for high-yield crops. This caused severe depletion of groundwater (water table fell >30m in some areas), soil degradation from chemicals, and health hazards. 80% of the state is now 'over-exploited'.
  5. Question: Describe the principles of sustainable development and explain how the three Rs (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle) help achieve it.
    Answer:
    • Principles: Respect all life, improve quality of human life, conserve Earth's vitality and diversity.
    • Three Rs:
      • Reduce: Use fewer resources (e.g., switch off lights, walk instead of drive).
      • Reuse: Use items again (e.g., old clothes into rugs, refill bottles).
      • Recycle: Process old materials into new things (e.g., paper, plastic, metal).
    • Together, they minimize waste and conserve resources for future generations.
  6. Question: Write a detailed note on responsible and wise use of resources with examples from the Sikkim organic farming initiative and the Chipko Movement.
    Answer: Responsible use involves stewardship—acting as caretakers. Sikkim Initiative: Sikkim adopted organic farming (2016), avoiding chemical fertilizers. This improved soil fertility, increased biodiversity, and raised farmer incomes. Chipko Movement: In Uttarakhand, people hugged trees to stop logging, protecting forests. Both examples show how community action and wise practices ensure resource sustainability and ecological balance.

Categories of Natural Resources

Natural resources are not all used in the same way. Some are necessary for our survival, while others are used for making things or for producing energy. Broadly, we can group them into three categories:

  • Resources Essential for Life: Some natural resources are so important that without them, no living being can survive. Air, water and food are the most basic needs for humans, animals and plants. Air provides oxygen that we breathe in and plants use carbon dioxide from the air to make their own food through photosynthesis. Water satisfies our thirst, helps us stay clean and is needed for growing crops. Soil plays an equally important role because it is where crops and plants grow, which provide us with food. For example, clean drinking water keeps us healthy, fresh air prevents diseases and nutritious food gives us energy to live, study and work. Imagine a world without water—crops would dry, people and animals would suffer and life on Earth would collapse. That is why these resources are called essential resources for life. They form the foundation of survival and must be protected carefully to ensure the well-being of all living beings.

Classification of Natural Resources

On the Basis of Origin

Biotic Resources

Living things are biotic resources. They include plants, animals, fish, forests, agricultural crops and even tiny micro-organisms. Since they have the ability to reproduce and grow naturally, they are considered renewable resources. For example, a tree that is cut down can grow again if another is planted and animals reproduce to maintain their population. Forests provide timber, fruits and medicines, while domestic animals like cows and goats give us milk and meat. These resources are directly linked to human survival and economic activities like farming, fishing and animal husbandry.

Abiotic Resources

Non-living things, such as land, water, minerals, metals, coal, petroleum and air are abiotic resources. These are extremely important for industries, agriculture and human settlements. However, most abiotic resources are non-renewable, meaning that once they are used up, they cannot be replaced quickly. For example, minerals like iron, copper and gold take millions of years to form inside the earth, so their total stock cannot be increased by human effort. Excessive exploitation of such resources can cause shortages in the future. Therefore, careful use and conservation of these resources is very important for sustainable development.

On the Basis of Development

Actual Resources

Actual resources are those whose quantity is known and which are being used at present. For example, the coal reserves in Jharkhand are actual resources because we know how much coal is there and we are already using it to produce energy in factories. Similarly, the water of the Ganga River is an actual resource because it is being used for irrigation, drinking, transportation and hydroelectricity. The dark, fertile soils of the Deccan Plateau in Maharashtra and the petroleum deposits of West Asia are also examples of actual resources. These resources are measurable and play a direct role in our lives and economy.

Potential Resources

Potential resources are those whose actual quantity is not known and which are not being used at present, but may be used in the future. For example, the uranium deposits in Ladakh are potential resources because they are not being fully utilised now but may be used later to generate nuclear energy. Similarly, unexplored oil wells near the Western Coast of India are potential resources waiting to be tapped. Wind energy is another example; earlier, it was not widely used in India, but today, states like Tamil Nadu have developed wind power plants and become leading producers of wind energy. This shows how a potential resource can be turned into an actual resource when technology and demand make it possible.

On the Basis of Ownership

National Resources

National resources are those that belong to a country and are under the control of its government. These include land, forests, rivers, minerals and wildlife found within the political boundaries of that country. It also includes resources in the ocean up to 12 nautical miles (19.2 km) from the coastline. The government can use these resources for the development of the nation, such as building dams, conserving forests or mining minerals. Citizens can use them, but always under rules set by the government.

International Resources

International resources are those that lie beyond the control of any one country. For example, the resources in the open ocean beyond 200 km from a country's Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) belong to all of humanity. No single country can claim them as its own. These resources, like deep-sea minerals, oil or marine life, can only be used with the permission and agreement of international organisations such as the United Nations. This rule ensures fairness so that stronger countries do not exploit all the resources for themselves.

Individual Resources

Individual resources are those owned privately by people. In villages, farmers own land, houses, wells or livestock, while in cities, people own flats, plots, shops or vehicles. These resources are used by individuals for their personal benefit and livelihood. Ownership is usually recognised by legal documents like sale deeds or property papers. For example, a farmer's field or a family's house in the city is an individual resource.

Community-Owned Resources

Community-owned resources are those which are used by all members of a community and belong to no single person. These include public parks, playgrounds, lakes, community halls and grazing lands. For example, villagers may use common grazing fields for their cattle and urban residents may use public parks for recreation. These resources are important because they promote sharing, cooperation and collective responsibility in society.

On the Basis of Renewability

Renewable Resources

Renewable resources are those which can be replaced or restored by natural processes within a human lifetime. Unlike resources that take millions of years

to form, renewable resources are continuously available in nature, but they are not unlimited. Their availability depends on how carefully we use them. For example, sunlight and wind are freely available and never run out, while forests and soil can regenerate if used responsibly. However, if these resources are exploited carelessly—such as cutting down too many trees, polluting rivers or overusing farmland—they may become scarce and difficult to use. Thus, renewable resources are called 'renewable' only when human beings manage them wisely through proper conservation practices.

Examples of Renewable Resources

  • Sunlight: An unlimited and clean source of energy that reaches us daily, useful for solar panels and natural light.
  • Wind: Strong winds can generate electricity through windmills, providing a pollution-free energy source.
  • Water from Rivers: Freshwater is needed for drinking, irrigation and also for generating hydropower.
  • Soil: Fertile soil allows crops to grow, supporting farming and food security.
  • Timber (wood): Forest trees provide wood for houses and furniture, but replanting is necessary to maintain supply.
  • Geothermal Energy: Heat from inside the Earth can be used to produce electricity and warm buildings naturally.
  • Biomass: Organic materials like crop waste, animal dung and plant matter can be converted into biofuel for cooking and electricity generation.

Importance of Renewable Resources

  • Support for Survival: Renewable resources like air, water and soil provide the foundation of life by giving oxygen, drinking water and fertile land for crops. Without them, humans, animals and plants cannot survive.
  • Help in Development: These resources are essential for human progress. For example, water supports agriculture, timber supports construction and soil supports farming communities, ensuring economic growth.
  • Eco-friendly Energy: Resources like sunlight and wind provide clean, renewable energy that does not cause pollution, helping fight climate change and reduce dependence on fossil fuels.

Conservation and Challenges of Renewable Resources

  • Risk of Overuse: Even though renewable, these resources can become scarce if exploited carelessly, like deforestation reducing timber and harming wildlife.
  • Pollution Threat: Pollution of rivers, lakes and air can make renewable resources unsafe for use, harming health and reducing availability.
  • Soil Degradation: Overfarming, heavy use of chemicals and erosion reduce soil fertility, making it difficult to grow crops in the future.
  • Conservation Measures Needed: Practices like afforestation, rainwater harvesting, organic farming and promoting solar and wind energy are necessary to protect renewable resources for the future.

Non-Renewable Resources

Non-renewable resources are those that take millions of years to form in nature and cannot be quickly replaced once they are used up. These resources are stored deep inside the Earth and are the result of slow geological processes such as the decay of plants and animals over ages or the compression of minerals under heat and pressure. Since they are formed over such a long period, they are present only in limited amounts. Once consumed, they cannot be replenished in our lifetime. This makes them precious and important for development, but also a cause for concern if used without care. Their overuse not only reduces their supply but also causes serious damage to the environment through pollution, mining and greenhouse gas emissions.

Examples of Non-Renewable Resources

  • Coal: A fossil fuel used in thermal power plants and industries.
  • Petroleum (crude oil): Provides petrol, diesel, kerosene, LPG and plastics used in daily life.
  • Natural Gas: Used for cooking in households and as fuel in industries.
  • Minerals and Metals: Iron, copper, aluminium and gold, which are vital for construction, transport, machines and jewellery.
  • Nuclear Energy: Produced from uranium and thorium, it is a powerful non-renewable source used in nuclear power plants to generate electricity efficiently with very little fuel.

Importance of Non-Renewable Resources

  • Foundation of Industries: Coal, petroleum and natural gas are the backbone of industries, transport and electricity generation, making them central to modern development.

Causes of the Resource Curse

There are many reasons why resource-rich countries may still struggle with poverty:

RESPONSIBLE AND WISE USE OF RESOURCES: STEWARDSHIP

The Earth's resources are limited and precious, so they must be used with great care. This careful management of resources is called stewardship. It means acting as responsible caretakers of nature so that resources are available not only for us but also for future generations. If resources are wasted or overused, they may get exhausted and human life will face serious problems. Therefore, wise planning and sustainable practices are necessary to ensure that development continues without harming the environment.

Natural resources such as forests, rivers and soils have the natural ability to renew and restore themselves, but this process works only if humans allow nature enough time and space to heal. If resources are continuously exploited without giving them a chance to recover, they become weak and eventually get destroyed.

Groundwater Crisis in Punjab

Punjab, known as the 'Granary of India,' played a key role in the Green Revolution by producing large amounts of wheat and rice to ensure food security for the country. However, this success came at a cost. Farmers shifted to high-yielding varieties of crops in the 1960s and 1970s, which required far more water than traditional varieties. To meet this demand, they began pumping groundwater extensively through tube wells.

In the short term, this practice increased crop production, but in the long term, it caused severe depletion of groundwater. Free electricity for agriculture encouraged over-pumping, while heavy use of chemical fertilizers and pesticides polluted underground water. As a result, the water table in Punjab has fallen to alarming levels, in some areas more than 30 metres deep. Today, almost 80% of the state's area is classified as 'over-exploited,' meaning water is being withdrawn much faster than it can be naturally replenished.

The combined effect is that irrigation has become difficult, wells have dried up and polluted water has created health hazards. While Punjab ensured food security for India in the short run, the long-term consequences—falling water levels, soil degradation and unsafe drinking water—pose a serious threat to sustainability. This shows how overexploitation of a single resource can damage the environment and affect millions of lives.

ENVIRONMENTAL IMPACT OF RESOURCE USE

The unplanned and careless use of natural resources not only reduces their availability, but also causes serious harm to the environment. The effects of human activities are felt in air, water, soil, plants, animals and even human health.

  • Soil Degradation: Farmers often use chemical fertilisers and pesticides to increase crop production quickly. However, long-term use of these chemicals damages soil health, kills helpful microorganisms and makes less fertile.
  • Air Pollution: Burning coal in power plants and petroleum in vehicles releases harmful gases such as carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides. These gases cause air pollution, acid rain, breathing problems and also trap heat in the atmosphere, leading to global warming.

SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT

Sustainable development means using natural resources carefully so that we can meet our present needs without destroying or exhausting them for future generations. It is like borrowing resources from the Earth and returning them in such a way that our children and grandchildren can also enjoy them. To achieve this, people must balance economic growth, social welfare and environmental care.

Some important principles of sustainable development are:
* Respect and Care for all Forms of Life – Every plant, animal and human being has a role to play in nature. We must protect them instead of destroying them.
* Improve the Quality of Human Life – Development should not just mean more wealth but also better health, education and happiness for people. (handwritten note: value)
* Conserve the Earth’s Vitality and Diversity – The variety of plants, animals, forests, rivers and ecosystems must be preserved, as they are all connected to human survival.

The Three Rs

One simple way to practice sustainable development in daily life is to follow the Three Rs: Reduce, Reuse and Recycle.

  • Reduce: We should try to use fewer resources wherever possible to prevent waste. For example, if your school is nearby, walking or cycling instead of taking a car saves fuel and reduces pollution. Switching off lights, fans and TVs when not in use saves electricity. Closing taps tightly helps save water. By reducing wastage, we also save money and protect nature.
  • Reuse: Instead of throwing things away, we can use them again in different ways. Old clothes can be stitched into rugs, mops or doormats. Used bottles can be refilled or turned into containers. Old newspapers and jute sacks can be made into shopping bags. Reusing not only reduces waste but also helps us to be creative and resourceful.
  • Recycle: Recycling means collecting and processing old materials to make new things. Items like paper, glass, tin, plastic and iron can be recycled many times. For example, old newspapers sold to scrap dealers are sent to recycling factories where they are turned into new paper products. Recycling reduces the need to cut more trees or mine new minerals, thus saving natural resources and reducing pollution.
