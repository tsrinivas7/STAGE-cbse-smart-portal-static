5 Exploring Forces

WHAT IS A FORCE ?

Force — A push or pull acting on a body which tends to change its state of rest or of motion.
Pull — A force that changes the direction of an object towards you and makes it move towards you.
Push — A force that moves an object away from you.
Direction of the force — The direction in which the body is pushed or pulled.
Newton — The standard unit (SI) of force, denoted by the letter N.

  • A force is required to change the state of motion of an object (from rest to motion, faster, slower, or changing direction).
  • Force does not always make an object move; for example, pushing a large box may not displace it.
  • Force is usually denoted by the letter F.
  • Examples of force application include running, catching a ball, hitting a ball, and jumping.

WHAT CAN A FORCE DO TO THE BODIES ON WHICH IT IS APPLIED ?

A Force Acting on a Body can Change its State of Motion or Rest

  • A force can make a stationary body move (e.g., kicking a stationary football).
  • A force can stop a moving body (e.g., a fielder catching a moving cricket ball).
  • A force can increase the speed of a moving object if applied in the direction of motion.
  • A force can decrease the speed of a moving object if applied opposite to the direction of motion.

A Force can Change the Direction of Motion of a Moving Object

  • Force can alter the path of a moving object (e.g., a batsman changing the direction of a cricket ball).
  • Force can push objects aside (e.g., waving smoke away from a bus).
  • Changing direction requires applying force at a suitable angle relative to the motion.

A Force can Change the Shape and Size of an Object

  • Applying force on soft objects changes their size and shape (e.g., pressing a balloon).
  • Compressing foam or sponge changes its shape due to applied force.
  • Pulling a spring changes both its shape and size.
  • Squeezing a toothpaste tube changes its shape and causes paste to come out.

A Force can Stop a Moving Object

  • When a force is applied on a moving object in the opposite direction, it brings the object to rest.
  • Brakes apply force to stop a moving cycle.
  • Catching a ball applies force to stop its motion.

A Force can Change the Speed of a Moving Object

  • Pushing a moving object in the direction of motion increases its speed.
  • Pushing a moving object opposite to its motion decreases its speed.
  • The result depends on the alignment of the force vector relative to the velocity vector.

ARE FORCES AN INTERACTION BETWEEN TWO OR MORE OBJECTS ?

Applied — The force exerted on an object by another object.
Applies — The object that exerts the force on another object.

  • Force is the cause and the change in motion is the effect.
  • For a force to come into play, there must be two interacting objects.
  • Interaction involves one object applying force and another receiving it.
  • If interaction ceases, the force disappears (e.g., stopping pushing a car stops the force).
  • Whenever two objects interact, each experiences a force from the other.

WHAT ARE THE DIFFERENT TYPES OF FORCES ?

Contact Force

Contact Force — A force which acts only when the objects are in physical contact with each other.

  • Requires actual physical contact between objects (e.g., kicking a football, pulling a cart).
  • Acts on the point of contact.
  • Includes muscular force and frictional force.
  • Examples include stretching a spring or pushing a car.

Muscular Force

Muscular Force — The force exerted by the muscles of a human or animal body.

  • Used during walking, running, kicking, and lifting objects.
  • Helps in chewing food and digestion processes inside the body.
  • Allows heart muscles to pump blood for circulation.
  • Animals use it for heavy work like ploughing or carrying loads.

Friction or Frictional Force

Friction — The force acting between two surfaces in contact which opposes the motion of one body over the other.
Force of Friction — The specific force arising due to surface roughness opposing motion.

  • Acts along the two surfaces in contact.
  • Caused by irregularities on the surface of bodies locking into each other.
  • Rough surfaces exert greater friction than smooth surfaces.
  • Fluids (air, water) also exert frictional force on moving objects.
  • Friction produces heat and sound, causing energy loss in machines.
Friction: A Necessary Evil
  • Friction is beneficial for walking, holding screws, fixing nails, and writing.
  • Friction is harmful as it wears out materials (soles, bearings) and reduces efficiency.
  • Grooved soles and vehicle treads increase friction for better grip.
  • Lubrication and smoothing surfaces help minimize unwanted friction.

Noncontact Force (or Action-at-a-distance Force)

Non-contact Force — A force which interacts even from a distance without touching the object.
Action-at-a-distance Force — Another name for non-contact force.

  • Exerted without actual physical contact between objects.
  • Examples include gravitational, electrostatic, and magnetic forces.
  • Interactions occur even when objects are separated by space.

Magnetic Force

Magnetic Force — The force exerted by a magnet on another magnet or magnetic substance.

  • Magnets attract iron nails and pins from a distance.
  • Like poles of magnets repel each other.
  • Unlike poles of magnets attract each other.
  • Acts from a distance without physical touch.

Electrostatic Force

Electrostatic Force — The force between two charged bodies or one charged and the other uncharged body due to which they repel or attract each other.
Static Electricity — Electrical charge on bodies that cannot flow continuously.
Charged Body — A body which possesses electric charge on it.
Uncharged Body — A body which does not possess any electric charge on it.

  • Rubbing objects (like a comb or balloon) creates static charge.
  • Charged bodies attract uncharged bodies (e.g., paper pieces).
  • Similarly charged bodies repel each other.
  • Opposite kind of charges attract each other.
  • Only insulators can be charged with static electricity.

Gravitational Force

Gravitational Force — The force of attraction between any two objects possessing mass.
Vertical Motion — Movement in a vertical direction under the influence of gravitational force.

  • Always an attractive force (unlike magnetic or electrostatic forces).
  • Exists everywhere in the universe (e.g., Earth-Moon attraction).
  • Causes objects thrown upwards to slow down and fall back down.
  • Depends on the mass of the objects and distance between them.
  • Gravity pulls every object towards the center of the Earth.

WEIGHT AND ITS MEASUREMENT

Weight of a Body

Weight — The force with which the Earth pulls an object towards itself.

  • Equal to the force with which the body is attracted towards the Earth.
  • Varies from place to place (not constant).
  • Measured in the unit of force: Newton (N).
  • In space, weight can be zero (weightlessness).

Mass of a Body

Mass — The quantity of matter in a body.

  • Remains constant at all places.
  • Expressed in mass units like gram (g) or kilogram (kg).
  • Denoted by 'm'.
  • Measured using a beam balance or two-pan balance.

Differences Between Mass and Weight

  • Mass is the amount of matter; Weight is the gravitational force on that matter.
  • Mass remains constant; Weight varies with location (gravity).
  • Mass is measured in kg/g; Weight is measured in Newton (N).

Spring Balance

  • A device used to measure weight (force) assuming Earth's gravity.
  • Consists of a spring fixed at one end with a hook at the other.
  • The spring stretches according to the weight of the hanging object.
  • Scale shows weight in Newtons and often corresponding mass in grams.
  • Range and smallest division vary between different balances.

FLOATING AND SINKING

Upthrust — An upward force exerted by a liquid on a body immersed in it.
Buoyancy — The tendency of a fluid to exert an upward force on a body immersed partly or wholly in it.
Buoyant Force — The resultant upward force experienced by a body when immersed in a fluid.
Density — Indicates how much of a substance occupies a specific volume.
Archimedes' Principle — States that when an object is immersed wholly or partly in a fluid, it experiences a buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by it.

  • Objects float if buoyant force equals gravitational force.
  • Objects sink if gravitational force is more than buoyant force.
  • Pumice floats because it is less dense than water due to air pockets.
  • Immersed objects appear lighter due to the upward buoyant force.
  • Ships and aeroplanes are shaped to reduce friction from fluids (water/air).

Section 4: Let's Revise! Understanding (Page 91)

1. What is the buoyant force?

Answer: The buoyant force (or upthrust) is the resultant upward force experienced by a body when immersed in a fluid (liquid or gas). It is the tendency of a fluid to exert an upward force on a body immersed partly or wholly in it.

2. When does an object sink in a liquid?

Answer: An object sinks in a liquid when the gravitational force acting on it is more than the buoyant force.

3. In which case does an object float on a liquid?

Answer: An object floats on a liquid when the buoyant force is equal to the gravitational force acting on it (or if the weight of the liquid displaced is equal to the weight of the object).

4. When you drop a coin in a glass of water, it sinks, but when you place a bigger wooden block in water, it floats. Explain.

Answer: A coin sinks because its mass is greater than that of water, meaning the gravitational force is greater than the buoyant force. On the other hand, a wooden block floats because the gravitational force on it is less than the buoyant force.

(ii) During its downward motion

Answer: The only force acting on it throughout is gravity, pulling it downwards.
* (i) During upward motion: Gravity acts downwards, opposing motion (slowing it down).
* (ii) During downward motion: Gravity acts downwards, aligning with motion (increasing speed).
* (iii) At topmost position: Gravity acts straight down.

* (i) Stopping before A: Increase friction on the horizontal surface (e.g., spread sand or use a rough carpet).

II. LOGIC-BASED QUESTIONS

1. You apply muscular force on a ball to throw it. The ball flies in the air and then falls to the ground. It stops after rolling for a few seconds on the ground. Till what moment was the muscular force acting on the ball?
Answer: Muscular force acted only while the ball was in contact with the hand during the throwing motion. Once the ball leaves the hand, muscular force ceases to act on it.

2. Is gravitational force applied on the air around us? Explain.
Answer: Yes. Gravitational force acts on all objects possessing mass, including air molecules. This is why the atmosphere stays around the Earth.

3. Can a wall apply force? Explain with example.
Answer: Yes. When you push against a wall, the wall applies an equal and opposite normal force (reaction force) back on you. This prevents you from passing through it.

4. In which of the following cases is gravitational force acting on the apple?
(a) An apple kept on the ground.
(b) An apple thrown upwards.
(c) An apple falling from a tree.
Answer: (a), (b), and (c). Gravitational force acts on the apple in all cases as long as it is within Earth's gravitational field.

5. Name the forces that act on a child when she comes down a slide.
Answer: Gravitational force (pulling her down), Frictional force (opposing motion between clothes and slide), and Normal force (from the slide surface).

6. A ball at rest starts moving when you kick it. What does this tell you about the effect of force?

Friction: A Necessary Evil

Friction has both harmful and beneficial effects and hence it is called a necessary evil. One can walk because ground offers friction. In the case of less friction, walking becomes almost impossible.

Soles of shoes have grooves on them. Grooved soles offer more friction to the ground which gives better grip while walking. Shoes or slippers with worn out soles can be slippery.

The tyres of vehicles have treads for better grip over road. When treads are worn out, the tyres need to be replaced with new one.

Friction holds a screw in wood. It helps to fix boards together using nails.

Friction is evil too. It wears out the material like screws, ball bearings or soles of shoes etc.

It causes loss of energy. For example, the jar of a mixer becomes hot when it is run for few minutes. In fact, when a machine is operated, heat is generated and its efficiency decreases. It causes a loss of energy. Thus, there is a need to minimise friction.

Differences Between Mass and Weight

Mass and weight differ from each other in the following respects.

Mass Weight
1. The mass of a body is the amount of matter contained in it. 1. The weight of a body is the force with which it is attracted towards the Earth.

Spring Balance

Mass is measured using a two-pan balance or a beam balance. On the other hand, weight (force) is measured using a simple device called spring balance. It measures the weight on the assumption that spring balance is used on the Earth, with the Earth's gravitational force attracting the object.

A spring balance consists of a spring fixed at one end, with a hook attached at the other end. When we hang an object from the hook, the spring stretches, and the amount of stretching gives the weight of the object. There is a scale on the balance which is marked to show the weight in newton. There is usually another scale to show the corresponding value of mass in gram (g).

When an object is placed in a liquid, there are two forces acting on it
* the gravitational force due to the Earth acting downwards.
* buoyant force applied on it by the liquid upwards.

If the gravitational force is more than the buoyant force, the object sinks.

Know More !
Archimedes (287-212 BC), a celebrated mathematician, physicist and philosopher, discovered a principle which is popularly known as the Archimedes' principle. It states that—"When an object is immersed wholly or partly in a fluid (liquid or gas), it experiences a buoyant force which is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by it."
If the weight of the liquid displaced by an object is smaller than the weight of the object, the object will sink in the liquid. If the weight of the liquid displaced is equal to the weight of the object, the object will float in the liquid.
