Chapter Summary: Electricity — Magnetic and Heating Effects

Electricity: Magnetic and Heating Effects (Introduction)

  • Effects of electricity — When electric current passes through a conductor, it can produce heat, magnetism, or chemical changes. These are known as the effects of electricity.
  • Electricity is an extremely useful form of energy used everywhere — at home, offices, factories, etc.
  • Understanding the effects of electricity helps us know how various electrical appliances work and how electricity makes our lives easier and more efficient.
  • This chapter focuses on two main effects: the magnetic effect and the heating effect of electric current.

Does an Electric Current Have a Magnetic Effect?

  • Magnetic field — The region around a magnet or a current-carrying wire where its magnetic effect can be felt.
  • Hans Christian Oersted (1777–1851), a Danish scientist, discovered in 1819 that when an electric current (DC) is passed through any conductor, a magnetic field is produced around it.
  • Magnetic effect of electric current — A phenomenon that occurs when electric current flows through a conductor (e.g., a wire) which produces a magnetic field around it. As soon as the current stops flowing, the magnetic field disappears.
  • The compass needle is a tiny magnet which deflects when a magnet is brought near it. This magnetic effect can act through any non-magnetic materials kept between the wire and the compass.
  • Electromagnets, electric bells, motors, fans, and loudspeakers are some practical applications of the magnetic effect of electric current.

Showing the Magnetic Effect of Current

  • A thin copper wire is connected to a battery through a key, with the wire placed in the north-south direction, and a magnetic needle is placed near it.
  • When the switch is turned ON, the magnetic needle gets deflected from its original north-south position.
  • When the switch is turned OFF, the needle returns to its original north-south position.
  • Reversing the direction of current also reverses the direction of deflection of the needle.
  • Conclusion: When electric current is passed through a wire, it acts like a magnet. The deflection indicates that the current-carrying wire has a magnetic effect on the compass needle.

Electromagnets

  • Electromagnet — A magnet made by winding a large number of turns of insulated copper wire on a soft iron core, which shows magnetic properties only as long as electric current flows through its coil.
  • Electromagnets are temporary magnets — they show magnetic properties only while current flows and lose magnetism when current is stopped.
  • Electromagnets of very high strength can be made easily.
  • The strength of an electromagnet depends on: (a) the number of turns in the coil, (b) the strength of the current flowing through the coil, and (c) the nature of the core material (soft iron core produces very strong electromagnets).
  • An electromagnet has two poles (north and south) like a bar magnet. The poles can be reversed by changing the direction of the current.

Uses of Electromagnets

  • For lifting heavy loads: Electromagnets are used on cranes in factories, steel-works, and scrap-yards for lifting, moving, and sorting heavy iron/steel loads. The operator can control the magnet by switching current ON and OFF.
  • In hospitals: Electromagnets are used for removing iron splinters from the eyes.
  • For separating magnetic substances from non-magnetic substances: Electromagnets can separate magnetic substances (iron, nickel, cobalt) from non-magnetic substances (copper, zinc, plastics).
  • In instruments: Electromagnets are used in electric bells, telegraphic and telephonic systems, speakers, and audio players.
  • The key advantage of electromagnets over permanent magnets is that their magnetic strength can be controlled by switching the current ON or OFF.

Earth's Magnetic Field

  • The Earth itself behaves like a giant magnet. The movement of liquid iron in the core of the Earth creates electric currents which generate a magnetic field.
  • A freely suspended magnet rests along the north-south direction due to Earth's magnetic field.
  • The Earth's magnetic field protects life on Earth by acting as a shield, blocking harmful particles from space.
  • It helps migratory birds, fish, and animals to navigate across continents and oceans.

Does a Current Carrying Wire Get Hot?

Heating Effect of Electric Current

  • Heating effect of current — The generation (production) of heat in a resistor (or conductor) when electricity passes through it.
  • When electric current flows through a resistor (such as a heating coil of nichrome wire), it gets heated up.
  • The heat produced in a resistor depends on three factors: (a) the current passed through the resistor, (b) the time (duration) for which the current is passed, and (c) the nature of the material.
  • Copper wires used for making connections in circuits do not get hot because copper is a good conductor and offers very little resistance to the flowing current.
  • Tube lights (fluorescent tubes) and CFLs do not contain current-carrying filaments, so they do not generate heat and are called cold sources of light.

Applications of the Heating Effect of Current

Electrical Heating Appliances

  • All electrical heating appliances are based on the heating effect of current.
  • Appliances such as electric iron, water heaters, geysers, room heaters, toasters, and hot plates/cooking ranges are fitted with heating coils made of high-resistance wire such as nichrome wire.
  • Nichrome — An alloy that contains iron, nickel, chromium, and manganese. It does not melt even at higher temperatures and has high resistivity.
  • In some appliances where the heating element is visible, it can be seen glowing red hot.
  • The heating effect can also cause problems like energy loss in wires during transmission, overheating of appliances, melting of plastic parts in plugs and sockets, and even fires.

Electric Filament Bulb

  • Filament — A very thin, high-resistance tungsten wire inside the glass shell of an electric bulb that gets heated up and emits light when current flows through it.
  • When current flows through the filament, it gets heated up, becomes white hot, and starts emitting light.
  • The filament is made of tungsten because it has high resistance and can withstand very high temperatures without melting.
  • All electrical heating appliances are fitted with a heating coil made from nichrome wire.

Electric Fuse

  • Electric fuse — A safety device which prevents damage to electrical appliances and possible fire by melting and breaking the circuit when excessive current passes through it.
  • Fuse wire — Made from a low-melting alloy. When large current passes through the circuit, the fuse wire gets heated up and melts away, breaking the circuit and preventing further damage.
  • Appropriate wires, plugs, and sockets should be rated for the specified electric current of the connections.
  • Miniature Circuit Breaker (MCB) — A kind of switch that automatically turns off in the case of a short circuit or overload.
  • Heating effect of electric current is also used in steel manufacturing industries, where specially designed high-temperature furnaces use electric current to melt and recycle scrap steel.

How Does a Battery Generate Electricity?

Voltaic Cell or Galvanic Cell

Invention

  • The voltaic cell is also known as the Galvanic cell, named after Italian scientists Alessandro Volta and Luigi Galvani.
  • In the late 1700s, Galvani observed that a dead frog's leg kicked when touched with copper or iron metals, and thought electricity came from the frog itself.
  • Volta believed the electricity came from the metals. He tested this by using saltwater-soaked paper instead of the frog's leg and still got electric current.
  • This proved that a combination of metals and a liquid (electrolyte) generated electric current, leading to the invention of the first battery.

Composition and Working

  • Electrodes — The two metallic plates made of different materials dipped in an electrolyte inside a voltaic cell.
  • Electrolyte — A liquid (usually a weak acid or salt solution) in which the electrodes are partly dipped, which helps conduct electricity through a chemical reaction.
  • A chemical reaction takes place between the plates (electrodes) and the electrolyte, which produces electricity.
  • When the circuit is connected, electric current flows from the positive terminal to the negative terminal through the external circuit.
  • When the chemicals get used up, the cell stops working and is called "dead" — it can no longer supply electricity.
  • A simple voltaic cell can be made at home using lemons (or salt solution in cups), copper strips/wires, and iron nails as electrodes, with lemon juice/salt solution as the electrolyte.

Dry Cell (or Battery Cell)

  • Dry cell — A source of electricity that generates direct current (DC) due to a chemical reaction inside it. A single dry cell gives a voltage of 1.5 volts.
  • The dry cell was invented by G. Leclanche in 1868 and is used in torches, transistors, toys, and clocks.
  • Zinc container — Acts as the negative terminal (anode) of the dry cell.
  • Carbon rod — Placed at the centre of the zinc container with a brass cap, it acts as the positive terminal of the dry cell.
  • The carbon rod is surrounded by a mixture of manganese dioxide and powdered charcoal. The remaining space is filled with a thick paste of ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl).
  • The cell is sealed from the top with pitch, and the outer side of the zinc container is covered with a cardboard cover.
  • Dry cells are actually not dry — they work only as long as the paste inside remains moist, as the moisture helps in the movement of ions (charged particles).
  • Electric current in a dry cell flows from the carbon electrode (positive) to the zinc electrode (negative) through the external circuit.

Advantages of Dry Cells

  • Dry cells are light in weight and small in size.
  • Dry cells can be transported from one place to another easily.
  • There is no fear of leakage or spillage in dry cells.

Disadvantage of Dry Cell

  • The biggest disadvantage of a dry cell is that it is a single-use cell — once used up, it has to be disposed of.
  • For this reason, rechargeable batteries are increasingly being used for several applications.

Rechargeable Batteries

  • Rechargeable batteries — Batteries that can be recharged and reused multiple times, overcoming the disadvantage of single-use dry cells.
  • They prevent wastage and save money.
  • They are increasingly being used for several applications now.
  • Common metal pairs for voltage cells include: zinc/copper, zinc/silver, aluminium/copper, iron/copper, magnesium/copper, and lead/copper.
  • Copper generally acts as the positive electrode while zinc acts as the negative electrode due to their chemical properties.

Activity 1 — To Show That When an Electric Current is Passed Through a Wire, It Behaves Like a Magnet

Materials Required: A piece of copper wire, Magnetic needle, Switch.

Procedure & Observations:
- When a bar magnet is brought near the magnetic needle, the needle gets deflected.
- When the magnet is removed, the needle returns to its N–S position.
- A thin copper wire is connected to a battery through a key, with section AB placed in the north-south direction.
- A magnetic needle is placed near wire AB and allowed to settle in the north-south direction.
- When the switch is turned ON, the magnetic needle gets deflected from its original north-south position.
- When the switch is turned OFF, the needle returns to its original north-south position.
- When the direction of current is reversed, the needle deflects in the opposite direction.

Conclusion: When electric current is passed through a wire, it acts like a magnet and produces a magnetic field around it. As soon as the current stops flowing, the magnetic field disappears.

Activity 2 — To Determine the Factors on Which the Strength of an Electromagnet Depends

Materials Required: Iron nail (long), Copper wire, Alpins, Key.

Procedure & Observations:
- A long thick iron nail is wound with an insulated copper wire to form a tight coil.
- The coil is connected to a battery and the switch is turned ON.
- When alpins are brought near one end of the nail, they stick to the nail.
- When the current is switched OFF, the alpins fall down.
- This shows the iron nail behaves like a magnet when current passes through the coil, and does not behave like a magnet when current is switched off.

Further experiments:
- (a) Increasing the number of turns in the coil → strength increases.
- (b) Changing (increasing) the current flowing through the coil → strength increases.
- (c) Replacing the soft iron nail with nails of steel, aluminium, etc. → strength varies with the nature of the core material. Soft iron produces the strongest electromagnet.

Observation: The strength of the electromagnet increases with:
- (a) The number of turns in the coil.
- (b) The strength of the current flowing through the coil.
- (c) The nature of the core material (soft iron core produces very strong electromagnets).

Activity 3 — To Determine How an Electromagnet Works

Materials Required: 100 cm long flexible insulated wire, A piece of chart paper, An iron nail, An electric cell, Two magnetic compasses, Few iron/steel paper clips.

Procedure & Observations:
- A cylinder is made from chart paper, and insulated copper wire is wound around it to form a tight coil.
- Compasses are placed near the two ends of the cylindrical coil.
- When the coil is connected to the cell, the compass needles get deflected — the coil behaves like a magnet.
- When the wire is disconnected from the cell, the magnetic effect disappears.
- When an iron nail is inserted in the paper cylinder and the steps are repeated, the coil becomes a stronger magnet — the deflection of the compass needles is much more, and it also attracts iron clips.
- The cylindrical coil loses its magnetic effect when the current is stopped.

Observation:
- When current is passed through the cylindrical coil, it behaves like a magnet and deflects the needle of a magnetic compass.
- When an iron nail is inserted in the core, the coil becomes a stronger magnet and the deflection is much more.
- It also attracts iron clips.
- The coil loses its magnetic effect when the current is stopped.

Activity 4 — To Check if Electromagnets Also Have Two Poles Like a Bar Magnet

Materials Required: Electromagnet (prepared in previous activity), Magnetic compass.

Procedure & Observations:
- The two ends of the coil are labelled A and B.
- A magnetic compass is placed near end A of the coil, and the coil is connected to the cell.
- If the north pole of the compass is attracted towards end A, then end A is the south pole (since unlike poles attract).
- If end A is south pole, then end B is the north pole.
- When the direction of current is reversed, the poles can be reversed.

Observation: Electromagnets do have two poles like a bar magnet. The polarity can be reversed by changing the direction of current.

Activity 5 — To Demonstrate the Heating Effect of Electric Current

Results: When electric current is passed through a bulb, it gives heat and light.

Conclusion: When electric current is passed through a resistor, heat is produced.

Activity 6 — To Show That When Electric Current is Passed Through a Resistor, Heat is Produced

Results: The nichrome wire gets warmer when electric current is passed through it.

Q: We have four coils, of similar shape and size, made up from iron, copper, aluminium, and nichrome as shown in Fig. 4.12. When current is passed through the coils, compass needles placed near the coils will show deflection:
(i) Only in circuit (a)
(ii) Only in circuits (a) and (b)
(iii) Only in circuits (a), (b), and (c)

  1. Copper has a high melting point — It does not melt easily when excessive current flows through it. A fuse wire must have a low melting point so that it melts quickly and breaks the circuit when excessive current flows.
  2. Copper is a good conductor with low resistance — It offers very little resistance to the flow of current, so it does not get heated enough to melt even when large current passes through it.
  3. If copper were used as a fuse wire, it would not melt in time to break the circuit during excessive current flow, and the electrical appliances could get damaged or a fire could occur.

A fuse wire is made from a low-melting-point alloy that melts quickly when excessive current flows, thereby breaking the circuit and protecting the appliances.

Question 2

Ans: When current is passed through the freely suspended copper wire coil, it will act as an electromagnet and will align itself along the north-south direction.

Reason: When current flows through the coil, it becomes an electromagnet with two poles (north and south). Like a freely suspended bar magnet, this electromagnet will experience the Earth's magnetic field and align itself along the north-south direction. Question 3

Q: You might have seen birds sitting on an overhead electric cable comfortably. Once a while, a monkey (or any other small animal) swings between the two parallel wires, gets electrocuted and dies. Give explanation.

Ans:

  • Birds sitting on a single cable: When a bird sits on a single electric cable, both its feet are at the same potential (voltage). Since there is no potential difference across the bird's body, no current flows through the bird's body. Without current flowing through it, the bird remains safe and unharmed.

  • Monkey swinging between two parallel wires: When a monkey swings between two parallel wires, it simultaneously touches two different wires. These two wires are at different potentials (one is the live wire at 220 volts, the other is the neutral wire at 0 volts). Because of this potential difference, a large current flows through the monkey's body (from the higher potential wire to the lower potential wire). This large current passing through the body causes electrocution, which can be fatal.

Key principle: Electric current flows only when there is a potential difference. On a single wire, there is no potential difference across the bird's body, so no current flows. Between two wires, there is a potential difference, so current flows through the animal's body, causing electrocution.

Voltaic cell or Galvanic Cell

Invention

The voltaic cell is also known as Galvanic cell. It gets its name from two Italian scientists, Alessandro Volta and Luigi Galvani. In the late 1700s, Galvani observed that when a dead frog's leg was touched with copper or iron metals, it kicked. Galvani thought that the electricity came from the frog itself because electricity could stimulate muscular motion. But Volta believed that the electricity came from metals. To check his theory, he used saltwater-soaked paper instead of the frog's leg and still got the electric current.

This proved that it was a combination of metals and liquid which generated electric current. This led to the invention of the first battery.

Composition and Working

A voltaic cell contains two metal plates made of different materials and a liquid called an electrolyte. This setup is placed in a glass or a plastic container. The plates are called electrodes. They are partly dipped in an electrolyte, which is usually a weak acid or salt solution. A chemical reaction takes place between the plates and the electrolyte, which produces electricity. When the circuit is connected, electric current flows from positive terminal to the negative terminal through the circuit.

When the chemicals get used up, the cell stops working. It is then called "dead" and cannot supply any more electricity.

You can make your own voltaic cell using some simple easily available materials.

In order to overcome the disadvantage of single-use dry cell, rechargeable batteries are used. They can be recharge and reuse multiple times which prevents wastage and saves money.
