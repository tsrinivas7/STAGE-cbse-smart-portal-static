Chapter Summary: Particulate Nature of Matter

MATTER

Matter — the material of which the universe is composed; something that occupies space, possesses mass, and offers resistance to any stress (force applied on it).

Substance — a definite variety of matter, all samples of which have the same properties. For example, sugar is a substance because even the smallest particle of sugar is sweet.

  • Greek and Indian philosophers believed everything was made up of five basic elements: Earth, Fire, Water, Air, and Sky (called panch tatva in Indian philosophy).
  • Science interprets nature in terms of energy and matter.
  • All objects around us, despite differences in colour, shape, size, and smell, share common properties — they have mass, occupy space, and can be sensed.

What is Meant by the Particulate Nature of Matter?

  • Ancient Indian philosophers considered all forms of matter to be made of five elements called bhutas — akasa (sky), vayu (air), tejas (fire), ap (water), and kshiti (earth).
  • Maharishi Kanada was perhaps the first to suggest that all forms of matter are composed of very small (tiny) particles in his work Vaisheshika Sutra.
  • Each tiny particle was given the name anu by Maharishi Kanada.
  • He further believed that each anu may be made of two or more still smaller particles called parmanu.

WHAT IS MATTER COMPOSED OF?

Constituent particle — the basic unit that makes up a larger piece of material; the tiny building blocks obtained when a substance is broken down to a stage where it cannot be broken further.

  • When a solid stick of chalk is broken and ground, only a physical change takes place — each piece reduces to powder without changing into a new substance.
  • Eventually, the chalk particles reach a stage where they cannot be broken down any further — these tiny units are the constituent particles of chalk.
  • Experiments (Activity 1) show that when sugar, potassium permanganate, or ink are added to water, they spread uniformly throughout the water, proving that matter is made of small particles.
  • The fragrance of burning incense sticks spreads from one room to another because the vapour particles mix with air particles.
  • These particles get into the interparticle spaces — the spaces between the particles (or molecules) of water or air — proving that all forms of matter consist of small particles.

STATES OF MATTER

  • Matter can exist in three states: Solid state, Liquid state, and Gaseous state.
  • For example, water can exist as ice (solid), water (liquid), and steam (gas).
  • All matter is made up of very small particles that are constantly in motion, thus possessing thermal energy.
  • Thermal energy of particles increases with a rise in temperature.
  • The particles in any matter attract each other; this force of attraction decreases as the distance between particles increases.

What Describes Different States of Matter

Intermolecular force (also called interparticle attraction) — the force of attraction between the particles in any matter.

  • The intermolecular force tends to bring particles closer together, while the motion of particles tends to push them apart.
  • The physical state of any matter depends on the net effect of these two factors: intermolecular force and particle motion.
  • When intermolecular force is very strong and particles move slowly → matter exists as a solid.
  • When intermolecular force is negligible and particles move very fast → matter exists as a gas.
  • When intermolecular force is moderately strong and particles move to and fro → matter exists as a liquid.

How does the strength of intermolecular forces vary in solids, liquids and gases?

  • Solids: Intermolecular forces are very strong; particles are closely packed → solids are incompressible, have definite shape, and high density.
  • Liquids: Intermolecular forces are strong enough to keep particles together but not strong enough to keep them in fixed positions → liquids can flow and do not have a definite shape.
  • Gases: Intermolecular forces are negligible (extremely weak); particles are free to move → gases can occupy any space available to them.

Solid State

Melting point — the minimum temperature at which a solid melts to become a liquid.

  • Solids are rigid and incompressible because particles are closely packed with virtually no space between them.
  • A solid has a definite shape and definite volume because particles are held together by strong intermolecular force.
  • In solids, intermolecular forces are strong; particles are held closely in fixed positions but can rotate and/or vibrate about their mean positions.
  • Solids have a high melting point due to strong intermolecular forces.
  • A sponge can be compressed easily because it has small pores with trapped air; when pressed, air is expelled and it gets compressed.

Liquid State

  • Liquids have no fixed (definite) shape — they take the shape of the container because constituent particles can move over short distances, slip, and slide past each other.
  • A liquid has a definite volume because interparticle forces are strong enough to keep constituent particles together.
  • Intermolecular forces in liquids are weaker than in solids, so intermolecular space in liquids is greater than in solids.
  • Liquids are slightly more compressible than solids due to greater intermolecular space (ice is an exception — its particles are farther apart than in water).
  • Liquids flow from higher level to lower level.
  • Activity 2 demonstrates that liquids have a definite volume but no fixed shape — water poured into containers of different shapes takes the shape of each container while the volume remains the same.

Gaseous State

  • The interparticle force in gases is negligible — constituent particles are free to move in any direction with high speeds.
  • Gases have no definite shape and no fixed volume — they fill the entire space available to them.
  • Gases are highly compressible because particles are far apart with enough space for compression.
  • Due to high compressibility, large volumes of gas can be compressed into small cylinders — e.g., LPG (Liquefied Petroleum Gas) and CNG (Compressed Natural Gas) cylinders.
  • Activity 3 demonstrates that gases do not have a fixed volume — smoke collected in one gas jar spreads to fill a second jar when the partition is removed.
  • Both liquids and gases are classified as fluids because they flow and do not retain a fixed shape.

HOW DOES THE INTERPARTICLE SPACING DIFFER IN THE THREE STATES OF MATTER?

  • Activity 4 demonstrates interparticle space: when sugar is added to water, the water level rises, but after dissolving, the level is lower than the sum of the two volumes — proving that sugar particles occupy the spaces between water particles.
  • The same result is obtained with other soluble solids like glucose or common salt.
  • Insoluble solids (like stone pieces or sand) settle down and increase the total volume in the container.
  • Even in solids, where particles are closely packed, there is some space left between particles — this space does not contain anything, not even air.
  • Interparticle spacing is least in solids, more in liquids, and most in gases.

HOW PARTICLES MOVE IN DIFFERENT STATES OF MATTER?

Diffusion — the intermixing of particles of two or more substances on their own.

  • When potassium permanganate is added to water, water particles (being in constant motion) pull out the particles from the crystals and spread them throughout the liquid.
  • Particles move fastest in hot water, slower in water at room temperature, and slowest in ice-cold water — showing that movement of particles increases when heat is provided.
  • Diffusion is very fast in gases, slower in liquids, and almost negligible in solids.
  • The particulate nature of matter plays an important role in daily life — e.g., soap particles surround oil particles on fabric, with one end attaching to oil and the other mixing with water, helping to lift and wash away oil stains.

Movement of Gas Particles

  • The fragrance of burning incense sticks can be smelled in other rooms because particles of air are constantly moving and hit the fragrance particles, helping them spread throughout the room.
  • This also explains how the fragrance of perfume reaches us.
  • The tiny particles of smoke suspended in air are constantly hit by invisible gas particles, and their movement helps us observe the motion of gas particles.

Distinguishing Properties of Solids, Liquids, and Gases

  • Arrangement of particles: Solids — packed in definite patterns; Liquids — loosely packed; Gases — very poorly packed.
  • Interparticle space: Solids — least; Liquids — more than solids; Gases — most.
  • Volume and Shape: Solids — definite volume and definite shape; Liquids — definite volume but no fixed shape; Gases — neither definite volume nor shape.
  • Compressibility: Solids — negligible; Liquids — slightly more than solids; Gases — high.
  • Rigidity and Fluidity: Solids — very rigid, cannot flow; Liquids — less rigid, can flow easily; Gases — no rigidity, flow most easily.
  • Movement of particles: Solids — only vibrate about mean position; Liquids — move in all directions within the liquid; Gases — move randomly in all directions.
  • Interparticle forces: Solids — strongest; Liquids — less than solids; Gases — weakest.

Examples of Some Common Solids, Liquids, and Gases

  • Solids: Stone, Wax, Plastic, Wood, Silver, Iron, Gold, Ice, Sugar.
  • Liquids: Water, Milk, Oils, Alcohol, Glycerine, Acids, Chloroform, Honey, Juice.
  • Gases: Air, Oxygen, Hydrogen, Nitrogen, Ammonia, Steam, Sulfur Dioxide, Methane, Vapour.

CHANGE OF STATE

Change of state — change of a substance from one physical state to another. For example, conversion of ice into liquid water is a change of state (from solid to liquid).

  • Matter can exist in three physical states: solid, liquid, and vapour (gaseous).
  • Water can exist as ice (solid), water (liquid), and water vapour (gas).
  • The physical state of any substance at room temperature is considered its common form — e.g., the common form of water is liquid.
  • States can be changed by changing temperature: heating ice → water → steam; cooling steam → water → ice.

Changing the Physical State from Solid to Liquid by Changing Temperature

  • On heating, ice converts to liquid water, and further heating converts liquid water to steam.
  • On cooling, water vapour gives water (liquid), which on further cooling gives ice (solid).
  • The transformation can be represented as: Ice ⇌ Water ⇌ Water vapour (steam), where heating drives the forward reaction and cooling drives the reverse.

Explanation of the Change of States

  • In solids, intermolecular forces are very strong and particles are closely packed.
  • On heating, thermal (heat) energy of particles increases, setting them in motion; at a certain stage, the energy becomes sufficient to overcome binding forces, and the solid melts into a liquid.
  • On further heating, particles in the liquid gain more energy until they free themselves, and the liquid changes into vapour (gaseous state).
  • In gases, particles are far apart and do not exert any attractive/repulsive force on each other.

Melting Point and Freezing Point

Melting — the process in which a solid, on heating, changes into a liquid. For example, conversion of ice into water.

Melting point — the temperature at which a solid substance gets converted into a liquid.

Freezing — the process in which a liquid substance, on cooling, changes into a solid. For example, conversion of water to ice.

Freezing point — the temperature at which a liquid gets converted into a solid.

  • Solids with strong interparticle forces of attraction have high melting points, while solids with weak attractive forces have low melting points.
  • Examples of melting points: Ice — 0°C; Urea — 133°C; Iron — 1538°C.

Boiling Point

Boiling — rapid formation and breaking of bubbles in the bulk of a liquid being heated; a bulk phenomenon where particles from the bulk of the liquid gain enough energy to get converted to vapour.

Boiling point — the fixed temperature at which a liquid boils and turns to vapour at atmospheric pressure.

Evaporation — the process in which a liquid changes into its vapour at temperatures below its boiling point.

  • Boiling occurs at a fixed temperature depending on the nature of the liquid.
  • During boiling, particles move vigorously apart, reducing interparticle forces, and eventually escape from the liquid state as vapour — observed as bubble formation.
  • Vapour formation occurs at all temperatures through the process of evaporation.
  • Evaporation is a slow and silent process that takes place only on the surface of the liquid.
  • During evaporation, the liquid absorbs heat from the surroundings or from any body in contact with it — e.g., spilled water disappears over time, and clothes dry in the Sun as water absorbs heat energy from sunlight.
  • The rate of evaporation depends on: surface area of the liquid, wind speed, humidity, and temperature.

Activity 1 — TO SHOW THAT MATTER HAS PARTICULATE NATURE

Experiment 1 — Beaker A (Sugar in water):
- When sugar is added to water and stirred, the sugar dissolves completely. The level of the solution remains the same as the original water level. The solution looks like pure water, yet every drop tastes sweet. This shows that sugar breaks down into tiny particles that occupy the interparticle spaces between water particles.

Experiment 1 — Beaker B (Potassium permanganate in water):
- When a small crystal of potassium permanganate is placed in water and left undisturbed, the purple colour slowly spreads throughout the water until the entire solution becomes uniformly purple. This shows that the particles of potassium permanganate move and spread into the spaces between water particles.

Experiment 1 — Beaker C (Blue ink in water):
- When a drop of blue ink is gently placed on the surface of water, the colour slowly spreads until the entire solution becomes uniformly light-blue. This again demonstrates that ink particles spread into the interparticle spaces of water.

Experiment 2 (Fragrance of agarbatti):
- When incense sticks are lit in one room, the fragrance can be smelled in other rooms as well. The vapour from the agarbatti mixes with air particles and spreads throughout the rooms.

Conclusions:
- Sugar, potassium permanganate, and ink spread uniformly in water.
- The fragrance of agarbattis spreads uniformly in air.
- All forms of matter consist of very small particles.
- These particles get into the interparticle spaces between the particles of water or air.

Activity 2 — TO SHOW THAT LIQUIDS HAVE A DEFINITE VOLUME BUT NO FIXED SHAPE

Observation: When 200 mL of water is poured from one container into another of a different shape, the water takes the shape of each new container. However, the volume remains 200 mL throughout.

Conclusion: Liquids do not have a fixed shape — they take the shape of the container they are poured into. However, liquids have a definite volume, as the water level remained at 200 mL with no change in volume.

Activity 3 — TO SHOW THAT GASES DO NOT HAVE A FIXED VOLUME

Observation: When smoke collected in gas jar A is exposed to gas jar B (by removing the glass plate between them), the smoke spreads and fills the entire space in gas jar B as well.

Conclusion and Analysis:
- Gas particles move freely in all directions and the interparticle attractions are negligible.
- Gases neither have a fixed shape nor a fixed volume — they acquire the shape and fill the entire space of the vessel they are in.
- The tiny particles of smoke suspended in air are constantly hit by invisible gas particles, and their movement helps us observe the motion of gas particles.

Activity 4 — TO SHOW THAT INTERPARTICLE SPACE EXISTS BETWEEN PARTICLES

Observation: When sugar is added to water, the water level rises (mark B). After stirring and dissolving the sugar, the water level decreases slightly from mark B but remains above the original mark A.

Conclusion: The volume of the solution is less than the sum of the volumes of water and sugar separately. This shows that there are interparticle spaces between water particles, and the particles of the dissolved substance (sugar) occupy these spaces. This confirms that interparticle spaces exist between particles of matter.

Let's Revise! (Page 134) — Understanding

1. Define matter.

Answer: Matter is the material of which the universe is composed. Matter may be defined as something that occupies space, possesses mass, and offers resistance to any stress (force applied on it).

2. What is a substance?

Answer: A substance is a definite variety of matter, all samples of which have the same properties. For example, sugar is a substance because even the smallest particle of sugar is sweet.

3. How small are the particles of matter?

Answer: The particles of matter are extremely small — so small that they cannot be seen with the naked eye. This is evident from Activity 1, where a tiny crystal of potassium permanganate was able to colour a large volume of water uniformly. Even after repeated dilution (4–5 times), the water retained a faint purple colour, proving that each tiny crystal contains an enormous number of extremely small particles.

4. A substance X has definite volume, but no fixed shape and is almost incompressible. What is the physical state of substance X?

Answer: Substance X is in the liquid state. Liquids have a definite volume but no fixed shape (they take the shape of the container). Liquids are also almost incompressible because the intermolecular forces are strong enough to keep the particles together, though the interparticle space is slightly more than in solids.

5. In which state of matter is the interparticle force of attraction minimum?

Answer: The interparticle force of attraction is minimum in the gaseous state. In gases, the particles are far apart from each other and the interparticle force is negligible (extremely weak), allowing the particles to move freely in any direction.

6. Arrange the following in increasing order of intermolecular force of attraction — water, common salt, air.

Answer: Air < Water < Common salt
- Air (gas) has the weakest intermolecular force.
- Water (liquid) has moderately strong intermolecular forces.
- Common salt (solid) has the strongest intermolecular forces.

Let's Revise! (Page 139) — Understanding

1. What is meant by change of state?

Answer: Change of state is the change of a substance from one physical state to another. For example, conversion of ice (solid) into water (liquid) is a change of state from solid to liquid. Similarly, water (liquid) changing into steam (gas) is a change of state from liquid to gas.

2. How does temperature affect the movement of particles?

Answer: The particles of matter are constantly in motion and possess thermal energy. When the temperature rises, the thermal energy of the particles increases, causing them to move faster. For example, potassium permanganate spreads fastest in hot water, less quickly in water at room temperature, and slowest in ice-cold water. Thus, the movement of particles increases with a rise in temperature.

3. Define evaporation. Give an example.

Answer: Evaporation is the process in which a liquid changes into its vapour at temperatures below its boiling point. It is a slow and silent process that takes place only on the surface of the liquid.
Example: Drying of wet clothes in the Sun — the water in the clothes absorbs heat energy from sunlight and evaporates, causing the clothes to dry.

4. On what factors does the process of evaporation depend?

Answer: The rate of evaporation depends on the following factors:
1. Surface area of the liquid — a larger surface area increases the rate of evaporation.
2. Wind speed — higher wind speed increases evaporation.
3. Humidity — higher humidity decreases the rate of evaporation.
4. Temperature — higher temperature increases the rate of evaporation.

5. State one similarity and one difference between boiling and evaporation.

Answer:
- Similarity: Both boiling and evaporation involve the conversion of a liquid into vapour (gas).
- Difference: Boiling occurs at a fixed temperature (the boiling point) and is a bulk phenomenon (bubbles form throughout the liquid), whereas evaporation occurs at all temperatures below the boiling point and is a slow surface phenomenon.

The intermolecular force between the particles tends to bring them closer to each other, whereas motion of the particles tends to push them away from each other. Thus, the physical state of any matter depends the net effect of these two factors.

* When the intermolecular force between the particles is negligible but they are able to move very fast, the matter exists as a gas.
* When the intermolecular force between the particles is moderately strong and the particles are able to move to and fro, the matter exists as a liquid.

The forces operating between the constituent particles (atoms or molecules), in any form of matter are called intermolecular forces.

CHANGE OF STATE

Matter can exist in three physical states, viz., solid, liquid and vapour (or gaseous) states.

For example, water can exist in three different physical states : solid (ice), liquid (water), and gas (water vapour).

Change of a substance from one physical state to another is called change of state.

For example, conversion of ice into liquid water is a change of state (from solid to liquid).

The physical state of any substance at room temperature is considered its common form. For example, the common form of water is liquid.

Changing the Physical State from Solid to Liquid by Changing Temperature

It is our common experience that on heating ice gets converted to liquid water and the liquid water on further heating gets converted into steam.

On the other hand, water vapour when cooled gives water (liquid), which on further cooling gives ice (solid).

The transformation of various physical states of water may be described as follows :

$$ \text{Ice} \underset{\text{cool}}{\stackrel{\text{heat}}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Water} \underset{\text{cool}}{\stackrel{\text{heat}}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Water vapour (steam)} $$

Explanation of the Change of States

In solids, the intermolecular forces are very strong, and the particles are closely packed. On heating, the thermal (heat) energy of the particles increases and they are set in motion.

At a stage, it becomes sufficient to overcome the binding forces in the solids. At this stage, solid starts melting and the solid gets converted into liquid.

On further heating, the particles in liquid gain more energy. At a stage, the energy of the particles becomes so large that the particles are able to free themselves, and the liquid starts changing into vapour (the gaseous state).

In gases, the particles are far away from each other, and the particles do not exert any attractive/repulsive force on each other.
