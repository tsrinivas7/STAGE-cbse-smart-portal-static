Chapter Summary

Particulate Nature of Matter

Matter — Anything that occupies space, possesses mass and offers resistance to any stress.
Substance — A definite variety of matter, all samples of which have the same properties.
Bhutas — The five basic elements in Indian philosophy (akasa, vayu, tejas, ap, kshiti).
Maharishi Kanada — The great Indian sage who suggested that all forms of matter are composed of very small particles.
Anu — The name given by Maharishi Kanada to the tiny particles of matter.
Parmanu — The name given by Maharishi Kanada to particles made of two or more still smaller particles.

  • All objects, living and non-living, have mass, occupy space, and can be felt through sense organs.
  • Science interprets nature in terms of energy and matter.
  • Matter is the material of which the universe is composed.
  • Ancient Indian philosophers believed matter was made of five elements called panch tatva.
  • Maharishi Kanada proposed that matter is made of tiny particles called anu and parmanu.

What is Meant by the Particulate Nature of Matter ?

Constituent particle — The basic unit that makes up a larger piece of material.

  • Matter is made up of very small (or tiny) particles.
  • These particles are the building blocks of the material.
  • Even when a solid like chalk is ground into powder, it eventually reaches a stage where particles cannot be broken down further.
  • These tiny units are the constituent particles of the material.
  • This concept explains that matter is not continuous but particulate.

What is Matter Composed of ?

  • Matter consists of small particles that can spread and mix.
  • Experiments show that sugar, potassium permanganate, and ink spread uniformly in water.
  • Fragrance from agarbattis spreads uniformly in the air.
  • These particles get into the 'spaces' between the particles of water or air.
  • These spaces between the particles are called interparticle spaces.

States of Matter

Solid — A state where intermolecular force is very strong and particles move slowly.
Liquid — A state where intermolecular force is moderately strong and particles move to and fro.
Gas — A state where intermolecular force is negligible and particles move very fast.
Intermolecular force — The force of attraction between the particles in any matter.
Interparticle attraction — Another name for intermolecular force.

  • Matter can exist in three states: solid, liquid, and gas.
  • Water exists as ice (solid), water (liquid), and steam (gas).
  • The physical state depends on the net effect of intermolecular force and particle motion.
  • Thermal energy of particles increases with a rise in temperature.
  • Particles in any matter are constantly in motion and possess thermal energy.

How does the strength of intermolecular forces vary in solids, liquids and gases ?

Rigid — Solids are rigid because particles are closely packed.
Incompressible — Solids cannot be compressed because there is virtually no space between particles.
Definite shape — Solids have a definite shape due to strong intermolecular forces.
Definite volume — Solids have a definite volume.
Intermolecular forces — The forces operating between constituent particles in any form of matter.
Melting point — The minimum temperature at which a solid melts to become a liquid.

  • In solids, intermolecular forces are very strong, and particles are closely packed.
  • Solids are incompressible and have high density.
  • Particles in solids can rotate and/or vibrate about their mean positions.
  • Solids have a high melting point due to strong intermolecular forces.
  • Solids do not flow and maintain their shape.

Liquid State

No fixed — Liquids have no fixed shape.
Definite — Liquids have a definite volume.
Intermolecular forces — In liquids, these are weaker than those in solids.
Higher level to lower level — Liquids flow from higher level to lower level.

  • Liquids have no fixed shape and take the shape of the container.
  • Liquids have a definite volume.
  • Intermolecular forces in liquids are weaker than in solids.
  • Liquids are slightly more compressible than solids.
  • Liquids can flow because constituent particles can slip and slide past each other.

Gaseous State

Interparticle force — In gases, this force is negligible.
Negligible — Extremely weak; in gases, particles are free to move.
No definite shape — Gases do not have a fixed shape.
Fixed volume — Gases do not have a fixed volume.
Highly compressible — Gases can be compressed into a small volume.

  • In gases, constituent particles are far away from each other.
  • Gases have no definite shape and fixed volume.
  • Gases fill the entire space available to them.
  • Gases are highly compressible due to large spaces between particles.
  • Examples include LPG and CNG cylinders.

Fluids

Fluids — Both liquids and gases are classified as fluids because they flow and do not retain a fixed shape.

  • Liquids and gases can flow.
  • They do not retain a fixed shape.
  • They acquire the shape of the vessel they are in.
  • This property distinguishes them from solids.
  • Fluids allow particles to move freely.

How Does the Interparticle Spacing Differ in the Three States of Matter ?

Interparticle spaces — The spaces between the particles of water or air.

  • Interparticle space exists between particles in all states of matter.
  • In solids, space is least; in liquids, more; in gases, most.
  • When sugar dissolves in water, it occupies the spaces between water particles.
  • The total volume of the solution is less than the sum of the volume of water and sugar.
  • This proves that particles are not packed tightly without any space.

How Particles Move in Different States of Matter ?

Diffusion — The intermixing of particles of two or more substances on their own.

  • Particles are constantly in motion.
  • Movement of particles increases when heat is provided.
  • Potassium permanganate spreads fastest in hot water.
  • Diffusion in gases is very fast, slower in liquids, and almost negligible in solids.
  • Particles of air hit fragrance particles and help them spread.

Distinguishing Properties of Solids, Liquids, and Gases

  • Solids have particles packed in definite patterns with definite volume and shape.
  • Liquids have particles packed loosely with definite volume but no fixed shape.
  • Gases have particles packed very poorly with no definite shape or volume.
  • Compressibility is negligible in solids, slightly more in liquids, and high in gases.
  • Rigidity is highest in solids, less in liquids, and none in gases.

Change of State

Change of state — Change of a substance from one physical state to another.
Common form — The physical state of any substance at room temperature.

  • Matter can exist in solid, liquid, and vapour states.
  • Water exists as ice, water, and steam.
  • Heating ice converts it to water, and further heating converts it to steam.
  • Cooling steam gives water, and further cooling gives ice.
  • The common form of water is liquid.

Melting Point and Freezing Point

Melting — The process in which a solid changes into a liquid on heating.
Melting point — The temperature at which a solid substance gets converted into a liquid.
Freezing — The process in which a liquid substance on cooling changes into solid.
Freezing point — The temperature at which a liquid gets converted into a solid.

  • Melting involves overcoming binding forces in solids.
  • Solids with strong interparticle forces have high melting points.
  • Ice melts at 0 °C, Urea at 133 °C, and Iron at 1538 °C.
  • Freezing is the reverse process of melting.
  • The temperature remains constant during the change of state.

Boiling Point

Boiling — Rapid formation and breaking of bubbles in the bulk of a liquid being heated.
Boiling point — The fixed temperature at which a liquid boils and turns to vapour at atmospheric pressure.
Evaporation — The process in which a liquid changes into its vapour at temperatures below its boiling point.

  • Boiling is a bulk phenomenon where particles gain enough energy to escape.
  • Evaporation occurs only on the surface of the liquid.
  • Evaporation is a slow and silent process.
  • The rate of evaporation depends on surface area, wind speed, humidity, and temperature.
  • During evaporation, the liquid absorbs heat from the surroundings.

Activities

Activity 1: Multidisciplinary Approach

Objective: To show that matter has particulate nature.
Procedure:
1. Beaker A: Added 10–15 g of sugar to 50 mL of water. Stirred to dissolve. Observed that the sugar disappeared, the solution tasted sweet, and the water level remained the same.
2. Beaker B: Placed a small crystal of potassium permanganate in water. Observed the purple colour spreading uniformly into the water over a few hours.
3. Beaker C: Poured a drop of blue ink on water. Observed the colour spreading uniformly.
4. Agarbatti: Lit incense sticks and smelled the fragrance in another room.
Conclusion: Sugar, potassium permanganate, and ink get spread uniformly in water. The fragrance spreads in air. This shows that matter consists of small particles that get into the 'spaces' between the particles of water or air.

Activity 2: Multidisciplinary Approach

Objective: To show that liquids have a definite volume but no fixed shape.
Procedure:
1. Marked 200 mL level in three containers (A, B, C) of different shapes.
2. Filled Container A with water up to the mark.
3. Poured water from A to B, then B to C, observing the level and shape each time.
Observation: Water takes the shape of the container into which it is poured.
Conclusion: Liquids do not have a fixed shape. Liquids have a definite volume because the water level remained at 200 mL in all containers.

Activity 3: Multidisciplinary Approach

Objective: To show that gases do not have a fixed volume.
Procedure:
1. Created smoke by burning an incense stick and trapped it in Gas Jar A.
2. Covered Jar A with a glass plate.
3. Placed Gas Jar B upside down over Jar A and removed the glass plate slowly.
Observation: Smoke spread inside Gas Jar B and filled the entire space in it.
Conclusion: Gas particles move freely in all directions with negligible interparticle attraction. Gases neither have a fixed shape nor volume; they acquire the shape of the vessel.

Activity 4: Multidisciplinary Approach

Objective: To show that interparticle space exists between particles.
Procedure:
1. Marked water level A in a glass vessel.
2. Added two teaspoons of sugar and marked the new level B.
3. Dissolved the sugar by stirring.
4. Observed the final water level.
Observation: The water level increased initially but decreased to some extent after dissolution (level C is lower than B but higher than A).
Conclusion: The volume of the solution is less than the sum of the volume of water and sugar. This shows there is space between water particles which is occupied by the particles of the dissolved substance.

Let's Revise! (Page 134)

1. Define matter.
Answer: Matter is the material of which the universe is composed. It is defined as something that occupies space, possesses mass, and offers resistance to any stress (force applied on it).

2. What is a substance?
Answer: A definite variety of matter, all samples of which have the same properties, is commonly called a substance. For example, sugar is a substance because even the smallest particle of sugar is sweet.

3. How small are the particles of matter?
Answer: The particles of matter are very small (or tiny). Maharishi Kanada suggested that all forms of matter are composed of very small particles called anu, which may be made of even smaller particles called parmanu.

4. A substance X has definite volume, but no fixed shape and is almost incompressible. What is the physical state of substance X?
Answer: The physical state of substance X is Liquid. Liquids have a definite volume but no fixed shape (they take the shape of the container) and are slightly more compressible than solids (almost incompressible).

5. In which state of matter is the interparticle force of attraction minimum?
Answer: The interparticle force of attraction is minimum in the Gaseous state. In gases, the intermolecular forces are negligible (extremely weak).

6. Arrange the following in increasing order of intermolecular force of attraction—water, common salt, air.
Answer: Air < Water < Common Salt.
* Air (Gas): Negligible intermolecular force.
* Water (Liquid): Moderately strong intermolecular force.
* Common Salt (Solid): Very strong intermolecular force.

Let's Revise! (Page 139)

1. What is meant by change of state?
Answer: Change of a substance from one physical state to another is called change of state. For example, conversion of ice into liquid water is a change of state (from solid to liquid).

2. How does temperature affect the movement of particles?
Answer: The movement of particles increases when heat is provided. Thermal energy of particles increases with a rise in temperature. For example, potassium permanganate spreads fastest in hot water, less quickly in room temperature water, and slowest in ice-cold water.

3. Define evaporation. Give an example.
Answer: The process in which a liquid changes into its vapour at temperatures below its boiling point is called evaporation.
* Example: During the drying of clothes in the Sun, water absorbs heat energy and evaporates. Spilled water disappearing after some time is also due to evaporation.

4. On what factors does the process of evaporation depend?
Answer: The rate of evaporation depends on:
1. Surface area of the liquid.
2. Wind speed.
3. Humidity.
4. Temperature.

5. State one similarity and one difference between boiling and evaporation.
Answer:
* Similarity: Both are processes where a liquid changes into vapour.
* Difference: Boiling occurs at a fixed temperature (boiling point) throughout the bulk of the liquid, whereas evaporation occurs at any temperature below the boiling point and only on the surface of the liquid.

Section II: Logic-Based Questions (Page 145)

1. Three vessels are filled with water. The first vessel has shape of a bottle, second vessel resembles a cup and the third vessel looks like a flat tray. In which of the vessels will the rate of evaporation be highest? Explain.
Answer: The rate of evaporation will be highest in the flat tray.
Reason: Evaporation depends on the surface area of the liquid. The flat tray has the largest surface area compared to the bottle and cup, allowing more particles to escape from the surface.

2. If sugar is dissolved in water, the level of water does not rise. Why?
Answer: This happens because the particles of sugar occupy the interparticle spaces between the water particles. Thus, the total volume does not increase significantly.

3. A drop of ink is placed in still water and it spreads evenly throughout. What does this prove?
Answer: This proves that particles of matter are very small and are in constant motion (diffusion). The ink particles move and mix with water particles.

4. If you press a balloon gently, it gets compressed. What does this indicate about the nature of matter?
Answer: It indicates that gases have large interparticle spaces. The particles can be pushed closer together, reducing the volume.

5. A solid block of salt is ground into fine powder. Does it still remain the same substance? Why?
Answer: Yes, it still remains the same substance. Grinding is a physical change that only changes the size and shape, not the chemical composition of the salt.

6. If two objects are made of the same material but have different shapes, do they have the same properties? Why or why not?
Answer: Yes, they have the same intrinsic properties (like density, melting point, chemical nature) because they are the same substance. However, external properties like shape and volume may differ.

7. Why can gases be compressed easily but not solids?
Answer: Gases can be compressed easily because their particles are far apart with large interparticle spaces. Solids cannot be compressed because their particles are closely packed with negligible interparticle spaces.

STATES OF MATTER

When we look around various forms of matter, we find that matter can exist in three states. These are :
* Solid state
* Gaseous state
* Liquid state

For example, water can exist as ice (solid state), water (liquid state) and steam (gaseous state).

What Describes Different States of Matter

What makes the matter to exist in any one of the three states ?
Why are the properties of solids, liquids, and gases different ?

Before we find an answer for these questions, let us recollect that,
* All matter is made up of very small particles.
* The particles in any matter are constantly in motion. Thus, they possess thermal energy.
* Thermal energy of these particles increases with a rise in temperature.

[IMAGE_START] A photograph of a small waterfall or stream flowing over mossy rocks. The water is white and turbulent as it moves downwards. Caption below reads: "Flowing water (liquid)" [IMAGE_END]

[IMAGE_START] A photograph of three clear, square ice cubes sitting on a dark, reflective surface. Caption below reads: "Ice cubes (solid)" [IMAGE_END]

The particles in any matter attract each other. This force of attraction decreases as the distance between the particles increases.

The force of attraction between the particles in any matter is called intermolecular force or interparticle attraction.

[IMAGE_START] A photograph showing a red pot on a stove burner. Steam is rising from the pot. A hand is visible near the pot, possibly holding a utensil. Caption below reads: "Steam (gas) coming out from a pan" [IMAGE_END]

The intermolecular force between the particles tends to bring them closer to each other, whereas motion of the particles tends to push them away from each other. Thus, the physical state of any matter depends the net effect of these two factors.

Thus,
* When the intermolecular force between the particles is very strong and the particles move slowly, the matter exists as a solid.
* When the intermolecular force between the particles is negligible but they are able to move very fast, the matter exists as a gas.
* When the intermolecular force between the particles is moderately strong and the particles are able to move to and fro, the matter exists as a liquid.

How does the strength of intermolecular forces vary in solids, liquids and gases ?

The forces operating between the constituent particles (atoms or molecules), in any form of matter are called intermolecular forces.

SCIENCE :: 8

  • In solids, the intermolecular forces are very strong, and the particles are closely packed. That is why, solids are incompressible and have a definite shape and high density.
  • In liquids, the intermolecular forces are strong enough to keep the particles tied up to each other but not strong enough to keep them in fixed positions. This is why, liquids can flow and do not have a definite shape.
  • In gases, the intermolecular forces are negligible (extremely weak), and the particles are free to move. As a result, gases can occupy any space available to them.

Solid State

All solids show the following characteristics :

  • Solids are rigid and incompressible. Because in solids, the particles are closely packed and there is virtually no space between the particles. So, solids cannot be compressed.

[IMAGE_START] A diagram showing the arrangement of particles in a solid. It depicts numerous green spheres packed tightly together in a regular, grid-like pattern, representing a fixed structure. The caption below reads "Arrangement of particles in a solid". [IMAGE_END]

A sponge can be compressed easily because it has small pores in it. Air is trapped between these pores. When a sponge is pressed, air is expelled out and it gets compressed.

  • A solid has a definite shape and volume. Because in solids, the particles are held together by a strong intermolecular force. For example, sugar and salt are solids because the shape of each individual crystal remains unchanged.
  • In solids, intermolecular forces are strong. The particles are held closely in fixed positions due to a strong force of attraction. These particles, however, can rotate and/or vibrate about their mean positions.
  • Solids have high melting point due to strong intermolecular forces. The minimum temperature at which a solid melts to become a liquid is called its melting point.

Liquid State

Liquids show the following general properties.

  • Liquids have no fixed (or definite) shape. Liquids take the shape of the container they are poured into, because in liquids, the constituent particles can move, over short distances, slip and slide past each other and readjust their positions.

[IMAGE_START] A diagram showing the arrangement of particles in a liquid. It depicts green spheres clustered together but not in a regular pattern, with some space between them, representing a fluid structure. The caption below reads "Arrangement of particles in a liquid". [IMAGE_END]

  • A liquid has a definite volume. In liquids, the interparticles forces are strong enough to keep the constituent particles together.
  • Intermolecular forces in liquids are weaker than those in solids. It is due to this that intermolecular space in liquids is greater than that in solids.

CHANGE OF STATE

Matter can exist in three physical states, viz., solid, liquid and vapour (or gaseous) states.

For example, water can exist in three different physical states : solid (ice), liquid (water), and gas (water vapour).

Change of a substance from one physical state to another is called change of state.

For example, conversion of ice into liquid water is a change of state (from solid to liquid).

The physical state of any substance at room temperature is considered its common form. For example, the common form of water is liquid.

Changing the Physical State from Solid to Liquid by Changing Temperature

It is our common experience that on heating ice gets converted to liquid water and the liquid water on further heating gets converted into steam.

On the other hand, water vapour when cooled gives water (liquid), which on further cooling gives ice (solid).

The transformation of various physical states of water may be described as follows :

$$ \text{Ice} \underset{\text{cool}}{\stackrel{\text{heat}}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Water} \underset{\text{cool}}{\stackrel{\text{heat}}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Water vapour (steam)} $$

Explanation of the Change of States

In solids, the intermolecular forces are very strong, and the particles are closely packed. On heating, the thermal (heat) energy of the particles increases and they are set in motion.

At a stage, it becomes sufficient to overcome the binding forces in the solids. At this stage, solid starts melting and the solid gets converted into liquid.

On further heating, the particles in liquid gain more energy. At a stage, the energy of the particles becomes so large that the particles are able to free themselves, and the liquid starts changing into vapour (the gaseous state).

In gases, the particles are far away from each other, and the particles do not exert any attractive/repulsive force on each other.

Melting Point and Freezing Point

The process in which a solid, on heating, changes into a liquid is called melting. For example, conversion of ice into water is the melting of ice.

[IMAGE_START] A simple reaction arrow diagram. Text: "Ice" followed by a rightward arrow with "melting" written above it, pointing to "Water". [IMAGE_END]

The temperature at which a solid substance gets converted into a liquid is called the melting point.

The process in which a liquid substance on cooling changes into solid is called freezing. For example, conversion of water to ice is called freezing (or solidification) of water.

[IMAGE_START] A simple reaction arrow diagram. Text: "Water" followed by a rightward arrow with "freezing" written above it, pointing to "Ice". [IMAGE_END]

The temperature at which a liquid gets converted into a solid is called its freezing point.

Solids with strong interparticle forces of attraction have high melting points whereas as solids with weak attractive forces have low melting points.

Boiling Point

Boiling is a bulk phenomenon. During boiling particles from the bulk of the liquid gain enough energy to get converted to vapour. Boiling of a liquid occurs at a fixed temperature depending on the nature of the liquid.

SCIENCE :: 8

The fixed temperature at which a liquid boils and to vapour at atmospheric pressure is called its boiling point.

For example, water on boiling gives steam.

Water $\xrightarrow{\text{boiling}}$ Steam

The particles start to move vigorously apart from each other reducing the interparticle forces of attraction. Eventually, the particles escape from liquid state and get converted into vapour or gaseous state.

The formation of vapour is very fast at the boiling point. It occurs on the surface as well as within the liquid. This process is observed as bubble formation in the liquid.

But we know that vapour formation occurs at all temperatures through the process of evaporation.

The process in which a liquid changes into its vapour at temperatures below its boiling point is called evaporation.

Thus, during evaporation, the liquid absorbs heat from the surroundings or from any other body in contact with it.

For example, during the drying of clothes in the Sun, the water in the clothes absorbs heat energy of the sunlight. Evaporation is the reason why spilled water disappears after sometime, at any temperature.

Evaporation is a slow and silent process that takes place only on the surface of the liquid. The rate of evaporation depends on the surface area of the liquid, wind speed, humidity and temperature.

Know More!

All matter is made up of tiny particles called atoms and molecules. For example, elements like iron and gold are made up of atoms of iron and gold respectively. But atoms of elements like hydrogen, oxygen and sulfur are not able to exist independently. So, atoms of the same element combine together to form a molecule. For example, two atoms of hydrogen combine to form a stable molecule of hydrogen. Water molecule is made up of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.
