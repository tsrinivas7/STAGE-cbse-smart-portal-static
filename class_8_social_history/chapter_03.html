SHIVAJI

  • Born in 1630 in hilly region of Pune, Maharashtra.
  • Father: Shahji Bhonsle — Brave military commander working for Deccan Sultanates.
  • Mother: Jijabai — Played important role shaping character; told stories from Ramayana and Mahabharata.
  • dharma — Righteousness; values Shivaji learned from mother.
  • dignity and self-respect — Dream of establishing a kingdom where people could live.
  • unique style of warfare — Developed due to knowledge of terrain; small groups attack suddenly and escape.
  • Swarajya — Idea of self-rule became guiding principle of his life.

Capture of Forts

  • Torna Fort — Captured in 1645 at age sixteen; true beginning of Maratha state.
  • Purandar — Fort taken over after Torna.
  • Rajgad — Fort taken over after Torna.
  • Kondana — Later renamed Sinhagad; taken over after Torna.
  • Forts acted as administrative centres storing food, weapons, and money.
  • Symbolised birth of Maratha power.

EXTENDED LEARNING

  • Maharashtra once had over 350 forts designed with unique geography.
  • Raigad had secret water tanks; Pratapgad blended into hills.
  • Quote: 'Forts are the ornaments of the land.'

Question: Discuss with your partner about how Shivaji protected his people, how Tarabai showed courage and how Ahilyabai showed kindness and care. Answer:
* Shivaji's Protection: Shivaji built strong forts on hilltops to protect his kingdom from invaders. He used guerrilla warfare to defend his people from larger armies like the Mughals. He ensured justice for ordinary farmers and traders.
* Tarabai's Courage: After her husband Rajaram died, Tarabai took charge of the kingdom. She led armies personally and used guerrilla tactics to fight against Aurangzeb, keeping the spirit of Swarajya alive during a crisis.

Imagine a time when India was divided into many small kingdoms, each struggling for power and survival. The Mughals ruled much of the north, while the Deccan was controlled by the Sultans. In the midst of these great empires, a new force began to rise from the rugged hills of Maharashtra—the Marathas. They were not born into wealth or privilege but were united by courage, determination and the dream of freedom. Their strength lay not only in the power of their swords but also in their deep love for their land, language and people. From simple farmers and soldiers, they transformed into fearless warriors who changed the course of Indian history.

At the heart of this transformation stood one extraordinary leader—Shivaji Maharaj. Guided by his mother's teachings and inspired by the spirit of self-rule or Swarājya, he built a kingdom based on justice, equality and independence. Through his intelligence, bravery and innovative strategies, he

created a strong foundation for what became one of India's most powerful empires. The story of the Marathas is not just about battles and conquests; it is about unity, courage and the will to stand tall against mighty empires. In this chapter, we will learn how the Marathas rose to power, defended their land and left behind a legacy that continues to inspire generations.

WHO WERE THE MARATHAS?

The Marathas were a community of warriors, farmers and administrators who lived in the Deccan plateau, mainly in present-day Maharashtra. They are closely associated with the Marathi language, which developed into a strong medium of literature and culture. Over time, the Marathas grew from being local chiefs and cultivators into a powerful political force that shaped the course of Indian history. Their rise was not only due to battles but also because of cultural revival, social unity and political organisation.

During the 13th century, much of Maharashtra was ruled by the Yadava dynasty, with Devagiri (modern Daulatabad) as its capital. However, in the early 14th century, the Yadavas were defeated by the Khilji Sultanate of Delhi. Later, different Deccan Sultanates controlled the region and the Maratha chiefs had to serve under them. Even during this period of political change, the people of Maharashtra preserved their unique language, customs and traditions, which gave them a strong cultural identity.

A major source of Maratha strength came from the Bhakti movement, which spread across India between the 7th and 17th centuries. Saints such as Dnyaneshwar, Namdev, Tukaram and Ramdas inspired people with devotional songs and simple poetry in Marathi, making spiritual teachings accessible to ordinary villagers. Dnyaneshwar translated the Bhagavad-Gita into Marathi, while Tukaram's abhangas (devotional hymns) moved people with their emphasis on faith, equality and humility. These saints also criticised social divisions and gave common people confidence in their own worth, preparing them to stand united against oppression. This cultural awakening laid the groundwork for later political unity. By the 17th century, some Maratha chiefs made attempts at independence, but they were not successful. However, the people were ready for a leader who could combine military skill, administrative wisdom and popular support. It was in this environment that Shivaji Maharaj rose to power. Inspired by the cultural traditions of Maharashtra and by the struggles of his time, he built a strong and disciplined state. His dream of Swaraja (self-rule) not only gave the Marathas political strength but also inspired later generations of Indians in their fight for freedom.

Capture of Forts

Shivaji's political and military career began at a very young age. In 1645, when he was just sixteen years old, he captured the Torna Fort near Pune. This was his first major achievement and is considered the true beginning of the Maratha state. After this success, Shivaji steadily expanded his control by taking over other forts such as Purandar, Rajgad and Kondana (later renamed Sinhagad). Each of these forts was very important because they were located on hilltops or near major routes, which made them easy to defend and useful for controlling trade and movement in the region.

Instead of always fighting large and costly battles, Shivaji often used clever negotiations, sudden attacks and surprise raids to capture forts. This showed his intelligence and ability to use strategy rather than relying only on strength. By slowly increasing the number of forts under his control, Shivaji laid the foundation for his dream of Swarajya (self-rule).

The forts were not only military strongholds but also acted as administrative centres. They stored food, weapons and money and served as safe shelters for soldiers and local people during times of war. From these forts, Shivaji could send out troops quickly, watch enemy movements and protect his people.

Maratha Navy

Shivaji realised very early that controlling the seas was just as important as controlling the land. The Konkan coast of Maharashtra was often attacked by foreign powers like the Portuguese, British and Dutch, as well as by the Siddis of Janjira, who had strong naval bases. To protect his people, trade routes and coastal towns, Shivaji became one of the first Indian rulers to build a proper naval force, which was rare in India at that time.

He constructed strong sea forts such as Sindhudurg, Vijaydurg and Suvarnadurg along the Konkan coast. These forts were equipped with cannons and secret escape routes, making them almost impossible to capture. Shivaji also built fleets of small but fast-moving ships that could move quickly, attack suddenly and disappear before enemy ships could respond. These ships helped in protecting fishermen, traders and local villages from pirate and enemy raids.

The Maratha navy not only protected the kingdom from foreign invasions but also gave Shivaji control

over maritime trade along the Arabian Sea. This made sure that goods like spices, textiles and salt, which were traded through sea routes, remained under Maratha protection and contributed to the kingdom's wealth.

Guerrilla Warfare

One of Shivaji's most brilliant innovations in warfare was the use of guerrilla tactics, which depended on speed, surprise and clever use of geography. At a time when most rulers fought long, open-field battles with large armies, Shivaji realised that the Marathas, with their smaller numbers, could not always win in such battles against the huge Mughal and Bijapur forces. Instead, he trained his soldiers to fight smartly.

In this method, soldiers would attack suddenly at night or in rough terrain, loot enemy camps, destroy supplies and then disappear quickly into the hills, forests or narrow passes of the Sahyadris before the enemy could respond. Since the Marathas knew the local terrain very well, they could move swiftly, hide easily and confuse larger armies. This made it almost impossible for the enemy to capture Shivaji or defeat him in battle.

Guerrilla warfare allowed small groups of Maratha soldiers to defeat much stronger armies, saving both time and resources. It gave the Marathas a strong advantage because their enemies could never predict when or where the next attack would happen. Shivaji's clever use of this strategy earned him the title of a master military strategist.

This method was so great that it became an inspiration for later generations. Even during India's struggle for freedom in the 19th and 20th centuries, freedom fighters used guerrilla-style surprise attacks against colonial powers. Thus, Shivaji's tactics were not only successful in his own time but also became a legacy for future struggles against oppression.

DID YOU KNOW?

The word 'guerrilla' was later used in Europe during the Napoleonic wars, long after Shivaji had already mastered such tactics. Historians credit him as one of the earliest rulers in Asia to formalise this hit-and-run method as a military doctrine.

Soon afterwards, the Mughal nobleman Shaista Khan invaded Shivaji's territories with a large army and occupied Pune for nearly three years. Finally, in 1663, with only a small group of soldiers, Shivaji carried out one of his most daring acts—a surprise night raid on Khan's camp. Shivaji and his men quietly entered his residence and launched an attack. Shaista Khan was badly injured, losing some of his fingers and barely managed to escape. Humiliated by this defeat, he left Maharashtra and was later transferred by Aurangzeb. This bold raid is often compared to a modern-day surgical strike because it was sudden, precise and highly effective.

In 1664, Shivaji turned towards Surat, one of the richest Mughal port cities in present-day Gujarat. Surat was a centre of trade and finance, filled with warehouses, merchants and officials loyal to the Mughals. Shivaji's army raided the city and seized enormous treasure worth nearly one crore rupees—a massive amount at that time. The wealth was wisely used to strengthen his forts, expand his army and build his navy. Shivaji was careful not to attack places of worship or harm ordinary people. He even spared the house of Mohandas Parekh, a respected charitable man. A few years later, he sacked Surat once again to secure more resources.

These events became so famous that reports appeared in the London Gazette, an English newspaper of the time. The paper mentioned how Shivaji wrote to European diplomatic agents in Surat, demanding 'immediate presents of money,' and warned that if they refused, he would 'return and ruin that city.'

These three encounters—the killing of Afzal Khan, the daring raid on Shaista Khan and the sacking of Surat—spread Shivaji's fame far beyond Maharashtra. People across India began to see him as a heroic figure who fought for Swarajya (self-rule) against powerful enemies. His actions not only gave confidence to his followers but also inspired respect and fear even among his rivals.

Treaty of Purandar and Agra Episode

The sacking of Surat in 1664 was a huge blow to the Mughal Empire, as Surat was one of its richest port cities and a centre of trade and wealth. Shivaji's daring raid greatly insulted the power and prestige of Emperor Aurangzeb. To defeat the growing Maratha power, Aurangzeb sent Raja Jai Singh of Amber, a distinguished Rajput general, with a massive and well-equipped army.

In 1665, Shivaji's forces, though brave and determined, were not strong enough to face such a large Mughal army in open battle. To protect his people and avoid unnecessary destruction, Shivaji was forced to accept defeat at Purandar Fort near Pune and signed the Treaty of Purandar. According to this treaty, Shivaji had to hand over 23 forts and a large-part of his territory to the Mughals. He was, however, allowed to retain control over 12 forts and some adjoining lands. As part of the terms, his son Sambhaji had to enter Mughal service as a mark of loyalty. Although this seemed like a setback, Shivaji used it as a temporary compromise to safeguard the core of his Swarajya (self-rule) and to prepare for future opportunities.

Jai Singh later persuaded Shivaji to visit the Mughal court at Agra in 1666. However, instead of being treated with honour, Shivaji was insulted when Aurangzeb placed him behind lesser nobles and even a Mughal general whom he had defeated earlier. Angered by this humiliation, Shivaji stormed out of the court. Soon after, Aurangzeb placed him under strict house arrest, with guards watching his every move.

Coronation at Raigad (1674)

After years of constant battles, clever strategies and building a strong administration, Shivaji felt it was time to formally declare himself a sovereign and independent ruler. Until then, he was often considered just a local chief or rebel by larger powers like the Mughals and the Deccan Sultanates. To change this image and give his Swarajya (self-rule) greater recognition, Shivaji organised a grand coronation ceremony at the strong mountain fortress of Raigad in June 1674.

In this magnificent ceremony, Shivaji was crowned with full Vedic rites and crowned as Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj, the protector and sovereign of the Marathas. His formal royal title was 'Shri Raja Shiva Chhatrapati' and like some past rulers, he even started his own era, the Rājyābhiṣeka shaka (coronation era). The rituals followed traditional Hindu practices, with priests from different regions invited to perform sacred rites. This was very significant because it revived the idea of Hindu kingship based on dharma (righteousness) at a time when most of India was ruled by the Mughals or the Sultanates. The coronation thus gave a strong cultural and religious identity to the Maratha kingdom.

Southern Campaign (Dakshina Digvijaya)

In the last phase of his life, Shivaji turned his attention towards the southern regions of India in what is known as the Dakshina Digvijaya or the Southern Conquest. Until then, Maratha influence was mainly limited to Maharashtra and nearby areas, but Shivaji realised that controlling the 'southern territories would give the Marathas both wealth and strategic strength. He began capturing important forts, towns and strongholds in present-day Karnataka and Tamil Nadu. These regions were rich in fertile land, resources and trade routes, which added to the prosperity of the Maratha kingdom.

Through his campaigns, Shivaji extended Maratha influence far beyond Maharashtra, making the Marathas a dominant power in peninsular India. His armies moved swiftly, defeating local rulers, securing key forts and bringing new areas under Maratha control. These victories not only expanded the size of the empire but also improved its economic base by adding new sources of revenue and trade.

Shivaji also showed his sense of justice and humanity during these campaigns. On the southern coast, he strongly opposed the European practice of slave trading, where people were captured and sold as slaves by foreign traders. By taking a stand against this cruel activity, Shivaji proved that his rule was not just about power and expansion but also about protecting the dignity and rights of people.

The southern campaign was highly significant because it prepared the ground for future Maratha expansion. After Shivaji's death, his successors like Sambhaji and later the Peshwas continued this policy, spreading Maratha power even further into southern and northern India. Thus, Shivaji's Dakshina Digvijaya became the starting point for the transformation of the Marathas into one of the strongest empires in 18th-century India.

Shivaji's Legacy and Death

Shivaji Maharaj is remembered in Indian history as a visionary leader, brilliant strategist and true nation-builder. He was not only a skilled warrior but also a wise administrator who cared deeply about the welfare of his people. By creating a strong civil administration, a disciplined army and even a navy, he laid the foundation of the Maratha Empire, which continued to remain powerful long after his death. His policies of religious tolerance, justice and protection of ordinary people made him a beloved ruler among Hindus, Muslims and other communities living under his rule.

Shivaji's courage and innovative military methods such as guerrilla warfare and fort-based defence inspired his contemporaries and earned him respect even from his enemies. More importantly, he became a symbol of resistance against foreign domination. Generations after him, Indian leaders and freedom fighters looked up to Shivaji as an example of bravery, determination and love for Swarajya (self-rule).

THE MARATHAS AFTER SHIVAJI

Shivaji's death in 1680 marked a major turning point in the history of the Marathas. The great leader who had laid the foundation of Swarajya (self-rule) was no more, but the ideals of freedom, justice and independence that he had inspired did not disappear. His strong administration, network of forts, disciplined army and vision of a people's kingdom gave the Marathas the strength to continue. Even though they faced immense challenges from the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, who came to the Deccan with a huge army, the Marathas resisted bravely. They relied on the guerrilla tactics that Shivaji had perfected and used the rugged geography of the Deccan to their advantage. This period showed how deeply Shivaji's ideas had taken root among his followers, who fought not just for a ruler but for the dream of self-rule.

In the years after Shivaji, the Marathas went through many ups and downs but remained one of the strongest powers in India for almost 150 years. Leaders like Sambhaji, Rajaram and Tarabai kept the struggle alive against Aurangzeb, even in the face of defeat and hardship. The Marathas after Shivaji proved that his vision of Swarajya was strong enough to outlive him and continued to inspire generations to fight for freedom and justice.

RISE OF THE PESHWAS

(Handwritten note: They are Chitnavis?)

After Shivaji's death and the weakening of direct royal authority, the Maratha kingdom gradually changed its structure. The centralised state of Shivaji's time gave way to a more decentralised confederacy, where different chiefs and powerful families controlled separate regions but still remained loyal to the larger Maratha banner. The Chhatrapati continued as the symbolic head of the state, but the real authority slowly shifted to the Peshwas (a Persian term meaning 'prime minister'). Originally appointed as chief ministers to assist the king, the Peshwas eventually rose to hold more power than the Chhatrapati himself. Their capital was established at Pune, which became the centre of Maratha politics and administration.

The Peshwas played a crucial role in providing organisation, effective leadership and military strategy. From Pune, they not only managed civil administration but also directed extensive campaigns that expanded Maratha power far beyond Maharashtra. They preserved Shivaji's spirit of Swarajya (self-rule) while transforming the Maratha state into a powerful empire. Under their leadership, the Marathas became the most decisive political force of 18th-century India, often influencing the Mughal court in Delhi and shaping the destiny of the subcontinent.

Expansion under the Peshwas

The period of the Peshwas marked the peak of Maratha power and influence. The most famous among them was Bajirao I (1720–1740), remembered as a brilliant cavalry commander and strategist. He believed that the declining Mughal Empire could be replaced by Maratha dominance. His bold statement, 'Let the Mughal-court bow to the Marathas at Delhi,' reflected his vision of Maratha supremacy in northern India. Bajirao's rapid cavalry raids, quick movements and fearless leadership brought large territories under Maratha control. His son, Nana Saheb Peshwa continued this vision by consolidating Maratha power and strengthening administration. Together, they were instrumental in transforming the Marathas from a regional kingdom into a pan-Indian empire.

Powerful Maratha generals like Mahadji Shinde (Scindia), the Holkars and the Gaekwads expanded Maratha influence across northern and western India. Their armies marched as far as Delhi, Lahore, Attock and even Peshawar, proving the wide reach of Maratha authority. By the middle of the 18th century, the Marathas had become the most powerful political and military force in India after the decline of the Mughal Empire. However, as regional chiefs gained more power and autonomy, discipline sometimes weakened and the values of justice and restraint taught by Shivaji were not always followed. For example, the Marathas' ten-year campaign in Bengal caused much suffering and devastation to ordinary people, showing the challenges of managing such a vast empire.

The Afghan ruler Ahmad Shah Abdali invaded India and the Marathas marched north to stop him. Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1818)

By the latter half of the 18th century, the chief rivals of the British in India were the Marathas. At this time, the Mughal Empire had declined and the Marathas were still a powerful force, though divided among several chiefs like the Scindias, Holkars, Bhonsles and Gaekwads. The British East India Company, with its growing strength, modern weapons and superior organisation, saw the Marathas as the biggest obstacle to their expansion in India. A series of three wars, known as the Anglo-Maratha Wars, were fought between 1775 and 1818. These were ultimately ended Maratha political independence.

  • First Anglo-Maratha War (1775–1782): This war began due to the succession disputes within the Maratha leadership. The British tried to take advantage of these internal rivalries. However, the Marathas fought bravely and the war ended with the Treaty of Salbai, which brought temporary peace and left the Marathas still strong.
  • Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803–1805): In this phase, the British defeated some of the most powerful Maratha chiefs, including the Scindias and Bhonsles. The Marathas lost key territories, which greatly reduced their strength and influence in India. The war highlighted the superior military organisation and modern technology of the British compared to the Marathas' divided forces.
  • Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–1818): This was the final and most decisive conflict. By now, the Marathas were weakened by internal disunity and constant rivalries among their chiefs. The British launched well-planned campaigns with their modern army, completely defeating and crushing Maratha resistance. The Peshwa, who was the nominal head of the Maratha confederacy, was forced to surrender and was given a pension.

By 1818, almost all Maratha territories were annexed by the British, marking the end of Maratha political power in India. In reality, the British gained control of India more from the Marathas than from the Mughals or any other power, since the Marathas were the last major Indian force resisting them.

Yet, the Maratha legacy did not die. Their bravery, resistance and love for Swarajya (self-rule) inspired later generations of Indians. Many 19th- and 20th-century freedom fighters drew inspiration from the Marathas' long struggle against the British, keeping their spirit alive in India's independence movement.

MARATHA ADMINISTRATION

Civil Administration

Shivaji set up a strong and centralised civil administration that reduced the excessive power of old feudal nobles and replaced it with a fair system based on merit and responsibility. Instead of granting land as jagirs (hereditary rights that often led to corruption), he ensured his officers were paid fixed salaries from the state treasury. This made them loyal to the government rather than to powerful families. To avoid misuse of power, officials were also transferred regularly. The welfare of soldiers and their families was given importance—for instance, if a soldier died in battle, his widow received a pension and sometimes his son was given a government job, which was a rare practice in those times. To govern effectively, Shivaji created the ashta pradhana mandala (council of eight ministers). Each minister had a specific duty, such as handling finances, justice, military affairs, foreign relations or religious matters. This division of work made the administration systematic, efficient and fair, ensuring that power was not concentrated in one hand.

Revenue Administration

A kingdom cannot run without revenue and Shivaji introduced a well-planned and fair taxation system. Land was measured carefully and revenue was assessed based on the type of soil and crops that could be grown, making the process more accurate and just. Farmers felt secure because they knew how much tax they had to pay and were protected from exploitation by middlemen. Two special taxes were also introduced:

  • Chauth: A one-fourth (25%) tribute collected from areas not directly ruled by the Marathas, in return for military protection.
  • Sardeshmukhi: An additional 10% tax claimed as the right of the Marathas for being the traditional chiefs or overlords of Maharashtra.

These taxes allowed the Marathas to expand their influence without directly ruling every region. Detailed record-keeping was also maintained so that no farmer was unfairly burdened. This revenue system not only gave the state a steady income but also built trust between the people and the Maratha rulers.

Military Administration

The Maratha army became one of the strongest and most feared forces in India because of its discipline, speed and organisation. It was divided into three wings: infantry (foot soldiers), cavalry (horsemen) and navy. The cavalry was the backbone of the army and was divided into two categories:

  • Bargirs: Soldiers provided with horses, arms and salaries by the state.
  • Shiledars: Soldiers who maintained their own horses and weapons but fought under state command.

This system balanced government control with private contribution. The Marathas used both traditional weapons like swords, spears and bows as well as firearms and cannons. Remarkably, they even experimented with rockets, showing their innovative spirit. They used guerrilla tactics—striking the enemy suddenly and escaping into hills or forests—which gave them an advantage over much larger Mughal armies. This made their military both flexible and effective.

Forts

more than 450 forts

Forts were the lifeline of Maratha power and defence. Built on mountains, near rivers and along the coast, they provided security, control over trade routes and protection from enemy invasions. Forts like Raigad, Pratapgad and Sindhudurg became strongholds of Maratha power. These forts had strong stone walls, secret passages and clever designs to make them difficult to capture. They were not just military bases but also administrative centres, food and weapon storehouses and safe shelters for people during attacks. Ramchandrapant Amatya, a senior Maratha minister, famously said that without forts, the kingdom would not survive—showing how central they were to Maratha strategy.

Navy

Shivaji was one of the first Indian rulers to understand the importance of a navy in defending the kingdom. He built a strong fleet to guard the Konkan coast, prevent piracy and control sea trade. Under the command of Kanhoji Angre, the Maratha navy grew powerful enough that even European powers like the Portuguese and the British had to recognise Maratha authority at sea. The Marathas demanded passes from foreign ships entering their waters, showing their dominance. Although their ships were smaller and less advanced than European ones, their knowledge of the coastline and swift tactics gave them an advantage. For many years, they maintained supremacy in the Arabian Sea, protecting trade and strengthening Maratha power.

Justice

Shivaji gave great importance to fair justice for ordinary people. Small disputes were settled by panchayats, which were councils of respected village elders. This made justice quick, cheap and fair at the local level. In towns, kotwals (police officials) maintained law and order, while more serious cases went to higher authorities. Harsh punishments like the death penalty were rare; instead, punishments were moderate and based on fairness. People also had the right to appeal to higher officials if they felt wronged. Shivaji also instructed his officers to protect people from exploitation and not to harm farmers, women or ordinary citizens during wars. This created a sense of trust between the people and the Maratha rulers.

Trade and Economy

The Marathas understood that a strong economy was as important as a strong army. To improve trade, they built and maintained roads, river transport, ferries and bridges, which made the movement of goods and people easier and safer. Well-connected routes linked villages, towns and forts, helping farmers sell their crops and traders carry merchandise without too many delays. This improved communication also supported military needs, as soldiers and supplies could be moved quickly across regions.

The Marathas also engaged in maritime trade by developing coastal ports and building a navy. Important ports such as Mocha, Muscat and Malacca became centres of overseas trade. Through these ports, Indian goods like cotton textiles, spices and food grains were exchanged for horses, weapons and luxury items from other countries. This international trade not only brought wealth and prosperity but also increased the Marathas' political importance.

By encouraging both inland and overseas trade, the Marathas ensured that farmers, artisans and merchants benefitted. This wealth supported their administration and army, helping them expand their power. The focus on trade and economy shows that the Marathas were not just warriors but also skilled organisers who cared about the prosperity of their people.

She was deeply religious and is remembered for rebuilding many temples destroyed during earlier invasions. Most famously, she rebuilt the Kashi Vishwanath temple in Varanasi and the Somnath temple in Gujarat, restoring their cultural and spiritual importance. She also undertook large public works by constructing wells, ghats, roads and rest houses across India—from Kedarnath in the north to Rameswaram in the south. In addition, she promoted trade and gave special encouragement to the Maheshwar weaving industry in Madhya Pradesh, which continues to produce its famous handloom sarees even today.

Ahilyabai's rule is often described as a golden period, because she combined simplicity and devotion with efficient governance, cultural revival and economic development. She was not only a queen but also a symbol of motherly care, earning love and respect from her people across India.

MARATHAS IN THANJAVUR

The Marathas not only made their mark in Maharashtra but also established themselves in southern India, creating a unique chapter in Indian history. Their rule in Thanjavur (in present-day Tamil Nadu) brought together northern and southern traditions, giving rise to a rich cultural heritage that continues to influence the region.

Ekoji and the Founding of the Dynasty

Shivaji's half-brother Ekoji (also called Venkoji) conquered the Thanjavur region in the late 17th century, marking the beginning of Maratha rule in the area. He founded the Thanjavur Maratha dynasty and his descendants ruled for many generations. This brought Maratha culture, administration and traditions to Tamil Nadu, while also respecting and adapting to local Tamil practices. The Thanjavur Marathas were not just rulers but also patrons of literature, music and drama and many rulers themselves were poets and dramatists.

Serfoji II's Contributions

Among the rulers, Serfoji II (1798–1832) stands out as the most famous and influential ruler. A learned scholar, he was well-versed in Indian as well as European languages. He even wrote a Marathi play called Devendra Kuravanji, which described world geography in detail as it was known at that time. Serfoji II's court became a vibrant cultural hub where literature in Marathi, Tamil, Telugu and Sanskrit flourished.

Syncretic Culture

The Thanjavur Maratha rulers promoted a syncretic culture—a blending of traditions that brought together Tamil, Telugu and Marathi influences. Their courts were centres of art, music and learning, where different cultural streams enriched each other. This created a vibrant, multilingual cultural environment full of innovation. The legacy of the Thanjavur Marathas can still be seen today in the region's music, dance, architecture and literary traditions, making their rule an important link between the north and the south of India.

THE MARATHA LEGACY

The rise of the Marathas marked one of the most important chapters in Indian history, as they built a powerful empire and left behind lessons of unity, courage and administration. Their achievements in politics, military and culture shaped India's destiny and influenced later struggles for freedom. Following are some of the major contributions that form the lasting legacy of the Marathas:

  • Challenge to Mughal Supremacy: The Marathas emerged as one of the strongest Indian powers in the 17th and 18th centuries. They repeatedly challenged Mughal authority at a time when the empire was at its peak. Through their military campaigns and clever use of guerrilla-warfare, they broke Mughal dominance in many parts of India. By the mid-18th century, the Marathas had created the largest Indian empire before the British, stretching from the Deccan to Delhi and beyond. This proved that Indian powers could unite and resist mighty empires.

  • Efficient Administration and Military Strength: The Marathas established a well-organised system of governance, inspired by Shivaji's vision. Their administration was based on merit, discipline and careful revenue collection. They maintained a strong military that included infantry, cavalry, forts and even a navy. The system of chauth and sardeshmukhi gave them steady income while expanding their influence. Their ability to run an efficient government and maintain a disciplined army made them a model for later Indian rulers.

  • Encouragement of Cultural Revival: The Marathas not only focused on wars but also promoted culture, literature, religion and regional pride. They rebuilt temples destroyed in earlier invasions, patronised poets and scholars and promoted the use of Marathi and the Modi script in administration. They also encouraged arts, music and dance, blending local traditions with broader Indian culture. This revival gave people a sense of identity and unity.

  • Inspiration for Later Freedom Struggles: Perhaps the greatest legacy of the Marathas was the idea of Swarajya or self-rule. Shivaji's dream of an independent Indian state, free from foreign domination, inspired generations. During the 19th and 20th centuries, leaders of India's freedom movement often remembered Shivaji and the Marathas as symbols of courage and independence. Their resistance to the Mughals and later the British became a source of inspiration for freedom fighters like Bal Gangadhar Tilak, who proudly declared, 'Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it.'

KEY TERMS

Swarājya : A Marathi term meaning 'self-rule.' It was Shivaji's dream to establish a kingdom where people could live freely, with justice and dignity, without oppression from foreign rulers.

Guerrilla Warfare : A style of fighting that uses speed, surprise and knowledge of the land. Shivaji's soldiers attacked suddenly and disappeared into the hills, confusing large Mughal armies.

Fortresses : Strong stone structures built on hills or near coasts to protect people and store food, weapons and money. Examples include Raigad, Pratapgad and Sinhgadur.

Chauth and Sardeshmukhi : Special taxes introduced by the Marathas. Chauth was a 25% protection tax collected from regions outside their rule and Sardeshmukhi was an additional 10% claimed as a hereditary right.

Ashta Pradhana Mandala : Council of eight ministers created by Shivaji to help in administration. Each minister handled specific duties like finance, justice or military affairs.

Peshwa : The prime minister of the Maratha Empire. Over time, Peshwas became powerful leaders, especially under Bajirao I, who expanded Maratha power across India.

Coronation : A grand ceremony where a ruler is formally crowned. Shivaji's coronation at Raigad in 1674 declared him the independent king of the Marathas.

Dakshina Digvijaya : The southern campaign led by Shivaji to expand Maratha power into southern India. It increased the kingdom's wealth and influence.
