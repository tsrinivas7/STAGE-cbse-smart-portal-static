KEY TERMS

Growing up in the Sahyadri hills, he learned the terrain, which helped him develop guerrilla warfare. He observed the oppression under Mughals and Sultanates and realized people needed freedom, which he called Swarājya. He began his career by capturing Torna Fort at age 16 and steadily expanded by taking over other forts. He built a strong administration, navy, and army, laying the foundation for the Maratha Empire.

* Shivaji realized controlling the seas was important to protect his people, trade routes, and coastal towns from foreign powers like the Portuguese, British, and Siddis. He constructed strong sea forts like Sindhudurg and Vijaydurg. He built fleets of small but fast-moving ships that could attack suddenly and disappear. * Surat: In 1664, Shivaji raided Surat, seizing enormous treasure worth nearly one crore rupees. This wealth was used to strengthen forts and expand the army. He was careful not to harm ordinary people or places of worship.

* Military: Included infantry, cavalry, forts, and a navy. Soldiers were either Bārgirs (state-paid) or Shiledārs (maintained own equipment). Guerrilla tactics were used.

Q5. Labour-intensive production and Capital-intensive production

Steps:
1. Meaning - Labour-intensive: Industries where human effort and skills play most important role in making goods and services, depend less on machines.
2. Meaning - Capital-intensive: Industries where machines, technology and large financial investments play dominant role in creating goods and services.
3. Examples - Labour-intensive: Agriculture, handicrafts, cottage industries, small shops, bangle making where people work directly with natural resources or simple tools.
4. Examples - Capital-intensive: Automobile factories, semiconductor plants, space research centres where machines and technology carry out most work.
5. Features - Labour-intensive: Human Effort as primary resource; Worker Skills decide productivity and quality; Time and Energy - manual work slower, needs greater physical-mental energy; Limited Technology - simple tools, traditional techniques, low cost but limits large-scale production.
6. Features - Capital-intensive: Machines and Technology allow larger quantities, higher accuracy and speed; Large Investment to set up factories, buy and maintain equipment; High Efficiency, mechanisation makes production faster, cheaper per unit; Dependence on Experts - skilled engineers, scientists, technicians to design, monitor and repair.
7. Role of other factor: Labour-intensive still uses simple capital; Capital-intensive still needs skilled human capital to supervise machines, proving human effort and technology must complement.

Q1. What are the three main factors that decide whether something can be used as a resource? Explain briefly.

✅ Answer:
Step 1: Accessibility – Can we reach it? A natural element becomes a resource only when available for people to use, e.g. coal hidden deep inside the Earth cannot be useful until technology for mining is invented, and oil under ocean floor can only be used when machines to extract are developed.
Step 2: Feasibility – Is it possible to use it? Even if available, it is a resource only when practical and affordable to use, e.g. sunlight is everywhere but became a resource only after solar panels and energy storage were invented with money and technical knowledge; wind needs windmills and infrastructure.
Step 3: Cultural Acceptance – Is it useful to society? For wide use, people must find it desirable and acceptable, e.g. earlier firewood was main energy for easy collection, today society prefers clean wind and solar power due to awareness of deforestation and pollution.
Final: Accessibility, feasibility and cultural acceptance decide resource use.

WHO WERE THE MARATHAS?

The Marathas were a community of warriors, farmers and administrators who lived in the Deccan plateau, mainly in present-day Maharashtra. They are closely associated with the Marathi language, which developed into a strong medium of literature and culture. Over time, the Marathas grew from being local chiefs and cultivators into a powerful political force that shaped the course of Indian history. Their rise was not only due to battles but also because of cultural revival, social unity and political organisation.

During the 13th century, much of Maharashtra was ruled by the Yadava dynasty, with Devagiri (modern Daulatabad) as its capital. However, in the early 14th century, the Yadavas were defeated by the Khilji Sultanate of Delhi. Later, different Deccan Sultanates controlled the region and the Maratha chiefs had to serve under them. Even during this period of political change, the people of Maharashtra preserved their unique language, customs and traditions, which gave them a strong cultural identity.

A major source of Maratha strength came from the Bhakti movement, which spread across India between the 7th and 17th centuries. Saints such as Dnyaneshwar, Namdev, Tukaram, and Ramdas inspired people with devotional songs and simple poetry in Marathi, making spiritual teachings accessible to ordinary villagers. Dnyaneshwar translated the Bhagavad–Gītā into Marathi, while Tukaram’s abhangas (devotional hymns) moved people with their emphasis on faith, equality and humility. These saints also criticised social divisions and gave common people confidence in their own worth, preparing them to stand united against oppression. This cultural awakening laid the groundwork for later political unity. By the 17th century, some Maratha chiefs made attempts at independence, but they were not successful. However, the people were ready for a leader who could combine military skill, administrative wisdom and popular support. It was in this environment that Shivaji Maharaj rose to power. Inspired by the cultural traditions of Maharashtra and by the struggles of his time, he built a strong and disciplined state. His dream of Swaraja (self-rule) not only gave the Marathas political strength but also inspired later generations of Indians in their fight for freedom.

Shivaji's political and military career began at a very young age. In 1645, when he was just sixteen years old, he captured the Torna Fort near Pune. This was his first major achievement and is considered the true beginning of the Maratha state. After this success, Shivaji steadily expanded his control by taking over other forts such as Purandar, Rajgad and Kondana (later renamed Sinhagad). Each of these forts was very important because they were located on hilltops or near major routes, which made them easy to defend and useful for controlling trade and movement in the region.

Instead of always fighting large and costly battles, Shivaji often used clever negotiations, sudden attacks and surprise raids to capture forts. This showed his intelligence and ability to use strategy rather than relying only on strength. By slowly increasing the number of forts under his control, Shivaji laid the foundation for his dream of Swarājya (self-rule).

The forts were not only military strongholds but also acted as administrative centres. They stored food, weapons and money and served as safe shelters for soldiers and local people during times of war. From these forts, Shivaji could send out troops quickly, watch enemy movements and protect his people.

The capture of these forts symbolised the birth of Maratha power and gave Shivaji a strong and secure base to challenge much bigger kingdoms like the Mughals and the Bijapur Sultanate.

Coronation at Raigad (1674)

After years of constant battles, clever strategies and building a strong administration, Shivaji felt it was time to formally declare himself a sovereign and independent ruler. Until then, he was often considered just a local chief or rebel by larger powers like the Mughals and the Deccan Sultanates. To change this image and give his Swarajya (self-rule) greater recognition, Shivaji organised a grand coronation ceremony at the strong mountain fortress of Raigad in June 1674.

In this magnificent ceremony, Shivaji was coronated with full Vedic rites and crowned as Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj, the protector and sovereign of the Marathas. His formal royal title was 'Shri Raja Shiva Chhatrapati' and like some past rulers, he even started his own era, the Rāyābhiṣheka śhaka (coronation era). The rituals followed traditional Hindu practices, with priests from different regions invited to perform sacred rites. This was very significant because it revived the idea of Hindu kingship based on dharma (righteousness) at a time when most of India was ruled by the Mughals or the Sultanates. The coronation thus gave a strong cultural and religious identity to the Maratha kingdom.

Southern Campaign (Dakshina Digvijaya)

In the last phase of his life, Shivaji turned his attention towards the southern regions of India in what is known as the Dakshina Digvijaya or the Southern Conquest. Until then, Maratha influence was mainly limited to Maharashtra and nearby areas, but Shivaji realised that controlling the 'southern territories would give the Marathas both wealth and strategic strength. He began capturing important forts, towns and strongholds in present-day Karnataka and Tamil Nadu. These regions were rich in fertile land, resources and trade routes, which added to the prosperity of the Maratha kingdom.

Through his campaigns, Shivaji extended Maratha influence far beyond Maharashtra, making the Marathas a dominant power in peninsular India. His armies moved swiftly, defeating local rulers, securing key forts and bringing new areas under Maratha control. These victories not only expanded the size of the empire but also improved its economic base by adding new sources of revenue and trade.

Shivaji also showed his sense of justice and humanity during these campaigns. On the southern coast, he strongly opposed the European practice of slave trading, where people were captured and sold as slaves by foreign traders. By taking a stand against this cruel activity, Shivaji proved that his rule was not just about power and expansion but also about protecting the dignity and rights of people.

The southern campaign was highly significant because it prepared the ground for future Maratha expansion. After Shivaji's death, his successors like Sambhaji and later the Peshwas continued this policy, spreading Maratha power even further into southern and northern India. Thus, Shivaji's Dakshina Digvijaya became the starting point for the transformation of the Marathas into one of the strongest empires in 18th-century India.

Shivaji's Legacy and Death

Shivaji Maharaj is remembered in Indian history as a visionary leader, brilliant strategist and true nation-builder. He was not only a skilled warrior but also a wise administrator who cared deeply about the welfare of his people. By creating a strong civil administration, a disciplined army and even a navy, he laid the foundation of the Maratha Empire, which continued to remain powerful long after his death. His policies of religious tolerance, justice and protection of ordinary people made him a beloved ruler among Hindus, Muslims and other communities living under his rule.

Shivaji's courage and innovative military methods such as guerrilla warfare and fort-based defence inspired his contemporaries and earned him respect even from his enemies. More importantly, he became a symbol of resistance against foreign domination. Generations after him, Indian leaders and freedom fighters looked up to Shivaji as an example of bravery, determination and love for Swarajya (self-rule).

In 1680, when Shivaji was about fifty years old, he fell seriously ill with a fever at Raigad Fort and passed away. His death caused deep sorrow among his followers, but he left behind a powerful state, a loyal army and an efficient administration capable of carrying forward his vision. Within his lifetime, his exploits had already become legendary not only across India but also beyond. European travellers and writers compared him to great ancient generals like Alexander the Great.

Even today, he is celebrated as one of the greatest heroes of Indian history, remembered for his courage, justice, farsighted leadership and his dream of Swarajya.

Battle of Panipat (1761)

The Third Battle of Panipat, fought on 14 January 1761, was one of the biggest and bloodiest battles in Indian history. At this time, the Marathas were at the height of their power and had expanded their influence far into northern India. They even controlled important regions such as Lahore, Attock and Peshawar (in present-day Pakistan). Their rapid expansion, however, worried other Indian rulers and invited challenges from outside powers.

The Afghan ruler Ahmad Shah Abdali invaded India and the Marathas marched north to stop him. Although the Maratha army was vast and courageous, they faced severe difficulties—such as long supply lines, lack of food and reinforcements and very little support from other Indian kingdoms, many of whom feared Maratha domination. On the battlefield of Panipat, the Marathas suffered a terrible and disastrous defeat, with thousands of their soldiers and many key leaders killed. Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1818)

By the latter half of the 18th century, the chief rivals of the British in India were the Marathas. At this time the Mughal Empire had declined and the Marathas were still a powerful force, though divided among several chiefs like the Scindias, Holkars, Bhonsles and Gaekwads. The British East India Company, with its growing strength, modern weapons and superior organisation, saw the Marathas as the biggest obstacle to their expansion in India. A series of three wars, known as the Anglo-Maratha Wars, were fought between 1775 and 1818. These were ultimately ended Maratha political independence.

  • First Anglo-Maratha War (1775–1782): This war began due to the succession disputes within the Maratha leadership. The British tried to take advantage of these internal rivalries. However, the Marathas fought bravely and the war ended with the Treaty of Salbai, which brought temporary peace and left the Marathas still strong.
  • Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803–1805): In this phase, the British defeated some of the most powerful Maratha chiefs, including the Scindias and Bhonsles. The Marathas lost key territories, which greatly reduced their strength and influence in India. The war highlighted the superior military organisation and modern technology of the British compared to the Marathas' divided forces.
  • Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–1818): This was the final and most decisive conflict. By now, the Marathas were weakened by internal disunity and constant rivalries among their chiefs. The British launched well-planned campaigns with their modern army, completely defeating and crushing Maratha resistance. The Peshwa, who was the nominal head of the Maratha confederacy, was forced to surrender and was given a pension.

By 1818, almost all Maratha territories were annexed by the British, marking the end of Maratha political power in India. In reality, the British gained control of India more from the Marathas than from the Mughals or any other power, since the Marathas were the last major Indian force resisting them.

Yet, the Maratha legacy did not die. Their bravery, resistance and love for Swarājya (self-rule) inspired later generations of Indians. Many 19th- and 20th-century freedom fighters drew inspiration from the Marathas' long struggle against the British, keeping their spirit alive in India's independence movement.

MARATHA ADMINISTRATION

Civil Administration

Shivaji set up a strong and centralised civil administration that reduced the excessive power of old feudal nobles and replaced it with a fair system based on merit and responsibility. Instead of granting land as jagirs (hereditary rights that often led to corruption), he ensured his officers were paid fixed salaries from the state treasury. This made them loyal to the government rather than to powerful families. To avoid misuse of power, officials were also transferred regularly. The welfare of soldiers and their families was given importance—for instance, if a soldier died in battle, his widow received a pension and sometimes his son was given a government job, which was a rare practice in those times. To govern effectively, Shivaji created the ashta pradhāna maṇḍala (council of eight ministers). Each minister had a specific duty, such as handling finances, justice, military affairs, foreign relations or religious matters. This division of work made the administration systematic, efficient and fair, ensuring that power was not concentrated in one hand.

Navy

Shivaji was one of the first Indian rulers to understand the importance of a navy in defending the kingdom. He built a strong fleet to guard the Konkan coast, prevent piracy and control sea trade. Under the command of Kanhoji Angre, the Maratha navy grew powerful enough that even European powers like the Portuguese and the British had to recognise Maratha authority at sea. The Marathas demanded passes from foreign ships entering their waters, showing their dominance. Although their ships were smaller and less advanced than European ones, their knowledge of the coastline and swift tactics gave them an advantage. For many years, they maintained supremacy in the Arabian Sea, protecting trade and strengthening Maratha power.

Justice

Shivaji gave great importance to fair justice for ordinary people. Small disputes were settled by panchayats, which were councils of respected village elders. This made justice quick, cheap and fair at the local level. In towns, kotwāls (police officials) maintained law and order, while more serious cases went to higher authorities. Harsh punishments like the death penalty were rare; instead, punishments were moderate and based on fairness. People also had the right to appeal to higher officials if they felt wronged. Shivaji also instructed his officers to protect people from exploitation and not to harm farmers, women or ordinary citizens during wars. This created a sense of trust between the people and the Maratha rulers.

Trade and Economy

The Marathas understood that a strong economy was as important as a strong army. To improve trade, they built and maintained roads, river transport, ferries and bridges, which made the movement of goods and people easier and safer. Well-connected routes linked villages, towns and forts, helping farmers sell their crops and traders carry merchandise without too many delays. This improved communication also supported military needs, as soldiers and supplies could be moved quickly across regions.

The Marathas also engaged in maritime trade by developing coastal ports and building a navy. Important ports such as Mocha, Muscat and Malacca became centres of overseas trade. Through these ports, Indian goods like cotton textiles, spices and food grains were exchanged for horses, weapons and luxury items from other countries. This international trade not only brought wealth and prosperity but also increased the Marathas' political importance.

By encouraging both inland and overseas trade, the Marathas ensured that farmers, artisans and merchants benefited. This wealth supported their administration and army, helping them expand their power. The focus on trade and economy shows that the Marathas were not just warriors but also skilled organisers who cared about the prosperity of their people.

CULTURAL REVIVAL UNDER THE MARATHAS

The Marathas were not only great warriors and administrators but also strong patrons of culture. Under their rule, there was a revival of local traditions, languages and art forms, which gave the people of Maharashtra and other regions a strong sense of identity and pride.

  • Royal Seal and Identity: Shivaji introduced a special royal seal with an inscription in Sanskrit that highlighted the idea of Swarajya (self-rule). This seal was not just a mark of authority but also a symbol of cultural pride, showing that Indian traditions and languages were important even in matters of state.

  • Promotion of Marathi Language: Texts such as the Rajya-Vyavahara-Kosha (administrative glossary) promoted the use of Marathi in government and everyday administration. This reduced dependence on Persian, which was widely used in Mughal offices and gave common people a chance to connect with their rulers in their own language.

  • Religious Tolerance and Arts: The Marathas encouraged religious harmony and supported people of different faiths. Shivaji is remembered for protecting Hindu temples, rebuilding those destroyed during wars and also respecting Muslim saints and dargahs. This atmosphere of tolerance allowed literature, music and painting to flourish under Maratha patronage.

  • Use of Modi Script: The Marathas popularised the Modi script for administrative purposes. This script was simpler and faster to write compared to Sanskrit or Persian, making it highly practical for record-keeping, correspondence and revenue work. Its widespread use also strengthened Marathi cultural identity.

THE ROLE OF MARATHA WOMEN

Women in the Maratha period were not just confined to households; many of them played important roles in politics, administration, culture and even warfare. They became symbols of courage, wisdom and dedication and their contributions kept the Maratha spirit alive during some of the most difficult times in history. Two of the most inspiring women of this era were Tarabai and Ahilyabai Holkar.

Tarabai

[IMAGE_START] A painting of a woman, Tarabai, riding a white horse. She is wearing yellow/orange clothing and holding a sword or weapon. The background shows a landscape with hills. The caption below reads "Tarabai". [IMAGE_END]

Tarabai, the daughter-in-law of Shivaji and wife of Rajaram, emerged as a fearless warrior queen after the death of her husband. At a time when the Marathas were under constant attack by Aurangzeb, she personally took charge of the kingdom and led armies with great skill. Tarabai adopted guerrilla tactics, just like Shivaji, to harass and weaken Mughal forces. She not only defended Maratha territories but also realised that with

Aurangzeb busy in the Deccan, north India was left at risk. Taking advantage of this, she organised large Maratha armies and sent them to invade Mughal territories in the north. In this sense, Tarabai was the true architect of the Maratha northward expansion. Her courage, intelligence and determination outsmarted the Mughal Empire and preserved Maratha independence during a critical period. She remains an inspiration for later generations of women leaders in India.

Ahilyabai Holkar

Ahilyabai Holkar, one of the most respected queens in Indian history, belonged to the powerful Holkar dynasty that played a major role in Maratha expansion in north India. After the tragic death of her husband and son, she ruled the Malwa kingdom for thirty years with wisdom, fairness and compassion. Ahilyabai was known for her just governance, where she reduced corruption, cared for the common people and ensured justice for all. She was deeply religious and is remembered for rebuilding many temples destroyed during earlier invasions. Most famously, she rebuilt the Kashi Vishwanath temple in Varanasi and the Somnath temple in Gujarat, restoring their cultural and spiritual importance. She also undertook large public works by constructing wells, ghats, roads and rest houses across India—from Kedarnath in the north to Rameswaram in the south. In addition, she promoted trade and gave special encouragement to the Maheshwar weaving industry in Madhya Pradesh, which continues to produce its famous handloom sarees even today. Ahilyabai’s rule is often described as a golden period, because she combined simplicity and devotion with efficient governance, cultural revival and economic development. She was not only a queen but also a symbol of motherly care, earning love and respect from her people across India.

MARATHAS IN THANJAVUR

The Marathas not only made their mark in Maharashtra but also established themselves in southern India, creating a unique chapter in Indian history. Their rule in Thanjavur (in present-day Tamil Nadu) brought together northern and southern traditions, giving rise to a rich cultural heritage that continues to influence the region.

Ekoji and the Founding of the Dynasty

Shivaji’s half-brother Ekoji (also called Venkoji) conquered the Thanjavur region in the late 17th century, marking the beginning of Maratha rule in the area. He founded the Thanjavur Maratha dynasty and his descendants ruled for many generations. This brought Maratha culture, administration and traditions to Tamil Nadu, while also respecting and adapting to local Tamil practices. The Thanjavur Marathas were not just rulers but also patrons of literature, music and drama and many rulers themselves were poets and dramatists.

Serfoji II’s Contributions

Among the rulers, Serfoji II (1798–1832) stands out as the most famous and influential ruler. A learned scholar, he was well-versed in Indian as well as European languages. He even wrote a Marathi play called Devendra Kuravanji, which described world geography in detail as it was known at that time. Serfoji II’s court became a vibrant cultural hub where literature in Marathi, Tamil, Telugu and Sanskrit flourished.

He patronised musicians and it was under his guidance that modern Carnatic music took shape, while the dance form Bharatanatyam entered a new stage of revival. Serfoji also encouraged medical knowledge by establishing the Dhanwantari Mahal, a centre that offered free treatment using both Indian and Western systems of medicine. Importantly, he introduced printing technology by setting up one of the earliest printing presses in India run by a native

Syncretic Culture

The Thanjavur Maratha rulers promoted a syncretic culture—a blending of traditions that brought together Tamil, Telugu and Marathi influences. Their courts were centres of art, music and learning, where different cultural streams enriched each other. This created a vibrant, multilingual cultural environment full of innovation. The legacy of the Thanjavur Marathas can still be seen today in the region's music, dance, architecture and literary traditions, making their rule an important link between the north and the south of India.

THE MARATHA LEGACY

The rise of the Marathas marked one of the most important chapters in Indian history, as they built a powerful empire and left behind lessons of unity, courage and administration. Their achievements in politics, military and culture shaped India's destiny and influenced later struggles for freedom. Following are some of the major contributions that form the lasting legacy of the Marathas:

  • Challenge to Mughal Supremacy: The Marathas emerged as one of the strongest Indian powers in the 17th and 18th centuries. They repeatedly challenged Mughal authority at a time when the empire was at its peak. Through their military campaigns and clever use of guerrilla warfare, they broke Mughal dominance in many parts of India. By the mid-18th century, the Marathas had created the largest Indian empire before the British, stretching from the Deccan to Delhi and beyond. This proved that Indian powers could unite and resist mighty empires.

  • Efficient Administration and Military Strength: The Marathas established a well-organised system of governance, inspired by Shivaji's vision. Their administration was based on merit, discipline and careful revenue collection. They maintained a strong military that included infantry, cavalry, forts and even a navy. The system of chauth and sardeshmukhi gave them steady income while expanding their influence. Their ability to run an efficient government and maintain a disciplined army made them a model for later Indian rulers.

  • Encouragement of Cultural Revival: The Marathas not only focused on wars but also promoted culture, literature, religion and regional pride. They rebuilt temples destroyed in earlier invasions, patronised poets and scholars and promoted the use of Marathi and the Modi script in administration. They also encouraged arts, music and dance, blending local traditions with broader Indian culture. This revival gave people a sense of identity and unity.

  • Inspiration for Later Freedom Struggles: Perhaps the greatest legacy of the Marathas was the idea of Swarajya or self-rule. Shivaji's dream of an independent Indian state, free from foreign domination, inspired generations. During the 19th and 20th centuries, leaders of India's freedom movement often remembered Shivaji and the Marathas as symbols of courage and independence. Their resistance to the Mughals and later the British became a source of inspiration for freedom fighters like Bal Gangadhar Tilak, who proudly declared, 'Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it.'

The famous Indian leader Bal Gangadhar Tilak revived the public celebration of Ganesh Chaturthi in the 1890s to promote unity and nationalism. He chose Shivaji as a cultural hero, calling him the 'Father of Indian Nationalism'—linking Maratha Swarajya to modern India's freedom movement.

KEY TERMS

Swarajya
: A Marathi term meaning 'self-rule.' It was Shivaji's dream to establish a kingdom where people could live freely, with justice and dignity, without oppression from foreign rulers.

Guerrilla Warfare
: A style of fighting that uses speed, surprise and knowledge of the land. Shivaji's soldiers attacked suddenly and disappeared into the hills, confusing large Mughal armies.

Fortresses
: Strong stone structures built on hills or near coasts to protect people and store food, weapons and money. Examples include Raigad, Pratapgad and Sindhudurg.

Chauth and Sardeshmukhi
: Special taxes introduced by the Marathas. Chauth was a 25% protection tax collected from regions outside their rule and Sardeshmukhi was an additional 10% claimed as a hereditary right.

Ashta Pradhana Mandal
: A council of eight ministers created by Shivaji to help in administration. Each minister handled specific duties like finance, justice or military affairs.

Peshwa
: The prime minister of the Maratha Empire. Over time, Peshwas became powerful leaders, especially under Bajirao I, who expanded Maratha power across India.

Coronation
: A grand ceremony where a ruler is formally crowned. Shivaji's coronation at Raigad in 1674 declared him the independent king of the Marathas.

Dakshina Digvijaya
: The southern campaign led by Shivaji to expand Maratha power into southern India. It increased the kingdom's wealth and influence.

QUICK RECAP

  • The Marathas, a proud community of warriors, farmers and administrators from the Deccan plateau, rose to power through unity, courage and devotion to the ideal of Swarajya or self-rule, which became the foundation of their empire.
  • The Bhakti movement in Maharashtra, led by saints like Dnyaneshwar, Namdev, Tukaram and Ramdas, united the people through simple Marathi hymns and messages of faith, equality and self-respect, inspiring future political unity.
  • Shivaji Maharaj, born in 1630, was inspired by his mother Jijabai's teachings and trained in warfare and leadership; his vision of self-rule arose from witnessing the oppression of the people under foreign powers.
  • Beginning with the capture of Torna Fort at just sixteen, Shivaji expanded his control through strategy and courage, turning forts into centres of administration, defence and people's safety.
  • Recognising the importance of sea power, Shivaji built a strong navy and sea forts like Sindhudurg and Vijaydurg, protecting the Konkan coast from pirates and foreign invasions, thus pioneering naval defence in India.
  • His brilliant use of guerrilla warfare—swift, surprise attacks using the Sahyadri hills—allowed smaller Maratha forces to defeat much larger Mughal and Bijapur armies, a strategy that became legendary in Indian history.
  • Major encounters like the defeat of Afzal Khan, the daring raid on Shaista Khan's camp and the sacking of Surat showcased Shivaji's courage, intelligence and commitment to justice and self-rule.
  • The Treaty of Purandar and the Agra escape revealed Shivaji's diplomatic skill and determination; even when imprisoned by Aurangzeb, his clever escape restored Maratha pride and strengthened belief in Swarajya.
  • Shivaji's coronation at Raigad in 1674 marked his formal recognition as Chhatrapati, establishing Hindu kingship based on dharma and cultural pride and giving legitimacy to the Maratha state.
  • His southern campaign (Dakshina Digvijaya) extended Maratha influence into Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, making the Marathas a dominant force across peninsular India.
  • After Shivaji's death in 1680, successors like Sambhaji, Rajaram and Tarabai continued the struggle against Aurangzeb, proving that Shivaji's vision of freedom was stronger than any single ruler's life.
  • The Peshwas later expanded Maratha power under the leaders like Bajirao I and Nana Saheb, turning the kingdom into an empire that stretched from the Deccan to Delhi and became India's most powerful native force.
  • Despite their defeat in the Third Battle of Panipat (1761), the Marathas revived under Madhavrao I and generals like Mahadji Shinde, regaining Delhi and maintaining influence until the British conquest after the Anglo-Maratha Wars.
  • The Maratha legacy—strong administration, cultural revival, respect for justice, religious tolerance, women's leadership and the spirit of Swarajya—continues to inspire India's ideals of unity, freedom and self-governance.
