Quadrilaterals — Summary

Chapter Summary

Introduction to Quadrilaterals

  • Quadrilateral — A four-sided figure. The word comes from Latin words quadri (meaning four) and latus (meaning sides).
  • Angles of a quadrilateral — The angles between the sides of a quadrilateral.
  • A quadrilateral must have four straight sides; figures with curved sides or re-entrant indentations are not quadrilaterals.
  • Quadrilaterals can be convex (all interior angles less than 180 degrees) or concave (one interior angle greater than 180 degrees).
  • This chapter focuses on studying special types of quadrilaterals like rectangles and squares, and deducing their properties through geometric reasoning.

4.1 Rectangles and Squares

  • Rectangle — A rectangle is a quadrilateral in which (i) all the angles are right angles (90 degrees), and (ii) the opposite sides are of equal length.
  • The definition of a rectangle specifies the exact conditions a quadrilateral must satisfy to be called a rectangle.
  • Rectangles and squares are the most familiar types of quadrilaterals.
  • The chapter explores alternative ways to define a rectangle, particularly through properties of its diagonals.

A Carpenter's Problem

  • A carpenter needs to join two thin strips of wood so that a thread passed through their endpoints forms a rectangle.
  • The strips are modelled as the diagonals of the rectangle formed by their endpoints.
  • Three key questions arise: (1) What is the length of the other diagonal? (2) What is the point of intersection of the diagonals? (3) What should the angle be between the diagonals?
  • These questions are answered using geometric reasoning (deduction) and congruence of triangles.
  • The problem connects geometry to real-world applications used by carpenters and farmers.

Deduction 1 — What is the length of the other diagonal?

  • In rectangle ABCD, triangles ADC and DAB are congruent by the SAS congruence condition (AB = CD, angle BAD = angle CDA = 90 degrees, AD is common).
  • Since AC and BD are corresponding parts of congruent triangles, AC = BD.
  • This proves that the diagonals of a rectangle are always equal in length.
  • If one diagonal is 8 cm, the other must also be 8 cm.
  • This property can be verified by constructing and measuring rectangles.

Deduction 2 — What is the point of intersection of the two diagonals?

  • Bisecting a quantity — Dividing a quantity into two equal parts.
  • Triangles AOB and COD are shown to be congruent using the AAS congruence condition (vertically opposite angles are equal, angle 1 = angle 2, and AB = CD).
  • From the congruence, OA = OC and OB = OD, meaning O is the midpoint of both diagonals.
  • This proves that the diagonals of a rectangle always bisect each other (intersect at their midpoints).
  • To form a rectangle, the diagonals must be equal in length and must bisect each other.

Deduction 3 — What are the angles between the diagonals?

  • If two equal diagonals bisect each other at an arbitrary angle x, the four angles between the diagonals are x, x, (180 - x), and (180 - x).
  • In triangle AOB (isosceles since OA = OB), the base angles are each a = 90 - x/2.
  • In triangle AOD (isosceles since OA = OD), the base angles are each b = x/2.
  • Each angle of the quadrilateral equals a + b = (90 - x/2) + x/2 = 90 degrees.
  • Since triangles AOB is congruent to COD and AOD is congruent to COB, opposite sides are equal (AB = CD and AD = CB).
  • Therefore, no matter what the angle between the diagonals is, if the diagonals are equal and bisect each other, the quadrilateral formed is always a rectangle.

The Process of Finding Properties

  • Conjecture — A statement about which we are highly confident, but not yet sure if it always holds true.
  • Properties of geometric objects can be deduced through geometric reasoning (deduction/proof).
  • Observations through measurement give useful insights but only form conjectures, not proofs.
  • Constructing a few rectangles and observing that diagonals bisect each other does not prove it always holds; only a formal proof (like Deduction 2) can guarantee this.
  • Students are encouraged to deduce properties, but when challenged, they may experiment, observe, and use intuition to form conjectures.

Rectangle — Alternative Definition (from the Carpenter's Problem)

  • Rectangle (alternative definition) — A rectangle is a quadrilateral whose diagonals are equal and bisect each other.
  • This definition is different from the original one but captures the same class of quadrilaterals.
  • The original definition can be further simplified: a rectangle can be defined simply as a quadrilateral in which all angles are 90 degrees (the equal opposite sides follow automatically).

Deduction 4 — What is the shape of a quadrilateral with all angles equal to 90 degrees?

  • In quadrilateral ABCD with all angles 90 degrees, joining diagonal BD creates triangles BAD and DCB.
  • Using the same reasoning as Deduction 2: angle 1 = angle 2 (both equal to 90 degrees minus angle 3).
  • By the AAS congruence condition, triangle BAD is congruent to triangle DCB.
  • Therefore, AD = CB and DC = BA (corresponding sides of congruent triangles).
  • This proves that if all angles of a quadrilateral are 90 degrees, the opposite sides must be equal, making it a rectangle.
  • Thus, a rectangle can be simply defined as: A rectangle is a quadrilateral in which the angles are all 90 degrees.

Properties of a Rectangle

  • Property 1: All the angles of a rectangle are 90 degrees.
  • Property 2: The opposite sides of a rectangle are equal.
  • Property 3: The opposite sides of a rectangle are parallel to each other. (Justified using transversal properties: if the sum of internal angles on the same side of a transversal is 180 degrees, the lines are parallel.)
  • Property 4: The diagonals of a rectangle are of equal length and they bisect each other.
  • Every rectangle has opposite sides parallel, which can be shown using the transversal property (e.g., AB is a transversal to AD and BC, and angle A + angle B = 180 degrees, so AD is parallel to BC).

A Special Rectangle (Square)

  • Square — A square is a quadrilateral in which all the angles are equal to 90 degrees, and all the sides are of equal length.
  • Every square is also a rectangle, but not every rectangle is a square.
  • A square is a special type of rectangle where all four sides are equal.
  • Venn diagram — A diagram where a set of objects is represented as points inside a closed curve (typically ovals or circles). The set of all squares is shown inside the set of all rectangles, since every square is a rectangle.
  • The Venn diagram shows the relationship: the circle for "Square" is entirely contained within the oval for "Rectangle."

Properties of a Square

  • Since a square is a special rectangle, all properties of a rectangle hold for a square.
  • Property 1: All the sides of a square are equal to each other.
  • Property 2: The opposite sides of a square are parallel to each other.
  • Property 3: The angles of a square are all 90 degrees.
  • Property 4: The diagonals of a square are of equal length and they bisect each other at 90 degrees.
  • Property 5: The diagonals of a square bisect (divide into equal halves) the angles of the square. Each diagonal divides the corner angles into two equal parts of 45 degrees each.
  • The diagonals of a square bisect each other at right angles (90 degrees), which is an additional property not shared by all rectangles.
  • In triangle ADC of a square, since AD = DC, the base angles are equal: angle 1 = angle 3 = 45 degrees (since angle 1 + angle 3 + 90 = 180).

4.2 Angles in a Quadrilateral

  • Sum of all angles in any quadrilateral is 360 degrees.
  • A diagonal drawn in a quadrilateral divides it into two triangles.
  • Each triangle has angles summing to 180 degrees, so the total sum of all six angles is 180 + 180 = 360 degrees.
  • The six angles combine to form the four interior angles of the quadrilateral, so the sum of the four angles is 360 degrees.
  • This explains why it is impossible to construct a quadrilateral with three right angles and a fourth angle that is not 90 degrees (since 90 + 90 + 90 + fourth angle = 360, the fourth angle must also be 90 degrees).

Key Terms

AAS congruence condition — a condition for congruence where two angles and a side of one triangle are equal to the corresponding parts of another triangle.

Bisect — dividing a quantity into two equal parts.

Concave quadrilateral — a four-sided figure with a re-entrant angle or indentation.

Congruent triangles — triangles that have the same shape and size, with all corresponding sides and angles equal.

Conjecture — a statement about which we are highly confident, but not yet sure if it always holds true.

Convex quadrilateral — a four-sided figure where all interior angles are less than 180 degrees.

Corresponding parts of congruent triangles — the matching sides and angles of two congruent triangles that are equal to each other.

Diagonal — a line segment connecting two non-adjacent (opposite) vertices of a quadrilateral.

Isosceles triangle — a triangle with two equal sides and two equal base angles.

Linear pair — a pair of adjacent angles formed on a straight line whose sum is 180 degrees.

Quadrilateral — a four-sided figure, derived from Latin words 'quadri' meaning four and 'latus' referring to sides.

Rectangle — a quadrilateral in which all angles are 90 degrees (equivalently, a quadrilateral whose diagonals are equal and bisect each other).

SAS congruence condition — a condition for congruence where two sides and the included angle of one triangle are equal to the corresponding parts of another triangle.

Square — a quadrilateral in which all angles are 90 degrees and all sides are of equal length.

Transversal — a line that intersects two or more other lines.

Venn diagram — a pictorial representation where a set of objects is shown as points inside a closed curve, typically ovals or circles.

Vertically opposite angles — angles formed opposite each other when two lines intersect, which are always equal.

Q: Are there other ways to define a rectangle?

Answer:
Yes. Besides the standard definition (all angles 90° and opposite sides equal), a rectangle can also be defined as:
- A quadrilateral whose diagonals are equal and bisect each other.
- A quadrilateral in which all angles are 90° (this alone is sufficient, as shown in Deduction 4).

A Carpenter's Problem

Q1: What is the length of the other diagonal?

Answer:
The other diagonal must also be 8 cm.

Reasoning: In rectangle ABCD, AB = CD (opposite sides equal), angle BAD = angle CDA = 90°, and AD is common. So triangle ADC is congruent to triangle DAB by SAS congruence. Therefore, AC = BD (corresponding parts of congruent triangles). Since AC = 8 cm, BD = 8 cm as well.

Q2: What is the point of intersection of the two diagonals?

Answer:
The diagonals intersect at their midpoints. That is, if the diagonals AC and BD intersect at O, then OA = OC and OB = OD.

Reasoning: Using the AAS congruence condition, triangle AOB is congruent to triangle COD (vertically opposite angles are equal, angle 1 = angle 2 as shown in the text, and AB = CD). Hence OA = OC and OB = OD.

Q3: What should the angle be between the diagonals?

Answer:
The angle between the diagonals can be any angle. As long as the diagonals are equal in length and bisect each other, the resulting quadrilateral is always a rectangle, regardless of the angle between the diagonals. This is proven in the generalization section where the angle is taken as x (any value), and all four angles of the quadrilateral still come out to be 90°.

Deduction 2 — Inline Question

Q: Since we need to know the relation between OA and OC, and OB and OD, which two triangles of the rectangle ABCD should we consider?

Answer:
We should consider triangle AOB and triangle COD. These triangles share vertically opposite angles (angle AOB = angle COD), and we can show that angle ABD (angle 1) = angle BDC (angle 2) using the reasoning that both equal 90° - angle 3. Combined with AB = CD, this gives congruence by AAS, from which OA = OC and OB = OD follow.

Math Talk

Q: Can the following equalities be used to establish that triangle AOD is congruent to triangle COB?

  • AO = CO (proved above)
  • angle AOB = angle COD (vertically opposite angles)
  • AD = CB

Answer:
Yes. We have:
- AO = CO (proved in Deduction 2)
- angle AOB = angle COD (vertically opposite angles)
- AD = CB (opposite sides of a rectangle are equal)

By the SAS congruence condition, triangle AOD is congruent to triangle COB. This gives us OD = OB as corresponding parts, which is consistent with the result that O is the midpoint of both diagonals.

Deduction 3 — Inline Questions

Q: Can you find all the remaining angles? (When angle between diagonals is 60°)

Answer:
Given angle AOB = 60°:
- angle COD = 60° (vertically opposite to angle AOB)
- angle AOD = 180° - 60° = 120° (linear pair)
- angle BOC = 120° (vertically opposite to angle AOD)

Q: Can you find the value of a? (In triangle AOB, where OA = OB and angle AOB = 60°)

Answer:
In triangle AOB, since OA = OB, the base angles are equal. Let each base angle be a.

a + a + 60 = 180 (sum of interior angles of a triangle)

2a = 120

a = 60°

Q: Can we now identify what type of quadrilateral ABCD is?

Answer:
All four angles of quadrilateral ABCD add up to 90° each (30° + 60° = 90° at each vertex). Since all angles are 90° and opposite sides are equal (AB = CD and AD = CB, as shown by congruent triangles), ABCD is a rectangle.

Q: What can we say about its sides?

Answer:
Since triangle AOB is congruent to triangle COD, we get AB = CD. Since triangle AOD is congruent to triangle COB, we get AD = CB. So opposite sides are equal, which is consistent with ABCD being a rectangle.

Generalization — Inline Questions

Q: Will ABCD remain a rectangle if the angles between the diagonals are changed? Can we generalise this?

Answer:
Yes, ABCD will remain a rectangle no matter what the angle between the diagonals is. This is because when the diagonals are equal and bisect each other, all four angles of the quadrilateral always come out to be 90° and opposite sides are always equal, regardless of the angle between the diagonals.

Q: Can you find the other angles? (General case with angle x between diagonals)

Answer:
If angle AOB = x, then:
- angle COD = x (vertically opposite)
- angle AOD = 180° - x (linear pair)
- angle BOC = 180° - x (vertically opposite)

Q: What is the value of a (in degrees) in terms of x?

Answer:
In triangle AOB (isosceles with OA = OB), the base angles are a:

a + a + x = 180

2a = 180 - x

a = (180 - x) / 2 = 90 - x/2

Similarly, in triangle AOD (isosceles with OA = OD), the base angles are b:

b + b + (180 - x) = 180

2b = x

b = x/2

Each angle of the quadrilateral = a + b = (90 - x/2) + x/2 = 90°

Q: What can we say about AB and CD, and AD and BC?

Answer:
Since triangle AOB is congruent to triangle COD, AB = CD. Since triangle AOD is congruent to triangle COB, AD = CB. So opposite sides are equal, confirming the quadrilateral is a rectangle.

Simplified Definition of Rectangle (Page 89)

Q: Would we be wrong if we just define a rectangle as a quadrilateral in which all the angles are 90°?

Answer:
No, we would not be wrong. As proven in Deduction 4, if all angles of a quadrilateral are 90°, then the opposite sides must automatically be equal. So the condition "all angles are 90°" alone is sufficient to define a rectangle.

Q: If you think that this definition is incomplete, try constructing a quadrilateral in which the angles are all 90° but the opposite sides are not equal. Are you able to construct such a quadrilateral?

Answer:
No, it is impossible to construct such a quadrilateral. If all four angles are 90°, the opposite sides must be equal. This is proven in Deduction 4: by drawing a diagonal and using AAS congruence (triangle BAD is congruent to triangle DCB), we get AD = CB and DC = BA. So the condition of all angles being 90° automatically ensures opposite sides are equal.

Deduction 4 — Inline Questions

Q: Consider a quadrilateral ABCD with all angles measuring 90°. What can we say about the opposite sides of such a quadrilateral?

Answer:
The opposite sides are equal. By joining diagonal BD and showing that angle 1 = angle 2 (both equal to 90° - angle 3), we get triangle BAD congruent to triangle DCB by AAS congruence. Therefore, AD = CB and DC = BA (corresponding parts of congruent triangles).

Q: Is it wrong to write triangle BAD is congruent to triangle CDB? Why?

Answer:
Yes, it is wrong to write "triangle BAD is congruent to triangle CDB" because the order of vertices in congruence statements matters. The correct statement is triangle BAD is congruent to triangle DCB.

In triangle BAD and triangle DCB:
- angle A (of BAD) corresponds to angle C (of DCB) — both are 90°
- angle 1 (ABD) corresponds to angle 2 (CDB) — both are equal
- BD corresponds to BD (common side)

If we write "triangle CDB" instead of "triangle DCB", the vertex correspondence would be:
- B corresponds to C
- A corresponds to D
- D corresponds to B

This would imply BA = CD (correct), AD = DB (incorrect — AD is a side, DB is the diagonal), and BD = CB (incorrect). So the order must be maintained to get the correct correspondence of sides and angles.

Properties of a Rectangle — Inline Questions

Q: Are the opposite sides of a rectangle parallel?

Answer:
Yes. Consider AB as a transversal to AD and BC. The co-interior angles angle A + angle B = 90° + 90° = 180°. When the sum of co-interior angles on the same side of a transversal is 180°, the lines are parallel. Therefore, AD is parallel to BC.

Q: Can you similarly show that AB is parallel to DC (AB ∥ DC)?

Answer:
Yes. Consider AD as a transversal to AB and DC. The co-interior angles angle A + angle D = 90° + 90° = 180°. Since the sum of co-interior angles on the same side of the transversal is 180°, the lines AB and DC are parallel. Therefore, AB ∥ DC.

Square — Inline Questions

Q: Using this fact, construct a square with a diagonal of length 8 cm.

Answer:
Construction steps:
1. Draw a line segment AC of length 8 cm.
2. Find the midpoint O of AC (so AO = OC = 4 cm).
3. At point O, draw a perpendicular line to AC.
4. On this perpendicular line, mark points B and D such that OB = OD = 4 cm (since the diagonals of a square are equal and bisect each other).
5. Join A to B, B to C, C to D, and D to A.

The resulting quadrilateral ABCD is a square with diagonal 8 cm, because the diagonals are equal (both 8 cm), bisect each other (at O), and intersect at 90° (perpendicular).

Q: Verify if this is true by going through geometric reasoning in Deduction 1 and Deduction 2, and see if they apply to a square as well.

Answer:
Yes, both deductions apply to a square:

  • Deduction 1 (Diagonals are equal): Since a square is a rectangle, the same SAS congruence argument applies. AB = CD (all sides equal), angle BAD = angle CDA = 90°, AD is common. So triangle ADC is congruent to triangle DAB, giving AC = BD. The diagonals of a square are equal.

  • Deduction 2 (Diagonals bisect each other): The same AAS congruence argument applies. Triangle AOB is congruent to triangle COD, giving OA = OC and OB = OD. The diagonals of a square bisect each other.

Since a square is a special rectangle, all properties of rectangles automatically hold for squares.

Q: What are the measures of angle 1, angle 2, angle 3, and angle 4? (Angles made by the diagonal at the vertices of a square)

Answer:
In triangle ADC of square ABCD:
- angle D = 90° (angle of the square)
- AD = DC (all sides of a square are equal)
- So triangle ADC is isosceles with angle 1 = angle 3

angle 1 + angle 3 + 90 = 180

2(angle 1) = 90

angle 1 = angle 3 = 45°

Similarly, considering triangle ABC (or triangle ABD with diagonal AC):
- angle B = 90°
- AB = BC (all sides equal)
- So angle 2 = angle 4

angle 2 + angle 4 + 90 = 180

2(angle 2) = 90

angle 2 = angle 4 = 45°

So all four angles are 45° each. This shows that the diagonals of a square bisect the angles of the square.

Q2: Draw a quadrilateral whose diagonals have equal lengths of 8 cm that bisect each other, and intersect at an angle of (i) 30° (ii) 40° (iii) 90° (iv) 140°

Answer:

General construction steps for all parts:
1. Draw a line segment AC of length 8 cm.
2. Mark the midpoint O of AC (so AO = OC = 4 cm).
3. At point O, draw another line segment BD of length 8 cm at the specified angle to AC, with O as its midpoint (so BO = OD = 4 cm).
4. Join the endpoints: A to B, B to C, C to D, and D to A.
5. The resulting quadrilateral ABCD has diagonals of equal length (8 cm each) that bisect each other at the specified angle.

For each case:

(i) Angle between diagonals = 30°:
- Draw AC = 8 cm, mark midpoint O.
- At O, draw BD = 8 cm making a 30° angle with AC.
- Join A-B-C-D-A.
- The result is a rectangle (since diagonals are equal and bisect each other, as proven in the chapter). It will be a long, narrow rectangle.

(ii) Angle between diagonals = 40°:
- Same steps as above, but BD makes a 40° angle with AC.
- The result is again a rectangle, slightly less narrow than in (i).

(iii) Angle between diagonals = 90°:
- Same steps, but BD is perpendicular to AC (90° angle).
- Since the diagonals are equal, bisect each other, AND are perpendicular, the result is a square with diagonal 8 cm.

(iv) Angle between diagonals = 140°:
- Same steps, but BD makes a 140° angle with AC.
- The result is a rectangle (same shape as case (i) with 30°, since 180° - 140° = 40°, and the acute angle between the diagonals is 40°, giving the same rectangle as case (ii) but rotated).

Note: In all cases, since the diagonals are equal and bisect each other, the resulting quadrilateral is always a rectangle (as proven in the chapter). Only when the angle is 90° do we get a square.

Q3: Consider a circle with centre O. Line segments PL and AM are two perpendicular diameters of the circle. What is the figure APML? Reason and/or experiment to figure this out.

Answer:
The figure APML is a square.

Reasoning:
1. Since PL and AM are diameters of the same circle, they are equal in length (both equal to 2r, where r is the radius).
2. Since both are diameters, they pass through the centre O, so they bisect each other at O (OP = OL = r and OA = OM = r).
3. Since they are perpendicular, they intersect at 90°.

From the chapter, we know that if the diagonals of a quadrilateral are equal, bisect each other, and intersect at right angles (90°), the quadrilateral is a square.

Therefore, APML is a square.

Q4: We have seen how to get 90° using paper folding. Now, suppose we do not have any paper but two sticks of equal length, and a thread. How do we make an exact 90° using these?

Answer:
Method:
1. Take the two sticks of equal length. These will serve as the diagonals.
2. Place them so that they cross at their midpoints (i.e., they bisect each other). Since the sticks are of equal length, the diagonals are equal.
3. Use the thread to connect the four endpoints of the sticks in order, forming a quadrilateral.
4. Since the diagonals are equal and bisect each other, the quadrilateral formed is a rectangle (as proven in the chapter).
5. Each corner of this rectangle is exactly 90°.

You can now use any corner of this rectangle as your exact 90° angle.

Q5: We saw that one of the properties of a rectangle is that its opposite sides are parallel. Can this be chosen as a definition of a rectangle? In other words, is every quadrilateral that has opposite sides parallel and equal, a rectangle?

Answer:
No, not every quadrilateral with opposite sides parallel and equal is a rectangle.

A quadrilateral with opposite sides parallel and equal is called a parallelogram. While every rectangle is a parallelogram, not every parallelogram is a rectangle.

Counterexample: Consider a parallelogram with sides 4 cm and 6 cm, where the angles are 70° and 110°. Here:
- Opposite sides are equal (4 cm = 4 cm, 6 cm = 6 cm)
- Opposite sides are parallel
- But the angles are not 90°

So this quadrilateral has opposite sides parallel and equal, but it is not a rectangle.

For a parallelogram to be a rectangle, we additionally need all angles to be 90°. Therefore, "opposite sides parallel and equal" alone cannot serve as the definition of a rectangle.

4.2 Angles in a Quadrilateral

Q: Is it possible to construct a quadrilateral with three angles equal to 90° and the fourth angle not equal to 90°?

Answer:
No, it is not possible.

Reasoning: The sum of all angles in any quadrilateral is 360° (as proven by dividing the quadrilateral into two triangles using a diagonal, each triangle having angles summing to 180°, giving a total of 180° + 180° = 360°).

If three angles are 90° each, their sum is 3 x 90° = 270°.

The fourth angle = 360° - 270° = 90°.

So the fourth angle must also be 90°. It is impossible for a quadrilateral to have exactly three right angles with the fourth angle being different from 90°.

Q: (From the proof) What do we get when we add all six angles of the two triangles formed by drawing a diagonal in quadrilateral SOME?

Answer:
When we draw diagonal SM in quadrilateral SOME, we get two triangles: triangle SEM and triangle SOM.

  • In triangle SEM: angle 1 + angle 2 + angle 3 = 180°
  • In triangle SOM: angle 4 + angle 5 + angle 6 = 180°

Adding all six angles:
angle 1 + angle 2 + angle 3 + angle 4 + angle 5 + angle 6 = 180° + 180° = 360°

Now, (angle 1 + angle 4) is the full angle at vertex S, (angle 3 + angle 6) is the full angle at vertex M, angle 2 is the angle at vertex E, and angle 5 is the angle at vertex O.

These are exactly the four angles of the quadrilateral. Therefore:

The sum of all angles in any quadrilateral is 360°.

The Process of Finding Properties

As we have been seeing from lower grades, properties of geometric objects such as parallel lines, angles, and triangles can be deduced through geometric reasoning. We will continue to deduce properties of special types of quadrilaterals in this chapter.

Once you have deduced a property of a quadrilateral, it is good to verify it with a real-world quadrilateral, either the quadrilateral constructed on paper or simply a surface having the shape of the quadrilateral.

If you are not able to figure out the property using deduction, you could experiment by taking real-world quadrilaterals and observing the property through measurement. Note that these observations give useful insights about the property, but with them, we can only form a conjecture, that is, a statement about which we are highly confident, but not yet sure if it always holds true. For example, constructing a few rectangles and observing through measurement that their diagonals bisect each other does not necessarily mean that this will always be the case—can we be sure that the 1000th rectangle we construct will also have this property? The only way we can be sure of this property is by justifying or proving the statement, just as we did in Deduction 2.

Note to the Teacher: Gently encourage students to deduce or justify properties. Whenever students face challenges in doing it, encourage them to experiment and observe, and use their intuition to figure out the properties.

4.2 Angles in a Quadrilateral

? Is it possible to construct a quadrilateral with three angles equal to $90^{\circ}$ and the fourth angle not equal to $90^{\circ}$?

You might have observed through constructions that this may not be possible.

? But why not?

This is due to a general property of quadrilaterals related to their angles.

We have seen that the sum of the angles of a triangle is $180^{\circ}$. There is a similar regularity in the sum of the angles of a quadrilateral.

Quadrilaterals

Consider a quadrilateral SOME.
Draw a diagonal SM. We get two triangles $\Delta SEM$ and $\Delta SOM$.
In $\Delta SEM$, we have $\angle 1 + \angle 2 + \angle 3 = 180^{\circ}$.
And in $\Delta SOM$, $\angle 4 + \angle 5 + \angle 6 = 180^{\circ}$.
What do we get when we add all six angles?
We will have
$\angle 1 + \angle 2 + \angle 3 + \angle 4 + \angle 5 + \angle 6 = 180^{\circ} + 180^{\circ} = 360^{\circ}$.
Or, $(\angle 1 + \angle 4) + (\angle 3 + \angle 6) + \angle 2 + \angle 5 = 360^{\circ}$.
Since $(\angle 1 + \angle 4), (\angle 3 + \angle 6), \angle 2$ and $\angle 5$ are the angles of this quadrilateral, we have the following result—
The sum of all angles in any quadrilateral is $360^{\circ}$.
This explains why it is impossible for a quadrilateral to have three right angles, with the fourth angle not right angle.
