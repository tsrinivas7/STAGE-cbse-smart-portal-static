4 Quadrilaterals

Quadrilateral — A four-sided figure. The word is derived from Latin quadri meaning four and latus meaning sides.

The angles of a quadrilateral — The angles between its sides, as marked in the figures.

  • Figures (i), (ii) and (iii) are quadrilaterals (including convex and concave types), while a shape with a curved side and a five-sided shape are not quadrilaterals because they do not have four straight sides.
  • A quadrilateral is defined only by four straight sides meeting at four vertices; concave quadrilaterals with a reflex interior angle are also valid quadrilaterals.
  • This chapter focuses on special quadrilaterals, starting with the most familiar ones - rectangles and squares.

4.1 Rectangles and Squares

Rectangle — A rectangle is a quadrilateral in which (i) The angles are all right angles (90 degrees), and (ii) The opposite sides are of equal length.

  • The definition precisely states the conditions a quadrilateral must satisfy to be called a rectangle.
  • The chapter asks if there are other ways to define a rectangle, which is explored through the Carpenter's Problem using diagonals.
  • Understanding rectangles requires both angle properties and side properties together.

Deduction 1 — What is the length of the other diagonal?

Rectangle (diagonal property) — The diagonals of a rectangle always have the same length.

  • In rectangle ABCD, AB = CD, angle BAD = angle CDA = 90 degrees, and AD is common to triangles ADC and DAB.
  • By SAS congruence condition, triangle ADC is congruent to triangle DAB, so AC = BD as corresponding parts of congruent triangles.
  • Therefore if one strip is 8 cm long, the other strip must also be 8 cm long. This can be verified by constructing and measuring rectangles.
  • This establishes that equal length of diagonals is a necessary property of any rectangle.

The Process of Finding Properties

Conjecture — A statement about which we are highly confident, but not yet sure if it always holds true.

  • Properties of parallel lines, angles, triangles and quadrilaterals can be deduced through geometric reasoning.
  • After deduction, properties should be verified with a real quadrilateral constructed on paper or a real surface having that shape.
  • If deduction is challenging, experiment by measuring real quadrilaterals to observe patterns and form a conjecture.
  • Observation alone is not proof: measuring a few rectangles does not guarantee the 1000th rectangle will have the same property. Only justification or proof (as in Deduction 2) gives certainty.

Alternate Definitions of Rectangle

Rectangle (diagonal definition) — A rectangle is a quadrilateral whose diagonals are equal and bisect each other.

Rectangle (simplified definition) — A rectangle is a quadrilateral in which the angles are all 90 degrees.

  • Both definitions capture the same class of quadrilaterals but look very different from the original definition.
  • The first definition required both opposite sides equal and all angles 90 degrees; it can be simplified to just requiring all angles 90 degrees.

Deduction 4 — What is the shape of a quadrilateral with all angles 90 degrees?

  • Consider quadrilateral ABCD with all angles 90 degrees and join diagonal BD, forming triangles BAD and DCB.
  • Two equalities are directly visible: angle A = angle C = 90 degrees, and BD is common side. Need to show angle 1 = angle 2.
  • Using reasoning similar to Deduction 2: At vertex B, angle 3 + angle 1 = 90 degrees. In triangle BCD, angle 3 + angle 2 + 90 degrees = 180 degrees, so angle 3 + angle 2 = 90 degrees. Thus angle 1 = angle 2.
  • By AAS, triangle BAD is congruent to triangle DCB, therefore AD = CB and DC = BA. So opposite sides are automatically equal when all angles are 90 degrees.
  • Thus any quadrilateral with all angles 90 degrees satisfies the original rectangle definition.

Properties of a Rectangle

  • Property 1: All the angles of a rectangle are 90 degrees.
  • Property 2: The opposite sides of a rectangle are equal.
  • Property 3: The opposite sides of a rectangle are parallel to each other. Justification: AB is a transversal to AD and BC, angle A + angle B = 90 + 90 = 180 degrees. When sum of interior angles on same side of transversal is 180 degrees, lines are parallel, so AD is parallel to BC. Similarly AB is parallel to DC.
  • Property 4: The diagonals of a rectangle are of equal length and they bisect each other.

A Special Rectangle — Square

Square — A square is a quadrilateral in which all the angles are equal to 90 degrees, and all the sides are of equal length.

Venn diagram — In a Venn diagram, a set of objects is represented as points inside a closed curve. Typically these closed curves are ovals or circles. Each point in the region represents an object.

  • All four examples (i) 5 cm by 2 cm, (ii) 3.6 cm by 6 cm, (iii) 5 cm by 1 cm, and (iv) 4 cm by 4 cm are rectangles because all angles are 90 degrees. Example (iv) is special because all sides are equal.
  • Every square is also a rectangle, but every rectangle is not a square. This is similar to every Malayali is an Indian but not every Indian is a Malayali.
  • Venn diagram shows a large oval labelled Rectangle containing a smaller yellow circle labelled Square, indicating the set of squares is inside the set of rectangles.
  • Since square is a special rectangle, many rectangle properties automatically apply, but squares have extra properties.

Carpenter's Problem for Square and Deduction 5 — What should be the angle formed by the diagonals?

  • For a rectangle we needed diagonals (i) equal lengths and (ii) bisect each other. For a square we need additionally equal side lengths, achieved by choosing the correct angle between diagonals.
  • Suppose equal diagonals bisect each other to form square ABCD. Consider triangles BOA and BOC: OA = OC (bisected equal diagonals), OB is common side, AB = BC (sides of square). By SSS, triangle BOA is congruent to triangle BOC.
  • Therefore angle BOA = angle BOC as corresponding parts. These two angles form a linear pair, so angle BOA + angle BOC = 180 degrees. Hence each must be 90 degrees.
  • This shows the diagonals of a square bisect each other at right angles. To make a square, diagonals must be equal, bisect each other at 90 degrees.

Properties of a Square

  • Property 1: All the sides of a square are equal to each other.
  • Property 2: The opposite sides of a square are parallel to each other.
  • Property 3: The angles of a square are all 90 degrees.
  • Property 4: The diagonals of a square are of equal length and they bisect each other at 90 degrees.
  • Property 5: The diagonals of a square divide the angles of the square into equal halves. The diagonals bisect the angles of the square. Reasoning: In triangle ADC, angle 1 + angle 3 + 90 = 180 and AD = DC so angle 1 = angle 3, thus angle 1 = angle 3 = 45 degrees. Similarly angle 2 and angle 4 are 45 degrees.

4.2 Angles in a Quadrilateral

The sum of all angles in any quadrilateral is 360 degrees.

  • Consider quadrilateral SOME with diagonal SM forming triangles SEM and SOM. In triangle SEM, angle 1 + angle 2 + angle 3 = 180 degrees. In triangle SOM, angle 4 + angle 5 + angle 6 = 180 degrees.
  • Adding all six angles: angle 1 + angle 2 + angle 3 + angle 4 + angle 5 + angle 6 = 180 + 180 = 360 degrees. Regrouping: (angle 1 + angle 4) + (angle 3 + angle 6) + angle 2 + angle 5 = 360 degrees, which are the four interior angles of quadrilateral SOME.
  • Therefore any quadrilateral has angle sum 360 degrees, regardless of shape (convex or concave).
  • This explains why a quadrilateral cannot have three right angles and a fourth non-right angle: if three are 90 degrees, the fourth must be 360 - 90 - 90 - 90 = 90 degrees as well.

Key Terms

AAS congruence condition — two triangles are congruent if two angles and a non-included side of one equal those of the other
Bisect — to divide a quantity such as a segment or angle into two equal parts
Conjecture — a statement believed to be true from observation or experiment but not yet proved
Corresponding parts of congruent triangles — sides and angles in same relative positions in congruent triangles that are equal
Diagonal — a line segment joining opposite vertices of a quadrilateral
Interior angles — angles inside a polygon formed between two adjacent sides
Isosceles triangle — a triangle with two equal sides and angles opposite those sides equal
Linear pair — pair of adjacent angles on a straight line whose sum is 180 degrees
Midpoint — the point that divides a line segment into two equal halves
Opposite sides — sides of a quadrilateral that do not share a common vertex
Parallel lines — lines that never intersect; a transversal cutting them makes interior angles on same side sum to 180 degrees
Quadrilateral — a closed four-sided figure with four vertices, four sides and four interior angles
Rectangle — a quadrilateral in which all angles are 90 degrees and opposite sides are equal; equivalently a quadrilateral whose diagonals are equal and bisect each other
Right angle — an angle measuring 90 degrees
SAS congruence condition — two triangles are congruent if two sides and the included angle of one equal those of the other
Square — a quadrilateral with all angles 90 degrees and all sides equal; a special rectangle whose diagonals are equal and bisect each other at 90 degrees and also bisect the angles
SSS congruence condition — two triangles are congruent if all three sides of one equal all three sides of the other
Transversal — a line that intersects two or more other lines
Venn diagram — a diagram using closed curves like circles or ovals to show relationships between sets
Vertically opposite angles — pair of equal angles formed opposite each other when two lines intersect

A Carpenter's Problem

Question: A carpenter needs to put together two thin strips of wood, as shown in Fig. 1, so that when a thread is passed through their endpoints, it forms a rectangle. She already has one 8 cm long strip. What should be the length of the other strip? Where should they both be joined?

Answer:
Let the strips be the diagonals AC and BD of quadrilateral ABCD fig_p83_01_vlm.

  1. What is the length of the other diagonal? It must be 8 cm as well. Deduction 1 proves diagonals of a rectangle are always equal. So BD = AC = 8 cm.
  2. What is the point of intersection of the two diagonals? They must intersect at their mid-points. That is, point O must be the midpoint of both AC and BD, so AO = OC = 4 cm and BO = OD = 4 cm. In other words, the strips must be joined at their centres so that they bisect each other fig_p83_02_vlm.
  3. What should the angle be between the diagonals? Any angle will do. Deduction 3 proves that if two equal diagonals bisect each other, the quadrilateral formed is always a rectangle whatever be the angle x between them. You can choose 30 degrees, 60 degrees, 90 degrees etc. and you will still get a rectangle.

In short: Take another strip of 8 cm and join the two strips exactly at their middle points (4 cm from each end). The angle between them can be anything.

How it is used in practice: This method is used by carpenters in Europe to make rectangular frames and by farmers in Mozambique to lay the rectangular base of a house.

Deduction 1 - What is the length of the other diagonal?

Question and Proof:
Suppose ABCD is a rectangle with AC = 8 cm fig_p83_02_vlm. To prove AC = BD:

  • AB = CD (opposite sides of rectangle are equal) - shown with tick marks
  • angle BAD = angle CDA = 90 degrees (all angles are 90 degrees)
  • AD is common side

Math Talk Question: Can the following equalities be used to establish that triangle AOD is congruent to triangle COB?
AO = CO (proved above)
angle AOB = angle COD (vertically opposite angles)
AD = CB

Answer: No, this is not a correct way to prove congruence.
- For triangles AOD and COB, the angles that are included between the known sides are angle AOD and angle COB, which are vertically opposite and equal, not angle AOB and angle COD.
- Angle AOB is not an angle of triangle AOD at all.

Correct set for SAS would be:
AO = CO, OD = OB (proved that diagonals bisect each other), and angle AOD = angle COB (vertically opposite angles). Therefore triangle AOD is congruent to triangle COB by SAS. Similarly we can use AAS by taking angle OAD = angle OCB etc.

Question: Can we now identify what type of quadrilateral ABCD is? What can we say about its sides?

Answer:
Since every corner angle is 90 degrees, ABCD satisfies the definition of a rectangle (all angles 90 degrees).

About sides: Triangles AOB is congruent to COD (by SAS: OA=OC, OB=OD, included angles 60 degrees equal) and triangles AOD is congruent to COB (included angles 120 degrees equal). So corresponding sides AB = CD and AD = CB. Hence opposite sides are equal. Therefore ABCD is indeed a rectangle.

Question: Will ABCD remain a rectangle if the angles between the diagonals are changed? Can we generalise this? Take one of the angles between the diagonals as x.

Answer: Yes, it will always remain a rectangle for any value of x.

General proof with x:
Let angle AOB = x (and angle COD = x by vertically opposite). Then the other two angles angle AOD = angle BOC = 180 - x (linear pair).

  • In isosceles triangle AOB, let base angles be a.
    a + a + x = 180, so 2a = 180 - x, so a = (180 - x)/2 = 90 - x/2.
  • In isosceles triangle AOD, let base angles be b.
    b + b + (180 - x) = 180, so 2b = x, so b = x/2.

Each corner angle of the quadrilateral = a + b = (90 - x/2) + x/2 = 90 degrees.

Thus all four angles are 90 degrees irrespective of x. Also as before AB = CD and AD = CB by congruence. Hence the quadrilateral is a rectangle for any x. The only requirement is that diagonals are equal and bisect each other.

Deduction 4 - What is the shape of a quadrilateral with all angles equal to 90 degrees?

Question: Consider a quadrilateral ABCD with all angles measuring 90 degrees. What can we say about the opposite sides? Join BD. Triangles BAD and DCB seem congruent. Can we justify this claim?

Answer:
Yes. Join diagonal BD. Consider triangles BAD and DCB.

  • angle A = angle C = 90 degrees (given)
  • BD is common side
  • Need to show angle 1 (angle ABD) = angle 2 (angle CDB).
  • Since angle B = 90 degrees, angle 3 + angle 1 = 90 degrees.
  • In triangle BCD, angle 3 + angle 2 + 90 degrees = 180 degrees, so angle 3 + angle 2 = 90 degrees.
  • Therefore angle 1 = angle 2.

So by AAS condition, triangle BAD is congruent to triangle DCB. Therefore corresponding sides AD = CB and DC = BA.

Question: Is it wrong to write triangle BAD is congruent to triangle CDB? Why?

Answer: Yes, writing triangle BAD is congruent to triangle CDB with that order can be considered wrong if the correspondence is not maintained. Congruent notation must match corresponding vertices in order. The correct correspondence is BAD corresponds to DCB:
B corresponds to D, A corresponds to C, D corresponds to B.
So we should write triangle BAD is congruent to triangle DCB. Writing triangle BAD is congruent to triangle CDB matches B->C, A->D, D->B which is not the correct matching of equal angles and sides. Order matters to show which angle equals which.

Conclusion: If all angles of a quadrilateral are 90 degrees, then opposite sides are automatically equal. So we can simplify the definition.

Simplified Definition: Rectangle is a quadrilateral in which all angles are 90 degrees.

Properties of a Rectangle (summary)

Property 1: All angles of a rectangle are 90 degrees.
Property 2: Opposite sides of a rectangle are equal.
Question: Are the opposite sides of a rectangle parallel?

Answer: Yes.

Proof: Consider AD and BC with transversal AB.
angle A + angle B = 90 + 90 = 180 degrees. When sum of interior angles on the same side of transversal is 180 degrees, the two lines are parallel. So AD is parallel to BC (written as AD || BC). Similarly, considering AB and DC with transversal AD, angle A + angle D = 180 degrees, so AB || DC. Thus opposite sides are parallel to each other.

Property 3: Opposite sides of a rectangle are parallel to each other.
Property 4: Diagonals of a rectangle are of equal length and they bisect each other.

A Special Rectangle

Question: In the quadrilaterals below, are there any non-rectangles? (i) 5 cm by 2 cm tilted rectangle, (ii) 3.6 cm by 6 cm tall rectangle, (iii) 5 cm by 1 cm thin tilted rectangle, (iv) 4 cm by 4 cm square. All have right-angle marks at corners.

Answer:
All four quadrilaterals are rectangles, including (iv), because every angle is 90 degrees which satisfies the definition of a rectangle. Quadrilateral (iv) is a special kind of rectangle where all sides are of equal length. This special rectangle is called a Square.

Square is a quadrilateral in which all angles are 90 degrees, and all sides are of equal length. Thus every square is also a rectangle, but every rectangle is not a square.

Square - Carpenter's Problem for Square

Question: Let us consider the Carpenter's Problem again. If the wooden strips have to be placed so that the thread passing through their endpoints forms a square, what must be done? What more needs to be done to get equal side lengths as well? Can this be achieved by properly choosing the angle between the diagonals?

Answer:
To get a quadrilateral with all angles 90 degrees and opposite sides equal, we already know diagonals must be equal and bisect each other. To make all sides equal as well (to become a square), we need one more condition on the angle between the diagonals.

Deduction 5 - What should be the angle formed by the diagonals?

Suppose ABCD is a square with equal diagonals bisecting each other at O. Consider triangles BOA and BOC.

  • OA = OC (diagonals bisect each other and are equal, so all four half-diagonals OA = OB = OC = OD)
  • AB = BC (all sides of square are equal) - shown with tick marks
  • OB is common side

So by SSS condition, triangle BOA is congruent to triangle BOC.

Question: Can this be used to find angle BOA and angle BOC?

Answer: Yes. Since the triangles are congruent, corresponding angles angle BOA = angle BOC. But these two angles together form a straight line angle AOC = 180 degrees. So angle BOA + angle BOC = 180 degrees. Since they are equal, each must be 180/2 = 90 degrees. The same reasoning shows all four angles around O are 90 degrees.

Conclusion: Diagonals of a square bisect each other at right angles (90 degrees).

Final construction rule for a square: The wooden strips must be (i) of equal lengths, (ii) bisect each other (join at their midpoints), and (iii) be placed at right angles (90 degrees) to each other. If you join endpoints, you get a square. For an 8 cm diagonal, put two 8 cm strips crossing at 4 cm from each end and perpendicular to each other.

Question: Using this fact, construct a square with a diagonal of length 8 cm.

Answer:
1. Draw a line segment AC = 8 cm and mark its midpoint O (AO = OC = 4 cm).
2. At O, draw a line BD perpendicular to AC, with BO = OD = 4 cm, so BD = 8 cm as well.
3. Join AB, BC, CD, DA. ABCD is the required square.

Properties of a Square (summary)

Since a square is a special type of rectangle, all properties of rectangle hold, plus two extra:

Property 1: All sides of a square are equal to each other.
Property 2: Opposite sides of a square are parallel to each other.
Property 3: All angles of a square are 90 degrees.
Property 4: Diagonals of a square are of equal length and they bisect each other at 90 degrees (at right angles).
Property 5: Diagonals of a square bisect the angles of the square (divide each 90 degree angle into two 45 degree angles).

Question: What are the measures of angle 1, angle 2, angle 3, and angle 4 in square ABCD with diagonal AC?

Answer:
In triangle ADC, angle D = 90 degrees, AD = DC (sides of square), so base angles angle 1 and angle 3 are equal.
angle 1 + angle 3 + 90 = 180 (sum of triangle angles)
So angle 1 = angle 3, and 2 * angle 1 = 90, so angle 1 = 45 degrees, angle 3 = 45 degrees.
Similarly in triangle ABC, angle 2 = angle 4 = 45 degrees.
Thus diagonal AC divides angle A (90 degrees) into 45 + 45, and angle C into 45 + 45. Same for the other diagonal. Hence diagonals bisect the angles.

5. We saw that one of the properties of a rectangle is that its opposite sides are parallel. Can this be chosen as a definition of a rectangle? In other words, is every quadrilateral that has opposite sides parallel and equal, a rectangle?

Answer: No.

Explanation:
- A quadrilateral with opposite sides parallel is called a parallelogram.
- If a parallelogram also has opposite sides equal, it is still just a parallelogram - it does not have to be a rectangle.
- For a rectangle we need all angles to be 90 degrees. A parallelogram can have angles like 60 degrees and 120 degrees and still have opposite sides parallel and equal (this shape is a general parallelogram, or if all sides equal, a rhombus).
- For example, you can push a rectangle sideways to make a slanted shape - opposite sides remain parallel and equal, but angles are no longer 90 degrees, so it is no longer a rectangle.

Therefore, "opposite sides are parallel and equal" cannot be used as a definition of a rectangle. It defines a parallelogram.

4.2 Angles in a Quadrilateral

Question: Is it possible to construct a quadrilateral with three angles equal to 90 degrees and the fourth angle not equal to 90 degrees? Why not?

Answer: It is impossible. This is because of the angle sum property of quadrilaterals.

Proof that sum of all angles in any quadrilateral is 360 degrees:

Consider quadrilateral SOME. Draw diagonal SM, dividing it into two triangles: triangle SEM and triangle SOM.

  • In triangle SEM: angle 1 (at S inside SEM) + angle 2 (at E) + angle 3 (at M inside SEM) = 180 degrees.
  • In triangle SOM: angle 4 (at S inside SOM) + angle 5 (at O) + angle 6 (at M inside SOM) = 180 degrees.

Adding all six angles:
angle 1 + angle 2 + angle 3 + angle 4 + angle 5 + angle 6 = 180 + 180 = 360 degrees.

But angle at S = angle 1 + angle 4, angle at E = angle 2, angle at M = angle 3 + angle 6, angle at O = angle 5.

So (angle at S) + (angle at E) + (angle at M) + (angle at O) = 360 degrees.

Thus sum of all interior angles of any quadrilateral is 360 degrees.

Why three right angles force the fourth to be right:

If three angles are 90 degrees each, their sum = 90 + 90 + 90 = 270 degrees.
Since total sum must be 360 degrees, the fourth angle = 360 - 270 = 90 degrees.

So the fourth angle cannot be anything other than 90 degrees. You cannot have a quadrilateral with exactly three right angles - either it has four right angles (rectangle) or fewer.

Note: This property holds for any quadrilateral, whether convex or concave, as long as it has four straight sides.

The Process of Finding Properties

As we have been seeing from lower grades, properties of geometric objects such as parallel lines, angles, and triangles can be deduced through geometric reasoning. We will continue to deduce properties of special types of quadrilaterals in this chapter.

Once you have deduced a property of a quadrilateral, it is good to verify it with a real-world quadrilateral, either the quadrilateral constructed on paper or simply a surface having the shape of the quadrilateral.

If you are not able to figure out the property using deduction, you could experiment by taking real-world quadrilaterals and observing the property through measurement. Note that these observations give useful insights about the property, but with them, we can only form a conjecture, that is, a statement about which we are highly confident, but not yet sure if it always holds true. For example, constructing a few rectangles and observing through measurement that their diagonals bisect each other does not necessarily mean that this will always be the case—can we be sure that the 1000th rectangle we construct will also have this property? The only way we can be sure of this property is by justifying or proving the statement, just as we did in Deduction 2.

Note to the Teacher: Gently encourage students to deduce or justify properties. Whenever students face challenges in doing it, encourage them to experiment and observe, and use their intuition to figure out the properties.

4.2 Angles in a Quadrilateral

? Is it possible to construct a quadrilateral with three angles equal to 90° and the fourth angle not equal to 90°?

You might have observed through constructions that this may not be possible.

? But why not?

This is due to a general property of quadrilaterals related to their angles.

We have seen that the sum of the angles of a triangle is 180°. There is a similar regularity in the sum of the angles of a quadrilateral.

Quadrilaterals

Consider a quadrilateral SOME.
Draw a diagonal SM. We get two triangles $\Delta SEM$ and $\Delta SOM$.
In $\Delta SEM$, we have $\angle 1 + \angle 2 + \angle 3 = 180^{\circ}$.
And in $\Delta SOM$, $\angle 4 + \angle 5 + \angle 6 = 180^{\circ}$.
What do we get when we add all six angles?
We will have
$\angle 1 + \angle 2 + \angle 3 + \angle 4 + \angle 5 + \angle 6 = 180^{\circ} + 180^{\circ}$
$= 360^{\circ}$.
Or, $(\angle 1 + \angle 4) + (\angle 3 + \angle 6) + \angle 2 + \angle 5 = 360^{\circ}$.
Since $(\angle 1 + \angle 4)$, $(\angle 3 + \angle 6)$, $\angle 2$ and $\angle 5$ are the angles of this quadrilateral, we have the following result—
The sum of all angles in any quadrilateral is $360^{\circ}$.
This explains why it is impossible for a quadrilateral to have three right angles, with the fourth angle not right angle.
