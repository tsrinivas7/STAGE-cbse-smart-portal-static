4 QUADRILATERALS

In this chapter, we study interesting types of four-sided figures known as quadrilaterals. The word 'quadrilateral' comes from Latin words quadri meaning four, and latus referring to sides.

Quadrilateral — A closed figure made up of four line segments (sides) such that no three vertices are collinear.

  • Figs. (i), (ii), and (iii) in the introduction show convex and concave quadrilaterals where angles are marked between sides.
  • Figures with curved sides or five sides (pentagons) are not quadrilaterals.
  • The angles of a quadrilateral are the interior angles between its adjacent sides.

4.1 Rectangles and Squares

We begin with the most familiar quadrilaterals—rectangles and squares.

Definition of Rectangle

Rectangle — A quadrilateral in which:
(i) The angles are all right angles (90°), and
(ii) The opposite sides are of equal length.

This definition precisely states the conditions a quadrilateral has to satisfy to be called a rectangle.

  • A rectangle can also be defined as a quadrilateral whose diagonals are equal and bisect each other

- We can simplify the definition: A rectangle is a quadrilateral in which all the angles are 90°.

Definition of Square

A special kind of rectangle has all sides of equal length.

Square — A quadrilateral in which all the angles are equal to 90°, and all the sides are of equal length.

  • Every square is also a rectangle, but every rectangle is not a square.
  • A square is a special case where the rectangle's opposite sides are not just equal, but all four sides are equal.
  • This relationship can be represented using a Venn diagram, where the set of squares lies entirely within the set of rectangles.

Properties of Square

Since a square is a special type of rectangle, all properties of a rectangle hold true for a square. Additionally, there are special properties unique to squares.

Property 1 — All the sides of a square are equal to each other.
Property 2 — The opposite sides of a square are parallel to each other.
Property 3 — The angles of a square are all 90°.
Property 4 — The diagonals of a square are of equal length and they bisect each other at 90°.
Property 5 — The diagonals of a square divide the angles of the square into equal halves (45° each).

4.2 Angles in a Quadrilateral

Is it possible to construct a quadrilateral with three angles equal to 90° and the fourth angle not equal to 90°? No, this is impossible due to a general property related to angles.

The sum of all angles in any quadrilateral is 360°.

  • We can draw a diagonal SM in a quadrilateral SOME to get two triangles: Triangle SEM and Triangle SOM.
  • The sum of angles in Triangle SEM is 180°.
  • The sum of angles in Triangle SOM is 180°.
  • Adding all six angles gives 180° + 180° = 360°.
  • This explains why a quadrilateral cannot have three right angles; the fourth must also be 90° to sum to 360°.

Question: Can the following equalities be used to establish that triangle AOD is congruent to triangle COB?

2. Linear Pair Angles: angle AOD = 180 - 60 = 120 degrees.

Question: In triangle AOB, since OA = OB, the angles opposite them are equal, say a. Can you find the value of a?
Answer:
In triangle AOB:
* Sum of angles = 180 degrees.
* angle AOB = 60 degrees.

Question: Will ABCD remain a rectangle if the angles between the diagonals are changed? Can we generalise this? Take one of the angles between the diagonals as x.
Answer:
Yes, ABCD will remain a rectangle.
* If diagonals are equal and bisect each other, the opposite sides are equal (from congruence of triangles formed).
* The sum of adjacent angles becomes 90 degrees (calculated as 90 - x/2 + x/2 = 90).
* Thus, all four angles of the quadrilateral become 90 degrees.
* Therefore, the quadrilateral is a rectangle regardless of the angle x between the diagonals.

Question: Deduction 4 — What is the shape of a quadrilateral with all the angles equal to 90 degrees? Is it wrong to write triangle BAD is congruent to triangle CDB? Why?
Answer:
* Shape: A quadrilateral with all angles equal to 90 degrees is a rectangle.
* Congruence: Yes, it is wrong to write triangle BAD is congruent to triangle CDB.
* Correct correspondence: Triangle BAD is congruent to Triangle DCB.
* Reason: Vertex B corresponds to Vertex D, and Vertex D corresponds to Vertex B in the second triangle due to the shared diagonal BD and the right angles at A and C. Writing CDB implies vertex C corresponds to B, which is incorrect orientation for the congruence statement relative to the diagonal BD.

Question: Are the opposite sides of a rectangle parallel? Can you similarly show that AB is parallel to DC?
Answer:
Yes, the opposite sides of a rectangle are parallel.
* Reasoning: Consider transversal AB intersecting lines AD and BC.
* Angle A + Angle B = 90 degrees + 90 degrees = 180 degrees.
* When the sum of internal angles on the same side of the transversal is 180 degrees, the lines are parallel.
* Therefore, AD is parallel to BC (AD || BC).
* Similarly, considering transversal AD, Angle A + Angle D = 180 degrees, so AB is parallel to DC (AB || DC).

Question: Let us consider the Carpenter's Problem again. If the wooden strips have to be placed such that the thread passing through their endpoints forms a square, what must be done?
Answer:
To form a square using the wooden strips (diagonals):
1. The strips must be of equal length.

Question: Deduction 5 — What should be the angle formed by the diagonals?
Answer:
The angle formed by the diagonals of a square must be 90 degrees.
* Reasoning: In a square, all sides are equal. Triangles BOA and BOC are congruent by SSS condition (BO is common, OA = OC, AB = BC).
* Therefore, angle BOA = angle BOC.
* Since they form a straight line, angle BOA + angle BOC = 180 degrees.
* Thus, angle BOA = angle BOC = 90 degrees.

Question: Construct a square with a diagonal of length 8 cm.
Answer:
Steps to construct:
1. Draw a line segment AC of length 8 cm.
2. Find the midpoint O of AC.
3. Draw a line perpendicular to AC passing through O.
4. Mark points B and D on this perpendicular line such that OB = OD = 4 cm (half of diagonal).
5. Join AB, BC, CD, and DA.
6. ABCD is the required square.

Question: What are the measures of angle 1, angle 2, angle 3, and angle 4? (Diagonals of a square divide angles into equal halves).
Answer:
In a square ABCD with diagonal AC:
* Angle 1 (angle DAC) = 45 degrees.
* Angle 2 (angle BAC) = 45 degrees.
* Angle 3 (angle BCA) = 45 degrees.
* Angle 4 (angle DCA) = 45 degrees.
* Reasoning: In triangle ADC, angle D = 90 degrees. Since AD = DC, angle 1 = angle 3. Sum of angles = 180 degrees. So angle 1 + angle 3 + 90 = 180 => 2 * angle 1 = 90 => angle 1 = 45 degrees. Similarly for others.

Answer:
Since the diagonals are equal and bisect each other, the resulting quadrilateral in all cases will be a rectangle.

* (ii) 40 degrees: Same method, angle AOB = 40 degrees. Result is a rectangle.

Question 3: Consider a circle with centre O. Line segments PL and AM are two perpendicular diameters of the circle. What is the figure APML?
Answer:
The figure APML is a square.
* Reasoning: Diameters bisect each other at the centre O. So APML has diagonals AL and PM that bisect each other.
* Diameters are equal in length. So diagonals are equal.
* Diameters are perpendicular. So diagonals intersect at 90 degrees.
* A quadrilateral with equal diagonals that bisect each other at 90 degrees is a square.

Question 4: We have seen how to get 90 degrees using paper folding. Now, suppose we do not have any paper but two sticks of equal length, and a thread. How do we make an exact 90 degrees using these?
Answer:
1. Tie the two sticks together at their midpoints using the thread.
2. Ensure the sticks cross each other at the midpoint.
3. Adjust the angle between them until the distance between the ends of the sticks (forming a quadrilateral) creates a square shape (all sides equal).
4. Alternatively, if you want just the angle: Place the sticks crossing at the midpoint. Measure the distance between the endpoints. If the distance equals the length of half the diagonal times sqrt(2) (conceptually), or simply ensure the resulting quadrilateral formed by connecting endpoints is a square, the angle is 90 degrees. Practically, forming a square with the sticks as diagonals guarantees the angle is 90 degrees.

Question 5: We saw that one of the properties of a rectangle is that its opposite sides are parallel. Can this be chosen as a definition of a rectangle? In other words, is every quadrilateral that has opposite sides parallel and equal, a rectangle?
Answer:
No, not every quadrilateral with opposite sides parallel and equal is a rectangle.
* Such a quadrilateral is a parallelogram.
* A parallelogram does not necessarily have 90-degree angles.
* To be a rectangle, it must additionally have all angles equal to 90 degrees (or diagonals equal in length).

Section 4.2: Angles in a Quadrilateral

Question: Is it possible to construct a quadrilateral with three angles equal to 90 degrees and the fourth angle not equal to 90 degrees?
Answer:
No, it is not possible.
* Reasoning: The sum of all angles in any quadrilateral is 360 degrees.
* If three angles are 90 degrees, their sum is 90 + 90 + 90 = 270 degrees.
* The fourth angle must be 360 - 270 = 90 degrees.
* Therefore, the fourth angle must also be 90 degrees. You cannot have exactly three right angles in a quadrilateral without the fourth being a right angle as well.

Question: Prove that the sum of all angles in any quadrilateral is 360 degrees.
Answer:
Proof:
1. Consider a quadrilateral SOME.
2. Draw a diagonal SM.
3. This divides the quadrilateral into two triangles: triangle SEM and triangle SOM.
4. In triangle SEM, sum of angles = 180 degrees (angle 1 + angle 2 + angle 3 = 180).
5. In triangle SOM, sum of angles = 180 degrees (angle 4 + angle 5 + angle 6 = 180).
6. Adding both equations: (angle 1 + angle 2 + angle 3) + (angle 4 + angle 5 + angle 6) = 180 + 180 = 360 degrees.
7. Grouping the angles: (angle 1 + angle 4) + (angle 3 + angle 6) + angle 2 + angle 5 = 360 degrees.
8. Here, (angle 1 + angle 4), (angle 3 + angle 6), angle 2, and angle 5 represent the four interior angles of the quadrilateral.
9. Therefore, the sum of all angles in any quadrilateral is 360 degrees.

The Process of Finding Properties

As we have been seeing from lower grades, properties of geometric objects such as parallel lines, angles, and triangles can be deduced through geometric reasoning. We will continue to deduce properties of special types of quadrilaterals in this chapter.

Once you have deduced a property of a quadrilateral, it is good to verify it with a real-world quadrilateral, either the quadrilateral constructed on paper or simply a surface having the shape of the quadrilateral.

If you are not able to figure out the property using deduction, you could experiment by taking real-world quadrilaterals and observing the property through measurement. Note that these observations give useful insights about the property, but with them, we can only form a conjecture, that is, a statement about which we are highly confident, but not yet sure if it always holds true. For example, constructing a few rectangles and observing through measurement that their diagonals bisect each other does not necessarily mean that this will always be the case—can we be sure that the 1000th rectangle we construct will also have this property? The only way we can be sure of this property is by justifying or proving the statement, just as we did in Deduction 2.

4.2 Angles in a Quadrilateral

? Is it possible to construct a quadrilateral with three angles equal to 90° and the fourth angle not equal to 90°?

You might have observed through constructions that this may not be possible.

? But why not?

This is due to a general property of quadrilaterals related to their angles.

We have seen that the sum of the angles of a triangle is 180°. There is a similar regularity in the sum of the angles of a quadrilateral.

Quadrilaterals

Consider a quadrilateral SOME.
Draw a diagonal SM. We get two triangles $\Delta SEM$ and $\Delta SOM$.
In $\Delta SEM$, we have $\angle 1 + \angle 2 + \angle 3 = 180^{\circ}$.
And in $\Delta SOM$, $\angle 4 + \angle 5 + \angle 6 = 180^{\circ}$.
What do we get when we add all six angles?
We will have
$\angle 1 + \angle 2 + \angle 3 + \angle 4 + \angle 5 + \angle 6 = 180^{\circ} + 180^{\circ}$
$= 360^{\circ}$.
Or, $(\angle 1 + \angle 4) + (\angle 3 + \angle 6) + \angle 2 + \angle 5 = 360^{\circ}$.
Since $(\angle 1 + \angle 4), (\angle 3 + \angle 6), \angle 2$ and $\angle 5$ are the angles of this quadrilateral, we have the following result—
The sum of all angles in any quadrilateral is $360^{\circ}$.
This explains why it is impossible for a quadrilateral to have three right angles, with the fourth angle not right angle.
