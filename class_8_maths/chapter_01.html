A Square and A Cube — Complete Chapter Summary

Chapter Summary

Introduction: The Locker Puzzle

  • Queen Ratnamanjuri left a puzzle in her will for her son Khoisnam and 99 relatives involving 100 lockers.
  • Each person (numbered 1 to 100) toggles lockers that are multiples of their assigned number: Person 1 opens every locker, Person 2 toggles every 2nd locker, Person 3 toggles every 3rd locker, and so on.
  • A locker that is toggled an odd number of times remains open; a locker toggled an even number of times ends up closed.
  • The number of times a locker is toggled equals the number of factors of its locker number.
  • Lockers whose numbers are square numbers (1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100) remain open because squares have an odd number of factors.
  • The lockers toggled exactly twice are the prime numbers (2, 3, 5, 7, 11), since each prime has exactly two factors: 1 and itself.

1.1 Square Numbers

Square number (or square) — a number that can be expressed as the product of a number with itself (e.g., n * n = n^2).

Perfect squares — the squares of natural numbers. For example, 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, ... are all perfect squares.

  • The area of a square with side length n units is n * n = n^2 square units, which is why these numbers are called "squares."
  • The notation n^2 is read as "n squared" and means n multiplied by itself.
  • Squares can also be computed for fractions and decimals: (3/5)^2 = (3/5) * (3/5) = 9/25, and (2.5)^2 = 2.5 * 2.5 = 6.25.
  • Every factor of a number has a "partner factor" so that their product equals the number. For example, for 6: 1 * 6 and 2 * 3 are partner factor pairs.
  • Only square numbers have an odd number of factors because one of their factor pairs consists of the same number repeated (e.g., for 4: 2 * 2, for 9: 3 * 3, for 36: 6 * 6).

Patterns and Properties of Perfect Squares

  • The units (last) digit of a perfect square can only be 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, or 9. No perfect square ends with 2, 3, 7, or 8.
  • If a number ends with 2, 3, 7, or 8, it is definitely not a perfect square.
  • However, a number ending in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, or 9 is not necessarily a perfect square (e.g., 26 ends in 6 but is not a square).
  • If a number has 1 or 9 in its units place, its square ends in 1 (e.g., 1^2 = 1, 9^2 = 81, 11^2 = 121, 19^2 = 361).
  • If a number ends with n zeros, its square ends with 2n zeros. Squares can only have an even number of zeros at the end.
  • A number and its square always share the same parity (odd numbers have odd squares, even numbers have even squares).

Perfect Squares and Odd Numbers

  • The difference between consecutive perfect squares gives consecutive odd numbers: 4 - 1 = 3, 9 - 4 = 5, 16 - 9 = 7, 25 - 16 = 9, and so on.
  • Adding consecutive odd numbers starting from 1 gives consecutive square numbers:
  • 1 = 1 = 1^2
  • 1 + 3 = 4 = 2^2
  • 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 = 3^2
  • 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16 = 4^2
  • 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 = 25 = 5^2
  • The sum of the first n odd numbers is n^2. This can be visualized using dot diagrams where each new inverted-L shape (gnomon) adds the next odd number of dots.
  • A number is a perfect square if, by successively subtracting odd numbers starting from 1, we reach exactly 0. The number of subtractions gives the square root. For example, 25 - 1 - 3 - 5 - 7 - 9 = 0 (5 steps), so sqrt(25) = 5.
  • A natural number is not a perfect square if it cannot be expressed as a sum of successive odd natural numbers starting from 1.

Perfect Squares and Triangular Numbers

Triangular numbers — numbers that can be represented as dots arranged in an equilateral triangle. The first few are 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, ...

  • The sum of two consecutive triangular numbers gives a perfect square:
  • 1 + 3 = 4 = 2^2
  • 3 + 6 = 9 = 3^2
  • 6 + 10 = 16 = 4^2
  • 10 + 15 = 25 = 5^2
  • This relationship can be visualized by combining two triangular dot arrangements to form a square dot arrangement.
  • The number of squares between consecutive blocks of 100 decreases as numbers grow larger (e.g., there are 10 squares between 1-100, but fewer between 101-200, and so on).

Square Roots

Square root — if y = x^2, then x is the square root of y. Every perfect square has two integer square roots: one positive and one negative. The positive square root is denoted by the symbol sqrt().

  • For example, sqrt(49) = 7 (since 7 * 7 = 49), and sqrt(64) = +8 or -8 (since both 8^2 = 64 and (-8)^2 = 64).
  • In general, sqrt(n^2) = +n or -n. In this chapter, only the positive square root is considered.
  • Method 1 — Listing squares: List square numbers in sequence until you find or exceed the given number. This becomes inefficient for larger numbers.
  • Method 2 — Successive subtraction of odd numbers: Subtract consecutive odd numbers from 1 starting at 1 until you reach 0. The number of steps equals the square root. For example, 81 - 1 - 3 - 5 - 7 - 9 - 11 - 13 - 15 - 17 = 0 (9 steps), so sqrt(81) = 9.
  • Method 3 — Prime factorisation: If the prime factors of a number can be paired up (grouped into two identical groups), the number is a perfect square. For example, 324 = 2 * 2 * 3 * 3 * 3 * 3 = (2 * 3 * 3) * (2 * 3 * 3) = 18^2, so sqrt(324) = 18. If factors cannot be paired (e.g., 156 = 2 * 2 * 3 * 13), the number is not a perfect square.
  • Estimation: To estimate the square root of a number, find the two closest known perfect squares that bracket it, then narrow down using the units digit and intermediate calculations. For example, for sqrt(1936): 40^2 = 1600 and 50^2 = 2500, so 40 < sqrt(1936) < 50. The last digit 6 means the root ends in 4 or 6. Since 45^2 = 2025 > 1936, the root is between 40 and 45, so it must be 44.

1.2 Cubic Numbers

Cube — a solid figure where all sides meet at right angles and are equal in length.

Perfect cubes — numbers obtained by multiplying a number by itself three times (e.g., 1 = 1 * 1 * 1, 8 = 2 * 2 * 2, 27 = 3 * 3 * 3).

  • The number of unit cubes (side 1 cm) in a larger cube of side n cm is n * n * n = n^3.
  • For a cube of side 4 units: each layer has 4 * 4 = 16 unit cubes, and there are 4 layers, giving 4 * 4 * 4 = 64 unit cubes.
  • The notation n^3 means n multiplied by itself three times, read as "n cubed."
  • Cubes can also be computed for fractions, decimals, and negative numbers: (4/6)^3 = 64/216, (13.08)^3 = 2237.810112, and (-6)^3 = -216.
  • Unlike squares, the cube of a negative number is negative: (-6)^3 = -216.
  • Any digit (0-9) can appear as the last digit of a perfect cube, unlike squares which are restricted to 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, 9.
  • A perfect cube cannot end with exactly two zeroes (00), because if a number ends with n zeroes, its cube ends with 3n zeroes.

Taxicab Numbers

Hardy-Ramanujan Number — 1729, the smallest number that can be expressed as the sum of two cubes in two different ways: 1729 = 1^3 + 12^3 = 9^3 + 10^3.

Taxicab numbers — numbers that can be expressed as the sum of two cubes in two (or more) different ways.

  • The story comes from when G. H. Hardy visited Srinivasa Ramanujan at a hospital and mentioned arriving in a taxicab numbered 1729, calling it a "dull number."
  • Ramanujan immediately recognized 1729 as the smallest number expressible as the sum of two positive cubes in two different ways.
  • The next two taxicab numbers after 1729 are 4104 and 13832.
  • Ramanujan's deep intuition for numbers came from a lifelong passion for exploring numerical patterns.

Perfect Cubes and Consecutive Odd Numbers

  • Just as with squares, consecutive odd numbers are connected to cubes through a specific pattern:
  • 1 = 1^3
  • 3 + 5 = 8 = 2^3
  • 7 + 9 + 11 = 27 = 3^3
  • 13 + 15 + 17 + 19 = 64 = 4^3
  • 21 + 23 + 25 + 27 + 29 = 125 = 5^3
  • 31 + 33 + 35 + 37 + 39 + 41 = 216 = 6^3
  • For n^3, the sum consists of exactly n consecutive odd numbers.
  • The starting odd number for each group follows a pattern that can be predicted without full calculation.
  • The sum 91 + 93 + 95 + 97 + 99 + 101 + 103 + 105 + 107 + 109 (10 consecutive odd numbers) equals 10^3 = 1000.

Cube Roots

Cube root — if y = x^3, then x is the cube root of y, denoted by x = cbrt(y). For example, cbrt(8) = 2, cbrt(27) = 3, cbrt(1000) = 10.

  • In general, cbrt(n^3) = n.
  • Prime factorisation method: A number is a perfect cube if its prime factors can be split into three identical groups. For example, 3375 = 3 * 3 * 3 * 5 * 5 * 5 = (3 * 5) * (3 * 5) * (3 * 5) = 15^3, so cbrt(3375) = 15.
  • Alternatively, check if prime factors can be grouped into triplets: 3375 = (3 * 3 * 3) * (5 * 5 * 5) = 3^3 * 5^3.
  • If the prime factors cannot be grouped into triplets, the number is not a perfect cube. For example, 500 = 2 * 2 * 5 * 5 * 5 — the factor 2 appears only twice, so 500 is not a perfect cube.
  • Each prime factor of a number appears three times in the prime factorisation of its cube. For example, 6 = 2 * 3, and 6^3 = 216 = 2 * 2 * 2 * 3 * 3 * 3 = 2^3 * 3^3.

Successive Differences

  • For perfect squares, computing successive differences over two levels yields a constant value of 2:
  • Perfect squares: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, ...
  • Level 1 differences: 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, ... (consecutive odd numbers)
  • Level 2 differences: 2, 2, 2, 2, ... (constant)
  • For perfect cubes, computing successive differences over three levels yields a constant value of 6:
  • Perfect cubes: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, ...
  • Level 1 differences: 7, 19, 37, 61, 91, ...
  • Level 2 differences: 12, 18, 24, 30, ...
  • Level 3 differences: 6, 6, 6, ... (constant)
  • The number of levels needed to reach a constant difference relates to the power: squares (power 2) need 2 levels, cubes (power 3) need 3 levels.

1.3 A Pinch of History

  • The first known lists of perfect squares and perfect cubes were compiled by the Babylonians around 1700 BCE on clay tablets, used for land measurement, architecture, and geometric calculations.
  • In ancient Sanskrit works, the term varga was used for both the square figure/area and the square power (n^2).
  • The term ghana was used for both the solid cube and the product of a number with itself three times (n^3).
  • The fourth power (n^4) was called varga-varga (square of a square).
  • Aryabhata (499 CE) stated that a square figure of four equal sides and the number representing its area are called varga, and the product of two equal quantities is also called varga.
  • The word "root" for the mathematical operation sqrt() originates from the Sanskrit word mula, meaning root of a plant, basis, cause, or origin. Varga-mula was used for square root and ghana-mula for cube root.
  • This usage of "mula" was later adopted in Arabic as jidhr and in Latin as radix, both meaning root of a plant.
  • Another term pada (foot, basis, cause, origin) was also used. Brahmagupta (628 CE) explained: "The pada (root) of a krti (square) is that of which it is a square."
  • These terms have been used in India since at least the first century BCE.

Opening Puzzle: The Locker Problem

Q: Before the process begins, Khoisnam realises that he already knows which lockers will be open at the end. How did he figure out the answer?

Answer: Khoisnam realised that the number of times a locker is toggled equals the number of factors of its locker number. If a locker is toggled an odd number of times, it remains open; if toggled an even number of times, it remains closed.

Most numbers have an even number of factors because factors come in pairs (partner factors). For example, 6 has factors 1, 2, 3, 6 — the pairs are (1,6) and (2,3), giving 4 factors (even).

However, perfect square numbers have one factor pair where both numbers are the same (e.g., for 4: 2 x 2; for 9: 3 x 3). This means one factor is its own partner, resulting in an odd number of factors. Therefore, only lockers whose numbers are perfect squares will be toggled an odd number of times and remain open.

Q: Write the locker numbers that remain open.

Answer: The locker numbers that remain open are the perfect squares from 1 to 100:
1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100

Q: Which are these five lockers? (The first five locker numbers that were touched exactly twice.)

Answer: A locker is toggled exactly twice when its number has exactly two factors — that is, when the locker number is a prime number. Each prime number has only two factors: 1 and the number itself.

The first five prime numbers are: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11

So the passcode is 2-3-5-7-11.

Section 1.1: Square Numbers

Q: Does every number have an even number of factors?

Answer: No. Most numbers have an even number of factors because factors come in pairs. However, perfect squares have an odd number of factors because one of their factor pairs consists of the same number repeated. For example:
- 1 has 1 factor (odd)
- 4 has factors 1, 2, 4 — that is 3 factors (odd)
- 9 has factors 1, 3, 9 — that is 3 factors (odd)

Q: Can you use this insight to find more numbers with an odd number of factors? For instance, 36 has a factor pair 6 x 6 where both numbers are 6. Does this number have an odd number of factors?

Answer: Yes. The factors of 36 are: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36 — that is 9 factors (odd).

The factor pairs of 36 are: (1, 36), (2, 18), (3, 12), (4, 9), and (6, 6). Every factor other than 6 has a different partner. Since 6 pairs with itself, it is counted only once, making the total number of factors odd.

Similarly, 16 (factor pair 4 x 4), 25 (factor pair 5 x 5), 49 (factor pair 7 x 7), 64 (factor pair 8 x 8), and 100 (factor pair 10 x 10) all have an odd number of factors. In general, all perfect squares have an odd number of factors.

Answer: Several patterns can be observed:

  2. Units digit rules:
  3. Numbers ending in 0 have squares ending in 00
  4. Numbers ending in 1 or 9 have squares ending in 1

  7. Numbers ending in 4 or 6 have squares ending in 6

  11. Parity: Even numbers have even squares; odd numbers have odd squares.
  12. Zeros pattern: If a number ends with n zeros, its square ends with 2n zeros.

Show Answer
✅ **Answer:** Every factor of a number has a 'partner factor' such that their product equals the given number. For example, for 6: 1 x 6 = 6 and 2 x 3 = 6. However, for square numbers, one factor multiplied by itself gives the number. For example, for 9: 1 x 9 = 9 and 3 x 3 = 9. Here, the factor 3 is its own partner. Similarly, for 36: 1 x 36, 2 x 18, 3 x 12, 4 x 9, and 6 x 6. The factor 6 pairs with itself, so the factors are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36 — nine factors (odd). This is why only square numbers have an odd number of factors.

We can estimate the square root of larger perfect squares by looking at the closest perfect squares we are familiar with and then narrowing down the interval to search.

For example, to find $\sqrt{1936}$, we can reason as follows:

(i) 1936 is between 1600 ($40^2$) and 2500 ($50^2$), so $40 < \sqrt{1936} < 50$.

(ii) The last digit of 1936 is 6. So, the last digit of the square root must either be 4 or 6. It can be 44 or 46.

(iii) If we calculate $45^2$, we can compare it with 1936 to halve the interval to search from 40–50 to either 40–45 or 45–50. We can write $45^2$ as $(40 + 5) (40 + 5) = 40^2 + 2 \times 40 \times 5 + 5^2 = 1600 + 400 + 25 = 2025$.

(iv) $2025 > 1936$. So, $40 < \sqrt{1936} < 45$

(v) From the observation in point b we can guess and then verify that $\sqrt{1936}$ is 44.

Cube Roots

We know that $8 = 2^3$.

We call 2 the cube root of 8 and denote this by $2 = \sqrt[3]{8}$.

More generally, if $y = x^3$, then $x$ is the cube root of $y$. This is denoted by $x = \sqrt[3]{y}$. So, $\sqrt[3]{8} = \sqrt[3]{2^3} = 2$.

Similarly, $\sqrt[3]{27} = \sqrt[3]{3^3} = 3$ and $\sqrt[3]{1000} = \sqrt[3]{10^3} = 10$. In general, $\sqrt[3]{n^3} = n$.

How do we find out if a number is a cube? Taking inspiration from the case of squares, let us see if we can use prime factorisations.

? Let us check if 3375 is a perfect cube.
$$3375 = 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 5 \times 5.$$

Can the factors be split into three identical groups? For 3375, we can form three groups of $(3 \times 5)$. So,
$$3375 = (3 \times 5) \times (3 \times 5) \times (3 \times 5)$$
$$= (3 \times 5)^3 = 15^3.$$

Another way is to check if the factors can be grouped into triplet(s):
$$3375 = (3 \times 3 \times 3) \times (5 \times 5 \times 5) = 3^3 \times 5^3.$$
This means $\sqrt[3]{3375} = 15$.

In ancient Sanskrit works the term varga was used both for the square figure or its area, as well as the square power, and the term ghana was used both for the solid cube as well as the product of a number with itself three times. Why is the word 'root' (the root of a plant) used for the mathematical operation $\sqrt{}$ (square root, cube root, etc.)?

It is because, in ancient India, the Sanskrit word mula, meaning root of a plant, basis, cause, origin, etc., was used for the mathematical operations of taking roots.

In Sanskrit, varga-mula (the basis, cause, origin of the square) was used for square-root and ghana-mula was used for cube-root. This use of mula for the mathematical concept of root was subsequently emulated in Arabic and Latin through their corresponding words for the root of a plant — jidhr and radix respectively. The term mula for root has been used in India at least from the first century BCE. Another term used was pada (foot, basis, cause, origin). Brahmagupta (628 CE) explains, 'The pada (root) of a krti (square) is that of which it is a square.'

SUMMARY

  • A number obtained by multiplying a number by itself is called a square number. Squares of natural numbers are called perfect squares.
  • All perfect squares end with 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9. Squares can only have an even number of zeros at the end.
  • Square root is the inverse operation of square. Every perfect square has two integral square roots. The positive square root of a number is denoted by the symbol $\sqrt{}$. For example, $\sqrt{9} = 3$.
  • A number obtained by multiplying a number by itself three times is called a cube. For example 1, 8, 27, ... ,etc., are cubes.
  • A number is a perfect square if its prime factors can be split into two identical groups.
  • A number is a perfect cube if its prime factors can be split into three identical groups
  • The symbol $\sqrt[3]{}$ denotes cube root. For example, $\sqrt[3]{27} = 3$.
