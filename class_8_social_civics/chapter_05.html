Universal Franchise and India's Electoral System

Chapter Summary

  • Role of Youth: Describe the change in voting age and its impact on democracy.

Theme: Universal Franchise and India's Electoral System

  • This chapter explores the evolution of India's democratic system through universal adult franchise and the role of the Election Commission.
  • It highlights the process of elections, voting rights, model code of conduct, and measures ensuring inclusiveness, transparency, and fairness.
  • Emphasizes citizens' participation, equality, and accountability in strengthening democracy.

Power of the People: From Votes to a Strong Democracy

  • Before independence, voting was limited to wealthy and educated people; women and the poor were excluded.
  • After 1950, the Constitution introduced universal adult franchise, giving every citizen above 18 years an equal right to vote.
  • Challenges like low voter turnout and misuse of money still exist, requiring efforts from citizens, the Election Commission, and media.

Every Vote Counts!

  • In India, every citizen aged 18 or above has the right to vote regardless of wealth, gender, caste, or education.
  • This principle makes each vote equal, whether from a farmer in a village or a student in a city.
  • Equal voting rights and honest elections are necessary to keep democracy strong.

The Foundation of Indian Democracy

  • Before independence, only about 13% of Indians could vote, mostly wealthy landlords or educated individuals.
  • When the Constitution was adopted in 1950, a decision was taken to grant universal adult franchise to all citizens.
  • India became one of the earliest democracies to give equal voting rights to women along with men.
  • During the first elections in 1951–52, nearly 85% of eligible voters were illiterate, so parties were assigned unique symbols for easy identification.
  • Nearly two million wooden ballot boxes were used, transported by bullock carts, boats, and camels to remote areas.

Change in Voting Age

  • Initially, the Constitution allowed citizens aged 21 years and above to vote.
  • To empower youth who were more aware and educated, the government passed the 61st Constitutional Amendment Act in 1988.
  • This historic change reduced the voting age from 21 years to 18 years from the 1989 general elections onward.
  • This amendment added over 35 million new young voters to India's electoral rolls.

Universal Adult Franchise

  • Universal Adult Franchise — Means that every citizen of India who is 18 years of age or above has the legal right to vote in elections.
  • This right is given irrespective of caste, religion, gender, language, education, wealth, or social background.
  • Whether a person is rich or poor, educated or uneducated, male or female, all votes carry equal value.
  • It shows the belief that sovereignty lies with the people and ensures political equality among citizens.
  • It promotes equality, participation, and the idea that every citizen contributes to the decision-making process.

Importance of Universal Franchise

  • Equality of All Citizens: Ensures all citizens are treated equally in the political system regardless of background.
  • Voice in Governance: Allows people to express opinions and participate in selecting leaders who influence daily life laws and policies.
  • Foundation of Democracy: Makes democracy strong by ensuring the government is formed according to the will of the majority.
  • Representation of All Groups: Allows different communities (women, Scheduled Castes, minorities) to have a say in governance.
  • Prevention of Discrimination: Removes unfair practices where only wealthy men could vote previously.
  • Accountability of Leaders: Leaders must work honestly as people can remove them from power in the next election.
  • Encourages Participation: Motivates people to take interest in public issues and become responsible citizens.
  • Strengthens Unity and Trust: Builds a sense of belonging and reduces social divisions among citizens.

Bridging Barriers, Enabling Participation: India's Electoral System

  • India is the largest democracy with over 900 million eligible voters, making elections complex and challenging.
  • The Election Commission of India (ECI) organizes elections in a fair and transparent manner.
  • Accessibility measures include postal ballots, home voting for senior citizens and disabled persons, ramps, wheelchairs, and braille-enabled voter ID cards.
  • Modern technologies like app-based assistance and Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trail (VVPAT) slips increase transparency.
  • Polling teams travel to remote areas (like Tashigang at 15,256 feet) to ensure every citizen's vote matters.

Election Commission of India (ECI)

  • Election Commission of India (ECI) — An independent constitutional body created in 1950 under Article 324 of the Constitution.
  • It is not under the direct control of the government, ensuring neutral and unbiased elections.
  • Responsible for conducting elections to Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies, President, and Vice President.
  • Acts like a referee to ensure all parties follow rules and no one gets an unfair advantage.
  • Introduced modern tools like Electronic Voting Machines (EVMs) and VVPATs to improve efficiency.

Structure of the Election Commission of India

  • Chief Election Commissioner (CEC): The senior-most authority who heads the ECI and ensures decisions are made fairly.
  • Election Commissioners: Two other commissioners support the CEC to form a three-member body for collective decision-making.
  • State-Level Officers: Chief Electoral Officer (CEO) supervises election activities within the state.
  • District-Level Officers: District Election Officers and Returning Officers manage polling stations and voter lists at the district level.
  • Large Administrative Network: Thousands of polling officers and security personnel ensure smooth elections in urban and remote areas.

Functions of the Election Commission of India

  • Declaring Election Dates: Decides when elections will take place for various levels of government.
  • Model Code of Conduct (MCC): Enforces rules to maintain fairness, such as avoiding hate speech or bribery.
  • Registering Political Parties: Recognizes parties, decides their symbols, and ensures democratic principles.
  • Supervising Polls: Prepares electoral rolls, arranges ballot papers/EVMs, and supervises the voting process.
  • Ensuring Fairness: Monitors campaigns and expenses to prevent unfair advantages.

Accessibility Measures

  • Postal Ballots: Allowed for voters who cannot reach polling stations due to duty or health conditions.
  • Home Voting: Available for senior citizens and persons with disabilities via election officials visiting homes.
  • Braille-Enabled Voter ID Cards: Helps visually impaired voters identify candidates independently.
  • Ramps at Polling Stations: Make polling stations barrier-free for wheelchair users and those with mobility challenges.
  • Assistive Technologies: Includes voter helpline apps and sign language assistance for differently-abled voters.

Managing the Electoral Process

  • Elections involve lakhs of polling stations and millions of officials across towns, villages, and cities.
  • The process is organized step-by-step to ensure fairness and transparency.
  • Steps include checking the voter list, showing identity proof, applying indelible ink, casting votes on EVMs, and verifying through VVPAT.
  • Indelible ink stays for many days to prevent anyone from voting twice.
  • VVPAT displays a paper slip confirming the vote has been recorded correctly before dropping into a sealed box.

Model Code of Conduct (MCC)

  • Model Code of Conduct (MCC) — A set of rules prepared by the ECI to ensure free, fair, and peaceful elections.
  • Applies to governments, political parties, and candidates once election dates are announced.
  • Aimed at stopping the misuse of power, money, or government machinery for unfair advantage.
  • First started in Kerala in 1960 and applied across India in the 1962 general elections.
  • Became stricter in 1991 and during the tenure of T.N. Seshan in the 1990s.

Rules of the Model Code of Conduct (MCC)

  • Government Restrictions: Ruling government cannot announce new schemes or welfare benefits during elections.
  • Fair Campaigning: Parties must campaign honestly without spreading lies, false promises, or using caste/religion divisions.
  • Use of Public Property: Government schools, offices, and walls cannot be used for political advertisements.
  • Equal Opportunity: Ensures all candidates get a fair chance regardless of wealth or party strength.
  • Code of Behaviour for Leaders: Leaders must respect rivals and avoid personal attacks during speeches.
  • Unfair Campaigning: Spreading fake news or rumors to mislead voters is a violation.
  • Exceeding Spending Limits: Candidates must adhere to fixed spending limits on campaigning.

Violations of the Model Code of Conduct (MCC)

  • Bribery: Offering money, gifts, liquor, or free items to voters is strictly forbidden.
  • Misuse of Machinery: Using government vehicles, offices, or officials for election work is not allowed.
  • Abusive Speeches: Politicians cannot make hate speeches or threaten rivals during campaigns.

Types of Elections in India

  • Elections are held at national and state levels to ensure proper representation and functioning of the government.
  • Conducted under strict rules to ensure they are free, fair, and transparent.

Lok Sabha and State Assemblies

  • Lok Sabha (House of the People): Directly elected by citizens every five years.
  • First-Past-the-Post (FPTP) System: The candidate with the most votes wins, even without securing more than 50%.
  • Country divided into 543 constituencies for Lok Sabha; states divided into their own constituencies for assemblies.
  • Citizens above 18 years directly vote to choose representatives influencing state and national governance.
  • The party or coalition with a majority forms the government; leader becomes Prime Minister or Chief Minister.

Rajya Sabha

  • Rajya Sabha (Council of States): Upper house representing states at the national level.
  • Members are indirectly elected by MLAs of state assemblies, not directly by citizens.
  • 233 members elected by MLAs; 12 nominated by the President for contributions in literature, science, art, or social service.
  • Called the Permanent House because it is never dissolved; one-third retire every two years.
  • Elections conducted through the Single Transferable Vote (STV) system for proportional representation.

President of India

  • Head of state and highest constitutional authority, elected indirectly by an Electoral College of MPs and MLAs.
  • Ensures representation of both central and state governments.
  • Acts as symbolic leader, guardian of the Constitution, and commands armed forces.
  • Nominated members of Rajya Sabha and State Assemblies do not vote in this election.

Vice President of India

  • Second-highest constitutional position after the President.
  • Elected by an electoral college consisting of both elected and nominated members of Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha.
  • Acts as Chairperson of Rajya Sabha and maintains order during debates.
  • Acts as acting President whenever the President is unable to perform duties.

Challenges in India's Electoral System

  • Low Voter Turnout: Many citizens think their single vote does not matter or are too busy to vote.
  • Influence of Money Power: Wealthy candidates spend huge amounts on rallies and ads, sometimes bribing voters.
  • Criminalisation of Politics: Some candidates with serious criminal cases contest elections using power and fear.
  • Lack of Awareness Among Citizens: People may be influenced by caste, religion, or false promises instead of judging candidates by work.

Way Forward – Strengthening Democracy

  • Increasing Voter Participation: Organize awareness programs in schools and communities; improve facilities at polling booths.
  • Promoting Transparency: Publish voter lists, ensure proper use of EVMs/VVPATs, and provide timely information.
  • Encouraging Ethical Leadership: Motivate citizens to support candidates with honesty and integrity.
  • Education, Media and Youth Awareness: Spread civic education and use digital platforms to reduce confusion and spread correct information.
  • Fight Fake News: Election Commission partners with social media platforms to flag false election content.

Key Terms

61st Constitutional Amendment Act — Act passed in 1988 that reduced the voting age in India from 21 years to 18 years.
Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) — The senior-most authority who heads the Election Commission of India and ensures decisions are made fairly.
Chief Electoral Officer (CEO) — State-level officer who supervises election activities such as preparing voter lists within a state.
Coalition Governments — Governments formed when two or more political parties join hands because no single party secures a clear majority.
Constituency — A specific geographical area divided to elect one representative to the legislature.
Criminalisation of Politics — The situation where candidates with serious criminal cases contest elections using power and fear.
Democracy — A system of government described as 'of the people, by the people and for the people' where citizens have a voice.
Electoral College — The body consisting of elected MPs and MLAs that elects the President and Vice President of India.
Election Commission of India (ECI) — An independent constitutional body created in 1950 responsible for conducting free and fair elections.
Electronic Voting Machines (EVMs) — Devices introduced by the Election Commission that allow voters to cast their votes electronically.
First-Past-the-Post (FPTP) system — An electoral method where the candidate with the highest number of votes wins, even without an absolute majority.
Home Voting — A facility provided for senior citizens and persons with disabilities to cast their votes at their residences.
Indelible Ink — Special purple ink applied to a voter's finger to prevent them from voting more than once.
Lok Sabha — The House of the People, the lower house of Parliament directly elected by citizens every five years.
Model Code of Conduct (MCC) — A set of guidelines enforced by the Election Commission to ensure fair campaigning and prevent misuse of power.
Money Power — The unfair influence exerted by wealthy candidates who spend excessively to win elections.
Muscle Power — The use of force or intimidation by candidates to influence voters or opponents during elections.
Postal Ballots — Voting method allowed for government staff, soldiers, or those with health conditions who cannot reach polling stations.
Prime Minister — The leader of the majority party or coalition in the Lok Sabha who heads the Union government.
President — The head of state elected indirectly by an Electoral College of MPs and MLAs.
Rajya Sabha — The Council of States, the upper house of Parliament whose members are elected indirectly by state assemblies.
Returning Officer (RO) — Officer at the district level responsible for managing the conduct of elections in their area.
Single Transferable Vote (STV) system — A voting method used for indirect elections to ensure proportional representation for smaller parties.
Sovereignty — The belief that the ultimate power to choose leaders lies with the people rather than a privileged few.
Universal Adult Franchise — The principle granting every citizen aged 18 or above the legal right to vote regardless of caste, gender, or wealth.
Voter Turnout — The percentage of eligible voters who actually participate and cast their votes in an election.
Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trail (VVPAT) — A machine attached to EVMs that displays a paper slip to confirm the vote was recorded correctly.
Voting Age — The minimum age required for a citizen to exercise their right to vote, currently set at 18 years in India.

Over the years, the MCC was made stricter and more effective. A major turning point came in 1991, when the Election Commission began enforcing it with full constitutional authority. The MCC became an even stronger tool during the tenure of T.N. Seshan, the Chief Election Commissioner in the 1990s. He ensured that no political party or candidate could break the rules without facing strict punishment. Under his fearless leadership, the ECI earned immense respect and became known as a strong guardian of fairness and integrity in elections.

Features of Lok Sabha and State Assemblies Elections

Following are some of the features of the Lok Sabha and State Assembly elections:

Rajya Sabha

The Rajya Sabha, or the Council of States, is the upper house of Parliament and plays an important role in representing the states at the national level. Unlike the Lok Sabha, its members are not elected directly by citizens but indirectly by elected MLAs of state assemblies. Out of its 245 members, 233 are elected by the MLAs of states and union territories, while the President of India nominates 12 members who have made special contributions in fields like literature, science, art, or social service. The number of seats allotted to each state depends on its population—for example, populous states like Uttar Pradesh have more members than smaller states like Arunachal Pradesh.

The Rajya Sabha is called the Permanent House because it is never dissolved. Instead, one-third of its members retire every two years and new members are elected to replace them. Each member serves a six-year term, ensuring continuity while also allowing fresh voices to enter regularly. The elections are conducted through the Single Transferable Vote (STV) system, which provides proportional representation. This system ensures that even smaller parties and states have a fair chance of being represented in the Rajya Sabha, making the law-making process more inclusive.

Features of Rajya Sabha Elections

Following are some of the features of the Rajya Sabha elections:

  • Indirect Elections: Citizens do not directly vote for Rajya Sabha members. Instead, MLAs from state assemblies elect them, giving the states an indirect but strong voice in Parliament.
  • STV System: The Single Transferable Vote method allows proportional representation, ensuring that regional parties and smaller groups also gain seats, creating a more balanced Parliament.
  • Composition: The Rajya Sabha has 245 members, of which 233 are elected and 12 are nominated by the President for their expertise and contributions in various fields.
  • Permanent Body: It cannot be dissolved like the Lok Sabha. Instead, one-third of its members retire every two years, ensuring both continuity and regular renewal.
  • Six-Year Term: Each member serves for six years, which gives them time to gain experience and contribute effectively to national debates and decisions.
  • Voice of States: It safeguards the interests of the states by ensuring that laws are not framed only for the majority population but also consider the needs of smaller and less-populated regions.
  • Role in Law-Making: Along with the Lok Sabha, the Rajya Sabha reviews, debates and passes laws, acting as a check on rushed or one-sided decisions.

The election is held through the Single Transferable Vote (STV) system with proportional representation, which guarantees that the winning candidate has broad support across the country rather than just a simple majority. Nominated members of Rajya Sabha, State Assemblies, or Legislative Councils are not part of the voting process. This rule ensures that only directly elected representatives take part, maintaining the democratic legitimacy of the office. Thus, even though the people do not vote directly, the President still represents the will of the nation indirectly but meaningfully.

Features President of India Elections

Following are some of the features of the President of India elections:

  • Indirect Election: The President is elected by an Electoral College of MPs and MLAs, ensuring representation of both national and state interests.
  • STV with Proportional Representation: This system ensures fairness by giving more populous states greater weight, while also considering smaller states, creating balance in representation.
  • Exclusion of Nominated Members: Nominated members of Rajya Sabha, State Assemblies and Legislative Councils do not vote in the election, keeping the process democratic by including only directly elected representatives.
  • Head of State: The President acts as the symbolic leader of the country and represents India at national and international levels.
  • Guardian of the Constitution: The President ensures that government policies and actions follow constitutional principles.
  • Constitutional Powers: The President signs bills into law, appoints important officials, commands the armed forces and takes decisions during emergencies.
  • Democratic Legitimacy: Since only elected representatives vote, the process ensures that the President indirectly reflects the will of the people across India.

President of India

The President of India is the head of state and the highest constitutional authority in the country. Although not directly elected by the citizens, the President is chosen through a special system that makes the process representative of the entire nation. An Electoral College elects the President, consisting of elected Members of Parliament (MPs) from both Houses of Parliament (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha) and elected Members of Legislative Assemblies (MLAs) from all states and some union territories. This ensures that both the central and state governments have a say in the election.

Vice President of India

The Vice President of India holds the second-highest constitutional position after the President and plays a key role in both parliamentary and constitutional matters. Unlike the President, the Vice President is elected by an electoral college consisting of both elected and nominated members of the Lok Sabha

and the Rajya Sabha. The election is conducted using the Single Transferable Vote (STV) system, which allows fair representation to all political parties in Parliament.

The main duty of the Vice President is to act as the Chairperson of the Rajya Sabha, where he or she maintains order during debates, ensures impartiality and allows smooth passage of laws. Another important role of the Vice President is to act as the acting President whenever the President is unable to perform duties due to absence, illness, or vacancy until a new President is elected. This makes the Vice President an important link in maintaining stability in India's constitutional framework.

Features Vice President of India Elections

Following are some of the features of the Vice President of India elections:
- Indirect Election: The Vice President is elected by an electoral college consisting of members of both Houses of Parliament, including nominated members, making the process inclusive and representative.
- STV System: The use of proportional representation through the Single Transferable Vote system ensures that even smaller political parties in Parliament get a fair say in the election.
- Chairperson of Rajya Sabha: As the presiding officer of the Rajya Sabha, the Vice President ensures orderly discussions, fair debates and unbiased decision-making in the upper house of Parliament.
- Support Role in Parliament: Works closely with members of Parliament to ensure that legislative functions run smoothly, acting as a bridge between the executive and the legislature.
- Acting President: In case of the death, resignation, removal, or absence of the President, the Vice President steps in as the acting President, ensuring no disruption in constitutional authority and governance.

Challenges in India's Electoral System

Elections in India face many obstacles such as low participation of voters, misuse of money, criminalisation of politics and lack of proper awareness among citizens. These issues reduce fairness and weaken trust in democracy.

Following are some of the challenges seen in the electoral system:
- Low Voter Turnout: In many cities, voter turnout during elections is very low. People often think their single vote does not matter, or they are too busy with work and personal life. Some also do not update their names on the voter list after moving to a new place. This weakens democracy because it means decisions are made by only a part of the population instead of everyone.
- Influence of Money Power: Elections in India require huge spending on rallies, posters, advertisements and social media campaigns. Wealthy candidates use their money to create more visibility and sometimes even try to bribe voters with gifts or cash. Honest candidates with limited money find it very hard to compete. This creates unfairness in elections and allows rich people to dominate politics.
- Criminalisation of Politics: One of the biggest concerns is that some candidates with serious criminal cases still contest elections. They use power, fear and money to win votes. This reduces people's trust in the system because leaders are expected to be role models, not lawbreakers. When such candidates come to power, they may misuse their position instead of working for the public good.
- Lack of Awareness Among Citizens: In many rural and semi-urban areas, people are not fully aware of their voting rights and responsibilities. Some are influenced by caste, religion, or false promises instead of judging candidates by their work. Others may sell their vote for money or free items. This lack of awareness prevents people from making informed choices and affects the quality of leaders elected.
